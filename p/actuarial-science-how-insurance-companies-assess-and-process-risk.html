Actuarial Science: How Insurance Companies Assess and Process Risk

Insurance depends on an unusual business problem: an insurer must collect premiums today for losses that may occur months or years later, and the amount and timing of those losses are uncertain.

Actuarial science provides many of the mathematical and statistical methods insurers use to evaluate that uncertainty. Actuaries analyze historical experience, probability, financial data, models, and assumptions to estimate expected costs and assess whether an insurance product can remain financially sustainable.

Risk assessment is not limited to calculating a premium. It can affect underwriting, product design, reserves, capital management, claims analysis, and long-term financial planning.

The Actuarial Standards Board's standards recognize risk classification as a fundamental actuarial activity and describe risk classification systems as methods for grouping risks based on characteristics associated with expected outcomes.

1. What Is Actuarial Risk Assessment?

Actuarial risk assessment is the process of measuring and analyzing uncertain future outcomes.

For an insurance company, these outcomes can include:

  • How frequently claims occur
  • How severe claims are
  • How long claims remain open
  • How medical costs change
  • How vehicle repair costs change
  • How long policyholders remain insured
  • How investment returns affect financial results
  • How catastrophic events affect losses

The objective is not to predict every individual event accurately. Instead, actuarial analysis seeks to estimate patterns across sufficiently large groups of risks.

For example, an insurer cannot know whether a particular driver will file a claim next year. It can, however, analyze historical claims and relevant characteristics to estimate expected claim frequency and severity across a portfolio.

2. Risk Classification

One of the foundations of actuarial work is risk classification.

A risk classification system groups policyholders or potential policyholders according to characteristics associated with expected outcomes. The Actuarial Standards Board describes risk characteristics as measurable or observable factors used to assign risks to different classes.

Depending on the insurance product, characteristics may include factors such as:

  • Location
  • Type of property
  • Vehicle characteristics
  • Driving history
  • Coverage limits
  • Prior claims experience
  • Policy characteristics
  • Demographic or other legally permitted characteristics

The characteristics used vary considerably by insurance line and jurisdiction.

Actuaries consider whether the characteristics have a meaningful relationship with expected costs and whether the classification system is appropriate for its intended purpose.

3. Collecting and Evaluating Data

Actuarial analysis depends heavily on data.

An insurer may analyze information concerning:

  • Historical claims
  • Premiums
  • Loss amounts
  • Policy exposure
  • Claim frequency
  • Claim severity
  • Expenses
  • Lapse or retention rates
  • Economic conditions
  • Repair costs
  • Medical costs
  • Catastrophe experience

Historical data are useful, but they are not automatically representative of the future.

Changes in driving behavior, inflation, medical technology, vehicle technology, weather patterns, legal environments, or consumer behavior can make older experience less predictive.

For that reason, actuaries must evaluate whether the available data are appropriate for the analysis and whether adjustments are necessary.

4. Claim Frequency and Claim Severity

Two basic concepts appear throughout property and casualty actuarial analysis: frequency and severity.

Claim frequency measures how often claims occur relative to an appropriate exposure measure.

Claim severity measures the size of losses when claims occur.

For example, an insurer could experience:

  • Many small claims
  • Few very large claims
  • A combination of frequent small losses and occasional catastrophic losses

Understanding both dimensions is important because two portfolios with the same total historical losses can have very different risk characteristics.

Actuaries may analyze frequency and severity separately before combining them into broader loss-cost estimates.

5. Estimating Expected Losses

An insurer needs an estimate of the losses it expects to pay.

A simplified conceptual relationship is:

Expected Loss = Expected Claim Frequency × Expected Claim Severity

Actual actuarial models can be substantially more complicated.

They may incorporate:

  • Multiple risk classes
  • Development of claims over time
  • Inflation
  • Trends
  • Catastrophe exposure
  • Changes in policy mix
  • Reinsurance
  • Legal developments
  • Expenses
  • Investment considerations

The purpose is to develop a reasonable estimate rather than a guaranteed forecast.

6. Underwriting and Actuarial Analysis

Underwriting and actuarial work are closely connected but are not identical.

Underwriting generally concerns evaluating an individual application or risk against an insurer's eligibility and acceptance guidelines.

Actuarial analysis generally operates at a broader level, using data and models to estimate expected outcomes and support decisions about pricing, risk classification, reserves, and other financial matters.

An insurer may use actuarial analysis to develop a rating structure, while underwriters apply the company's underwriting rules to individual applicants.

The two functions therefore interact but perform different roles.

7. Insurance Pricing

Actuarial analysis is commonly used in developing insurance rates or premiums.

Pricing can involve estimating:

  1. Expected claims.
  2. Claim-related expenses.
  3. Operating expenses.
  4. Reinsurance costs.
  5. Taxes and assessments.
  6. Required or targeted returns.
  7. Other relevant financial considerations.

The precise pricing methodology depends on the insurance product and regulatory environment.

For life insurance and annuity products, the Actuarial Standards Board describes pricing as determining charges and benefits at issue while evaluating expected profitability and underlying risks. Its standards also discuss profitability metrics, risk capital, sensitivity analysis, and stochastic analysis.

8. Trends and Inflation

Historical claims cannot simply be copied into a future pricing period.

Actuaries may need to account for changes such as:

  • Medical inflation
  • Vehicle repair costs
  • Construction costs
  • Wage levels
  • Litigation expenses
  • Consumer behavior
  • Changes in claim frequency

For example, if vehicle repairs become more expensive because modern vehicles contain more sophisticated components, historical average repair costs may underestimate future claims unless the data are appropriately adjusted.

The Actuarial Standards Board maintains specific standards concerning trending procedures in property and casualty insurance.

9. Modeling Future Outcomes

Insurance outcomes are uncertain, so actuaries frequently use mathematical and statistical models.

Models can evaluate different assumptions and scenarios, including:

  • Higher claim frequency
  • Higher claim severity
  • Inflation
  • Lower investment returns
  • Catastrophic losses
  • Changes in policyholder behavior
  • Changes in interest rates

Some models use deterministic assumptions, while others use stochastic techniques that generate distributions of potential outcomes.

The objective is to understand a range of possible financial results rather than relying exclusively on a single forecast.

10. Credibility and Statistical Reliability

Not every group of insurance data is large enough to provide reliable conclusions on its own.

Actuarial practice therefore considers credibility, which concerns the predictive value assigned to a particular body of data.

The Actuarial Standards Board explains that risk classes should generally contain enough information to support credible statistical inferences, while recognizing that actuaries may need to balance statistical predictability against other considerations.

For example, an insurer may have extensive historical data for common automobile risks but relatively little data for a newly introduced type of vehicle.

Additional data or appropriate actuarial methods may be necessary before drawing conclusions.

11. Adverse Selection

Insurance markets can experience adverse selection when people with different levels of risk make choices that cause the insured population to differ from the assumptions underlying the insurer's pricing or classification system.

For example, if a particular risk is substantially more likely to purchase a certain type of coverage than the average applicant assumed in the pricing model, the insurer's expected claims may be different from its original projections.

Actuarial risk classification is partly concerned with understanding these differences and designing appropriate analytical frameworks.

The Actuarial Standards Board identifies adverse selection as an important consideration in risk classification.

12. Reserves for Future Claims

Pricing addresses the amount an insurer expects to charge for coverage. Reserving addresses the insurer's obligation for claims and related expenses.

An insurer may have claims that:

  • Have already occurred but have not yet been reported
  • Have been reported but not fully settled
  • Have been settled but remain subject to additional expenses
  • Require payments over an extended period

Actuaries estimate liabilities associated with these future payments.

These estimates are important because an insurer needs sufficient financial resources to meet obligations as claims develop.

State insurance regulators use actuarial and financial requirements as part of broader insurance solvency oversight. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners notes that actuarial guidelines provide interpretive direction concerning statutory actuarial requirements and areas including reserves, valuation, capital adequacy, and solvency.

13. Claims Development

A claim's ultimate cost may not be known when it is first reported.

For example, a bodily injury claim can develop over time as:

  • Additional medical treatment occurs
  • Liability becomes clearer
  • Injuries are evaluated
  • Settlement negotiations progress
  • Litigation begins or concludes

Actuaries analyze historical claims-development patterns to estimate how reported or incurred losses may develop in the future.

This work helps insurers estimate unpaid claim liabilities and evaluate financial results.

14. Reinsurance and Catastrophic Risk

Insurers also manage their own exposure by purchasing reinsurance.

Reinsurance allows an insurer to transfer some financial risk to another insurance company or reinsurer.

Actuarial analysis can help evaluate:

  • Expected catastrophic losses
  • Reinsurance costs
  • Attachment points
  • Coverage limits
  • Potential loss distributions
  • Remaining net exposure

This is particularly relevant for risks such as hurricanes, earthquakes, wildfires, severe storms, and other events capable of generating large numbers of claims simultaneously.

15. Enterprise Risk Management

Insurance companies face risks beyond individual claims.

Enterprise risk management can consider:

  • Insurance risk
  • Market risk
  • Credit risk
  • Operational risk
  • Liquidity risk
  • Strategic risk
  • Regulatory risk
  • Catastrophe risk

The Actuarial Standards Board's enterprise-risk-management guidance discusses evaluating how risks interact, their potential impact over different time horizons, capital implications, and organizational risk appetite.

This broader perspective recognizes that an insurer's financial position can be affected by several risks at the same time.

16. Scenario and Sensitivity Analysis

Actuaries can test what happens when important assumptions change.

For example, an insurer might examine the effect of:

  • A 10% increase in claim severity
  • Higher medical inflation
  • A major catastrophe
  • Changes in policy retention
  • Lower investment yields
  • Changes in repair costs

Sensitivity analysis changes one or more assumptions and compares the resulting outcome with the baseline.

Stochastic analysis goes further by allowing random variation in one or more inputs to estimate a distribution of possible outcomes. The Actuarial Standards Board identifies both approaches in its guidance on insurance pricing.

17. Regulatory Oversight

Insurance is primarily regulated at the state level in the United States, although federal laws and agencies can also affect particular aspects of insurance.

State insurance departments oversee insurers operating within their jurisdictions, while the NAIC develops model laws, regulations, and actuarial guidance used by state regulators.

Actuarial analysis therefore operates within a regulatory framework rather than being solely an internal business exercise.

Requirements can differ depending on the insurance line, jurisdiction, company structure, and purpose of the actuarial analysis.

18. Data, Models, and Professional Judgment

Actuarial science is quantitative, but it is not simply a matter of entering historical data into a formula.

Actuaries must consider:

  • Data quality
  • Model limitations
  • Assumptions
  • Statistical credibility
  • Changes in underlying conditions
  • Regulatory requirements
  • Uncertainty
  • Professional judgment

The Actuarial Standards Board's risk-classification standard specifically recognizes that professional judgment is required and that statistical tools may be used when developing or reviewing classification systems.

This is particularly important when historical experience is limited or when the insurance environment is changing rapidly.

19. Artificial Intelligence and Predictive Analytics

Insurance risk analysis increasingly incorporates advanced statistical techniques, predictive analytics, machine learning, and other data-driven methods.

The Actuarial Standards Board's proposed revision to ASOP No. 12 notes the evolution of predictive modeling, big-data sources, machine learning, artificial intelligence, and behavioral science in actuarial risk classification.

These tools can process large amounts of information, but their use does not eliminate the need for appropriate data, model validation, professional judgment, and compliance with applicable law.

A sophisticated model can still produce unreliable results if its underlying data are incomplete, inappropriate, or poorly interpreted.

20. How Risk Assessment Connects to the Insurance Lifecycle

Actuarial analysis can influence nearly every stage of an insurance product's lifecycle.

A simplified process looks like this:

Data → Risk Analysis → Classification → Pricing → Underwriting → Policy Issuance → Claims → Reserving → Experience Analysis → Pricing Updates

The process is iterative.

Actual claims experience can later be compared with previous assumptions. If experience changes materially, an insurer may revise its models, assumptions, underwriting practices, or pricing, subject to applicable regulatory requirements.

Final Takeaway

Actuarial science gives insurers a structured way to analyze uncertainty.

Actuaries use historical experience, statistical methods, risk classification, forecasting, modeling, and professional judgment to estimate claim costs and evaluate financial risks. Their work can support pricing, underwriting frameworks, reserves, reinsurance decisions, capital management, and enterprise risk management.

Risk assessment is not a guarantee that an individual claim or future event can be predicted. Instead, actuarial analysis seeks to estimate patterns and financial outcomes across groups of risks.

The process is also subject to regulatory and professional standards. The Actuarial Standards Board establishes standards governing actuarial practice, while state insurance regulators oversee many aspects of insurer financial and market conduct.

For consumers, understanding this process can help explain why insurers collect information about risk characteristics, how premiums are developed, why claim costs affect future pricing, and why insurers maintain reserves for losses that have already occurred but may not yet be fully settled.
