Standard Operating Procedures at Commercial Truck Accident Scenes

Commercial truck crashes can require a more complex response than ordinary passenger-vehicle collisions. A tractor-trailer or other commercial motor vehicle can block multiple lanes, carry hazardous or specialized cargo, require heavy recovery equipment, and create additional risks for people working near the roadway.

The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) describes traffic incident management as a coordinated process involving transportation agencies, law enforcement, fire and rescue, emergency medical services, towing and recovery companies, and other responders. Commercial vehicle crashes can require additional resources because of the size and weight of the vehicles, damaged trailers, cargo spills, and the potential for serious injuries.

1. Establish Scene Safety

The first operational objective is to protect crash victims, responders, and approaching motorists.

Responders may initially assess:

  • The position of the truck and other vehicles
  • Traffic flow and blocked lanes
  • Fire or smoke
  • Fuel or fluid leaks
  • Damaged electrical systems
  • Unstable vehicles or trailers
  • Cargo spills
  • Potential hazardous materials
  • Downed utility lines
  • The possibility of additional collisions

Commercial trucks can create a particularly large safety perimeter because a tractor-trailer may occupy substantial roadway space or become unstable after a rollover.

FHWA emphasizes traffic control as a fundamental part of traffic incident management because responders working near moving traffic can be exposed to secondary collisions.

2. Notify Emergency and Transportation Agencies

The appropriate emergency communications should provide enough information for dispatchers to understand the scale of the incident.

Useful information can include:

  • Exact crash location
  • Highway, route, or intersection
  • Direction of travel
  • Number of vehicles involved
  • Whether a commercial vehicle is involved
  • Apparent injuries
  • Whether anyone is trapped
  • Whether lanes are blocked
  • Whether the truck has overturned
  • Whether cargo has spilled
  • Whether hazardous materials may be involved
  • Whether fire, smoke, or a significant leak is present

The information may be updated as responders learn more.

A commercial truck crash can require coordination among several organizations rather than a response from a single agency. FHWA's traffic incident management framework specifically includes law enforcement, fire/rescue, EMS, transportation agencies, towing and recovery providers, and other responders.

3. Establish Traffic Control

Once emergency personnel arrive, controlling traffic becomes a major part of scene management.

Depending on the roadway and collision, responders may establish:

  • Lane closures
  • Shoulder closures
  • Detours
  • Traffic-control points
  • Warning signs
  • Cones or other channelizing devices
  • Emergency vehicle positioning
  • Variable message signs
  • Temporary traffic restrictions

The goal is to create a protected working area while allowing traffic to move safely when possible.

Commercial truck crashes can produce particularly long traffic disruptions because removing a large vehicle may require specialized recovery equipment. FHWA identifies safe and efficient incident clearance as an important component of traffic incident management.

4. Assess Injuries and Provide Emergency Medical Care

EMS personnel evaluate injured people and determine the appropriate level of treatment and transportation.

A truck crash may involve:

  • Truck occupants
  • Passenger-vehicle occupants
  • Pedestrians
  • Motorcyclists
  • Road workers
  • Other people near the collision

Severely damaged vehicles may require fire and rescue personnel to stabilize the vehicle or perform extrication before an injured occupant can be removed.

The driver's condition should also be considered independently of the condition of the vehicle. A truck that appears relatively intact can still contain occupants requiring medical evaluation.

5. Identify the Commercial Vehicle

Because commercial vehicles are subject to additional federal and state requirements, responders and investigators may need to identify the truck and carrier.

Information can include:

  • Tractor identification
  • Trailer identification
  • Motor carrier name
  • USDOT number
  • License plate information
  • Driver identification
  • Shipping or cargo information
  • Vehicle configuration

FMCSA defines a police crash report as a report used to collect information at the scene of a motor vehicle traffic crash. A post-crash investigation can involve additional information beyond the standard crash report.

6. Determine Whether Hazardous Materials Are Involved

Cargo identification can become particularly important when a commercial vehicle is carrying regulated hazardous materials.

Responders should not assume that cargo is safe merely because there is no visible fire or spill.

A damaged trailer may contain materials that present risks through:

  • Fire
  • Explosion
  • Toxic exposure
  • Corrosive substances
  • Environmental contamination

Emergency personnel may use shipping papers, vehicle markings, placards, container information, and other available information to identify cargo and determine appropriate response procedures.

Specialized hazardous-materials personnel may be required when the situation exceeds the capabilities of the initial responders.

7. Stabilize the Truck and Trailer

Large commercial vehicles can present mechanical and structural hazards after a collision.

Depending on the circumstances, responders may need to address:

  • Rollover conditions
  • Damaged landing gear
  • Trailer separation
  • Unstable cargo
  • Damaged fuel systems
  • Air-brake or suspension damage
  • Collapsed structures
  • Vehicles resting against barriers or other vehicles

Stabilization should be handled by appropriately trained personnel using equipment suited to the vehicle and circumstances.

The objective is to prevent further movement or collapse while allowing rescue, investigation, and recovery operations to proceed.

8. Preserve Evidence While Managing the Emergency

Emergency response takes priority over evidence preservation. However, once immediate hazards are controlled, investigators may document conditions that could later become important.

Potential evidence can include:

  • Vehicle positions
  • Skid or tire marks
  • Debris fields
  • Roadway conditions
  • Traffic signs and signals
  • Vehicle damage
  • Cargo condition
  • Lighting conditions
  • Surveillance footage
  • Electronic vehicle information
  • Driver records
  • Witness accounts
  • Photographs and measurements

Investigators may also document the condition of truck components.

The purpose of this documentation is not necessarily to determine civil liability at the scene. A later insurance or legal investigation can consider evidence from multiple sources.

9. Conduct a Post-Crash Commercial Vehicle Inspection

Commercial truck crashes may trigger a post-crash inspection by qualified commercial motor vehicle inspectors.

FMCSA defines a post-crash inspection as an inspection performed by a certified CMV inspector that documents violations and defects discovered during the inspection. The inspection can help identify whether a regulatory issue may have contributed to the crash and whether a defect existed before or resulted from the collision.

This distinction can be important.

For example, a damaged brake component found after a collision does not automatically establish that the component was defective before the crash. Investigators may need to determine whether the condition resulted from the collision itself.

FMCSA guidance specifically recognizes the importance of distinguishing pre-crash violations from conditions that occurred because of the crash.

10. Review the Driver and Carrier's Records

Depending on the circumstances, investigators may examine records relevant to the truck's operation.

Potential areas include:

  • Driver qualification information
  • Hours-of-service records
  • Inspection records
  • Maintenance documentation
  • Vehicle inspection reports
  • Dispatch information
  • Electronic logging device information
  • Shipping papers
  • Training records
  • Prior inspection information

Federal motor carrier regulations impose inspection, repair, and maintenance obligations on covered commercial motor vehicles. For example, FMCSA regulations require motor carriers to systematically inspect, repair, and maintain vehicles under their control and address certain safety-related defects before operation.

Whether a particular record is relevant depends on the facts of the crash and the applicable federal or state requirements.

11. Follow Applicable Post-Accident Testing Requirements

Some commercial truck crashes can trigger federal drug and alcohol testing requirements.

FMCSA's rules distinguish among different accident circumstances. Post-accident testing requirements can apply following a fatality, certain injury crashes involving immediate medical treatment away from the scene, and certain tow-away crashes when the regulatory conditions are met.

For example, FMCSA identifies a fatality as an event requiring testing, while some injury and tow-away situations depend on whether the commercial driver received a citation for a moving violation arising from the crash.

The applicable requirements and timing should be reviewed carefully because the federal testing rules contain specific conditions and deadlines.

12. Coordinate Vehicle Recovery

Once emergency treatment and necessary investigation are complete, damaged vehicles and cargo may need to be recovered.

A commercial truck recovery can require:

  • Heavy-duty tow trucks
  • Cranes
  • Specialized recovery vehicles
  • Cargo transfer
  • Trailer recovery
  • Debris removal
  • Fluid cleanup
  • Roadway repair

The recovery plan should account for vehicle stability, cargo, roadway conditions, and the safety of workers and motorists.

In some situations, investigators may need to complete important documentation before the truck is moved.

13. Clear the Roadway Safely

Incident clearance is not simply a matter of moving the truck as quickly as possible.

Responders may need to coordinate:

  1. Medical treatment
  2. Fire and rescue operations
  3. Investigation
  4. Traffic control
  5. Hazardous-material management
  6. Vehicle recovery
  7. Cargo removal
  8. Debris cleanup
  9. Roadway inspection
  10. Reopening lanes

FHWA notes that commercial vehicle crashes can create longer delays because of vehicle size, damaged trailers, cargo spills, and other complications. Specialized equipment and coordinated procedures can therefore be important for safe clearance.

14. Complete Required Reports

After the immediate response, agencies and involved organizations may have reporting responsibilities.

Depending on the circumstances, documentation can include:

  • Police crash reports
  • Post-crash inspection reports
  • EMS records
  • Fire department reports
  • Hazardous-material reports
  • Towing and recovery records
  • Carrier incident reports
  • Insurance documentation

FMCSA distinguishes a standard police crash report from a more detailed post-crash investigation and from a commercial vehicle post-crash inspection. Each can serve a different purpose.

15. Understand the Difference Between Regulatory Findings and Civil Liability

A commercial vehicle inspection can identify a regulatory violation or vehicle defect, but that finding does not automatically establish civil liability for a collision.

Likewise, the absence of a documented violation does not necessarily establish that no negligence occurred.

A civil claim may involve separate questions concerning:

  • Duty
  • Negligence
  • Causation
  • Comparative fault
  • Injuries
  • Economic losses
  • Insurance coverage
  • Applicable state law

Evidence from the emergency response and commercial vehicle investigation may become relevant to those questions, but legal liability is determined under the applicable law and based on the evidence as a whole.

Final Takeaway

Standard operating procedures at commercial truck accident scenes generally follow a sequence built around scene safety, emergency medical care, traffic control, hazard identification, investigation, commercial vehicle inspection, recovery, and roadway clearance.

Because large trucks can create additional hazards involving vehicle stability, cargo, hazardous materials, and prolonged lane blockage, effective response often requires coordination among multiple agencies and specialized recovery personnel. FHWA's traffic incident management framework emphasizes this multidisciplinary approach.

FMCSA rules can also create additional post-crash responsibilities involving commercial vehicle inspections, carrier records, and, in qualifying circumstances, driver drug and alcohol testing.
