Humanitarian Protection Workflows and Credible Fear Screenings

Humanitarian protection systems provide legal mechanisms for individuals who may face persecution, torture, or other serious harm if returned to a particular country. Depending on the jurisdiction, protection may be available through asylum, refugee status, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT), or other humanitarian programs.

In the United States, credible fear screening is an important part of the process for certain individuals placed in expedited removal proceedings. The screening is designed to determine whether an individual may have a sufficient basis to pursue certain forms of protection through a subsequent process. It is not itself a final grant of asylum or other protection.

The Purpose of Humanitarian Protection

Humanitarian protection generally addresses circumstances in which ordinary immigration categories do not adequately protect a person from serious danger.

Asylum, for example, can protect qualifying individuals who have suffered persecution or have a well-founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. Other forms of protection have different eligibility requirements and legal consequences.

Withholding of removal and CAT protection are particularly important distinctions. A person may be unable to qualify for asylum but still potentially qualify for another form of protection if the applicable legal standard is satisfied.

Because each protection category has separate requirements, applicants should identify the specific legal basis for the claim rather than treating all humanitarian protection as a single process.

The Credible Fear Screening Process

In the United States, credible fear screening is associated primarily with expedited removal procedures. The Department of Justice explains that Immigration Judges have jurisdiction over limited credible fear proceedings as well as other specialized immigration proceedings.

Under the regulatory framework, an asylum officer conducts the initial screening in applicable cases. The purpose is not to decide the entire asylum claim. Instead, the screening determines whether the person has established a significant possibility of qualifying for asylum or, where applicable, withholding of removal or protection under the Convention Against Torture under the standards governing the screening.

The distinction between screening and final adjudication is important. A positive screening result does not automatically establish eligibility for protection.

Initial Interview With an Asylum Officer

The credible fear process generally involves an interview with an asylum officer. The applicant has an opportunity to explain why they fear returning to the relevant country and to provide information concerning the circumstances underlying the fear.

The interview can involve questions about:

  • past persecution or serious harm;
  • threats or anticipated future harm;
  • the identity or characteristics of the alleged persecutors;
  • the reason the applicant believes they would be targeted;
  • possible relocation within the country;
  • government involvement or inability to provide protection; and
  • other facts relevant to the applicable protection standard.

The officer evaluates the information under the legal framework governing credible fear screenings.

The applicant's account should be truthful and as complete as reasonably possible. Inconsistencies, omissions, or misunderstandings can become relevant during later proceedings, although the significance of any particular inconsistency depends on the circumstances.

Credibility and Supporting Evidence

Credibility can be important throughout humanitarian protection proceedings. Applicants may be asked to explain events that occurred months or years earlier, sometimes under stressful circumstances.

Supporting evidence can include identity records, medical documentation, police reports, threatening messages, photographs, witness statements, news reports, membership records, or other materials relevant to the claim.

Not every applicant will have documentary evidence. Persecution or threats may occur in circumstances where records are difficult or impossible to obtain. The absence of a particular document does not by itself determine the outcome of a protection claim.

The governing law establishes the applicable evidentiary and credibility standards. The REAL ID Act, for example, contains specific provisions concerning credibility and corroboration in U.S. asylum and related protection cases.

Positive and Negative Screening Results

A credible fear screening can produce different procedural consequences.

A positive determination generally allows the protection claim to proceed through the applicable adjudicatory process. Depending on the circumstances, the claim may proceed before an Immigration Judge or through another authorized procedure.

A negative determination can lead to review by an Immigration Judge under the applicable procedures. Importantly, the Board of Immigration Appeals generally does not have jurisdiction over credible fear determinations, whether made by an asylum officer or an Immigration Judge.

This makes it important to distinguish credible fear review from an ordinary appeal of an Immigration Judge's decision.

The Role of the Immigration Judge

An Immigration Judge may review a negative credible fear determination in the circumstances provided by law. EOIR identifies credible fear proceedings as a limited category of proceedings within Immigration Judge jurisdiction.

The judge's role at this stage differs from a full merits hearing on an asylum application. The applicable screening standard and procedural framework determine what the judge must evaluate.

If the case proceeds to a full protection claim, the applicant may later have to establish eligibility under the relevant substantive standards, which are generally more demanding than the preliminary screening inquiry.

Asylum, Withholding, and CAT Protection

Humanitarian protection claims should be distinguished according to their legal requirements.

Asylum generally concerns persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution connected to one or more protected grounds recognized under U.S. law.

Withholding of removal requires satisfaction of a different and generally higher showing concerning the likelihood of persecution on a protected ground. A grant of withholding does not provide exactly the same immigration status or benefits as asylum.

Convention Against Torture protection focuses on whether the individual is more likely than not to be tortured if removed to the proposed country. The legal requirements differ from those governing asylum.

These distinctions matter because evidence that is relevant to one form of protection may not establish eligibility for another.

Country Conditions and Individual Circumstances

Humanitarian protection cases often require consideration of both general country conditions and the applicant's individual circumstances.

Country reports, governmental publications, international organizations' reports, and reliable evidence concerning political, social, security, or human-rights conditions can help establish the broader environment.

However, general evidence does not automatically establish that a particular individual qualifies for protection. Decision-makers may examine how the country conditions relate to the applicant's specific experiences, characteristics, and anticipated risks.

Recent changes in country conditions can also become legally significant. In 2026, the Board of Immigration Appeals issued decisions addressing how changes in country conditions may affect assessments of prospective persecution.

Confidentiality and Sensitive Information

Protection claims frequently involve highly sensitive information. Applicants may discuss political activity, religious practices, family relationships, sexual orientation, criminal victimization, threats, detention, or traumatic experiences.

Applicants should provide accurate information while understanding how their information is handled within the relevant legal process. Immigration proceedings are governed by specific confidentiality and disclosure rules, and the treatment of information can depend on the type of proceeding and applicable regulations.

Professional legal assistance can be particularly useful where disclosure of certain information could create additional legal or personal risks.

Legal Representation and Preparation

An applicant may be able to obtain legal assistance during humanitarian protection proceedings. An attorney or authorized representative can help explain the applicable legal standards, organize evidence, prepare the applicant for interviews, and identify procedural issues.

Legal representation does not guarantee a positive result. The applicant remains responsible for providing truthful information and complying with the requirements of the relevant process.

USCIS also provides information concerning asylum offices and credible or reasonable fear screening procedures. Its current appointment system identifies credible fear and reasonable fear protection screening as specific categories of asylum-office-related services.

Administrative and Judicial Review

Humanitarian protection proceedings can involve multiple levels of review, but not every decision can be appealed through the same mechanism.

The Board of Immigration Appeals has authority to review many Immigration Judge decisions involving asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT protection. However, EOIR expressly states that the Board does not generally review credible fear determinations.

For ordinary Immigration Judge decisions that are appealable to the BIA, the current EOIR guidance states that a Notice of Appeal generally must be received within 30 calendar days after the Immigration Judge's oral decision or mailing of the written decision.

The applicable review mechanism therefore depends heavily on the procedural stage and type of decision.

Preparing for a Humanitarian Protection Screening

Applicants can prepare by organizing a clear chronology of relevant events and gathering available supporting documentation.

Useful preparation can include:

  1. Creating a timeline of threats, persecution, detention, violence, or other relevant events.
  2. Identifying the people or organizations responsible for the alleged harm.
  3. Explaining why the applicant believes the harm occurred or would occur.
  4. Gathering available documentary evidence.
  5. Identifying witnesses who may be able to corroborate important facts.
  6. Reviewing relevant country-condition evidence.
  7. Maintaining consistency between applications, interviews, declarations, and testimony.
  8. Obtaining qualified legal assistance when the case involves complicated legal or factual issues.

Applicants should never manufacture evidence or alter documents to strengthen a claim. False information can create additional immigration consequences.

The Broader Humanitarian Protection Workflow

The humanitarian protection process can be understood as a sequence of legal decisions rather than a single application.

An individual may first enter a screening process, followed by review where permitted. If the screening threshold is satisfied, the case can proceed to a more comprehensive determination of eligibility. If protection is denied at a later stage, the applicant may have access to specified administrative or judicial review mechanisms, depending on the decision.

Each stage has its own legal standard, evidentiary considerations, and procedural rules.

The distinction between screening and final adjudication is particularly important. A credible fear screening determines whether a protection claim should receive further consideration under the applicable framework; it does not itself establish that the applicant ultimately qualifies for asylum, withholding of removal, or CAT protection.
