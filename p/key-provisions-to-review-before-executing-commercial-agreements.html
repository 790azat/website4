Key Provisions to Review Before Executing Commercial Agreements

Commercial agreements establish the rights, responsibilities, and financial obligations of businesses involved in a transaction. Whether the document concerns the sale of goods, professional services, licensing, distribution, technology, or a strategic partnership, the language of the agreement can determine how disputes, costs, delays, and unexpected events are handled.

Before signing a commercial agreement, businesses should review more than the price and primary business terms. Provisions concerning performance, payment, liability, termination, dispute resolution, intellectual property, confidentiality, and governing law can significantly affect the parties' obligations throughout the relationship.

Because contract requirements and enforceability can vary by jurisdiction and transaction type, significant agreements should be reviewed in the context of applicable law and the specific circumstances of the parties.

Parties and Authority

The agreement should clearly identify every party entering the transaction. Legal names, business entities, addresses, and relevant affiliates should be accurately stated.

The parties should also confirm that the individuals signing the agreement have appropriate authority to bind their respective organizations. An agreement may create significant obligations for a company, making authority and corporate approval important preliminary considerations.

Where a transaction involves subsidiaries, parent companies, guarantors, or other affiliated entities, the agreement should clearly distinguish which entity assumes each obligation.

Scope of Goods or Services

The agreement should precisely describe what each party is required to provide.

For goods, relevant provisions may address specifications, quantities, delivery locations, acceptance standards, inspection procedures, and delivery schedules. For services, the contract may identify specific deliverables, performance standards, milestones, personnel requirements, and reporting obligations.

Ambiguous descriptions can create disagreements about whether a party has actually satisfied its contractual responsibilities.

A detailed statement of work or schedule may therefore be useful for transactions involving complex services or multiple deliverables.

Pricing and Payment Terms

Financial provisions should be reviewed carefully before execution. The agreement should specify the price or pricing methodology, invoicing procedures, payment deadlines, applicable taxes, reimbursable expenses, and consequences of late payment.

Businesses should also examine whether the agreement allows prices to change during the contract term. Automatic increases, adjustment formulas, minimum purchase requirements, volume commitments, or additional fees can materially affect the total cost of the relationship.

The payment structure should be consistent with the commercial arrangement and sufficiently clear to minimize disputes over when payment becomes due.

Representations and Warranties

Representations generally concern factual statements made by a party, while warranties establish contractual assurances concerning goods, services, performance, or other matters.

A contract may contain warranties concerning ownership, authority, quality, conformity with specifications, intellectual property rights, or compliance with applicable requirements.

The scope and duration of warranties should be reviewed carefully. A business should understand what happens if a warranty is breached, including whether the available remedy is repair, replacement, refund, re-performance, or another form of compensation.

Federal commercial contracts, for example, can contain express provisions addressing warranties and acceptance, illustrating why these provisions should be considered together rather than in isolation.

Indemnification Obligations

Indemnification provisions can allocate responsibility for certain third-party claims, losses, damages, or expenses.

A review should determine:

  • Which types of claims are covered
  • Whether the obligation applies to third-party claims or other losses
  • Whether attorney fees and defense costs are included
  • Whether negligence or misconduct is covered
  • Who controls the defense of a claim
  • Whether settlement requires the other party's consent
  • Whether the obligation is subject to a liability cap

The wording of an indemnification clause can have substantial financial consequences. Certain forms of indemnification may also be restricted or treated differently under applicable state law, making jurisdiction-specific review important.

Limitation of Liability

A limitation-of-liability provision determines whether and to what extent one party can be held financially responsible for losses.

The agreement may establish an overall monetary cap, exclude certain categories of damages, or create exceptions for particular types of misconduct or contractual breaches.

Parties should therefore examine the interaction between the liability cap, indemnification obligations, warranties, insurance requirements, and other remedies.

A contract that appears to contain a broad liability limitation may nevertheless contain exceptions that significantly expand potential exposure. Conversely, an overly restrictive limitation may leave an affected party with limited remedies if the other side fails to perform.

Confidentiality and Information Protection

Commercial relationships frequently require businesses to exchange sensitive information, including pricing data, customer information, business plans, technical information, financial records, and proprietary processes.

A confidentiality provision should identify what information is protected, permitted uses, disclosure exceptions, security obligations, and the duration of confidentiality requirements.

Where personal or regulated information is involved, the agreement may also need provisions addressing applicable privacy and data-security requirements.

The parties should pay particular attention to obligations that continue after termination.

Intellectual Property Rights

Intellectual property provisions are especially important in technology, consulting, design, software, research, marketing, and creative-service agreements.

The contract should distinguish between intellectual property that a party already owns and materials created during the contractual relationship.

Important questions include who owns newly developed work, whether either party receives a license, whether the license is exclusive or nonexclusive, and whether rights continue after termination.

Failure to address ownership clearly can create disputes over the use of software, designs, written materials, inventions, trademarks, databases, or other business assets.

Term and Renewal

The agreement should specify when the relationship begins and how long it continues.

Businesses should examine whether the contract automatically renews and, if so, how much advance notice is required to prevent renewal.

Automatic-renewal provisions can create continuing financial obligations if a company fails to provide notice within the specified period. Renewal provisions should therefore be considered alongside pricing adjustments and termination rights.

Termination Rights

Termination provisions determine the circumstances under which the parties can end the relationship.

A contract may permit termination for material breach, insolvency, failure to pay, prolonged force majeure events, convenience, or other specified circumstances.

The agreement should also establish notice requirements and the consequences of termination. These may include final payment, return of property, transition assistance, data deletion or return, continuing confidentiality obligations, and treatment of unfinished work.

Government commercial contracting rules, for example, separately address termination for cause and other termination mechanisms, demonstrating how termination rights can be structured around defined events and procedures.

Force Majeure and Excusable Delay

Force majeure provisions address circumstances that may prevent or delay performance because of events outside a party's reasonable control.

Depending on the contract, covered events may include natural disasters, certain government actions, labor disruptions, epidemics, transportation interruptions, or other specified events.

The provision should be reviewed for its definition of qualifying events, notice requirements, mitigation obligations, and consequences when the disruption continues for an extended period.

A force majeure clause does not automatically excuse every form of nonperformance. Its effect depends on the language of the agreement and applicable law.

Governing Law and Dispute Resolution

The governing-law provision identifies which jurisdiction's law will generally apply to the contract.

The dispute-resolution section may specify litigation, arbitration, mediation, or another procedure. It may also identify the location where disputes must be resolved.

These provisions can materially affect the cost and practical consequences of a dispute, particularly when the parties operate in different states or countries.

Businesses should therefore review governing-law and forum provisions before signing rather than waiting until a dispute arises.

Order of Precedence and Incorporated Documents

Commercial agreements frequently incorporate schedules, exhibits, statements of work, purchase orders, technical specifications, policies, or online terms.

If these documents contain conflicting provisions, the agreement should explain which document controls.

Order-of-precedence clauses are used in some commercial contracts specifically to determine which provisions govern when inconsistencies exist.

Businesses should review every incorporated document rather than evaluating only the principal agreement.

Assignment and Change of Control

Assignment provisions determine whether either party can transfer its contractual rights or obligations to another entity.

A company should determine whether assignment requires consent and whether exceptions apply to mergers, acquisitions, reorganizations, or transfers to affiliates.

Change-of-control provisions may create additional consequences if ownership of a company changes. These clauses can become particularly significant during mergers, acquisitions, financing transactions, or corporate restructuring.

Compliance and Insurance Requirements

Some commercial agreements require the parties to comply with specified laws, industry standards, licensing requirements, or regulatory obligations.

Insurance provisions may also establish minimum coverage requirements and require evidence of insurance.

The business should confirm that the required coverage is commercially available and consistent with its existing insurance program before agreeing to the obligation.

Compliance provisions should likewise be evaluated according to the actual activities contemplated by the agreement rather than accepted as generic boilerplate.

Final Review Before Execution

Before executing a commercial agreement, businesses should conduct a structured review covering:

  1. The identities and authority of the contracting parties.
  2. The precise scope of goods or services.
  3. Pricing, invoices, taxes, and payment deadlines.
  4. Representations and warranties.
  5. Indemnification obligations.
  6. Liability limitations and exclusions.
  7. Confidentiality and information-security requirements.
  8. Intellectual property ownership and licensing.
  9. Contract duration and renewal.
  10. Termination rights and post-termination obligations.
  11. Force majeure provisions.
  12. Governing law and dispute-resolution procedures.
  13. Assignment and change-of-control restrictions.
  14. Insurance and compliance requirements.
  15. Exhibits, schedules, statements of work, and incorporated terms.

The review should also confirm that the final version reflects all negotiated changes and that no provisions were unintentionally altered between negotiation and execution.

Conclusion

Commercial agreements can distribute financial, operational, and legal risks long after the transaction is completed. Reviewing only the headline business terms may therefore leave important obligations undiscovered.

A comprehensive contract review examines not only what each party must provide, but also what happens when performance is delayed, defective, disputed, terminated, or affected by unexpected events. Careful attention to liability, indemnification, payment, intellectual property, confidentiality, renewal, and dispute-resolution provisions can help businesses understand the contractual risks they are accepting before they become legally bound.
