Emergency Care and Documentation Steps Following Pedestrian Incidents

A pedestrian collision can create an immediate medical emergency while also leaving important questions about what happened, who was involved, and what evidence may exist. The first priority should always be protecting the injured person and obtaining appropriate medical care. Documentation should come afterward, when it can be completed safely.

Pedestrians are particularly vulnerable in motor-vehicle crashes. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) reports that 7,080 pedestrians were killed and more than 71,000 were injured in U.S. traffic crashes in 2024. NHTSA's Safe System approach specifically identifies prompt post-crash care and preventing secondary crashes as important components of pedestrian safety.

1. Move Away From Immediate Traffic Hazards

After a pedestrian incident, the scene may remain dangerous even after the initial impact.

If the pedestrian can move safely, getting away from active traffic may reduce the risk of a second collision. However, someone with a serious injury should not be encouraged to move unnecessarily unless remaining in place creates an immediate danger.

Potential hazards include:

  • Moving vehicles
  • A vehicle stopped in a travel lane
  • Fire or smoke
  • Fuel or fluid leaks
  • Broken glass
  • Downed wires
  • Debris
  • Poor visibility
  • Additional traffic approaching the scene

Drivers and bystanders should also avoid creating additional hazards while attempting to help.

2. Call 911 When Emergency Assistance Is Needed

A pedestrian struck by a motor vehicle should receive prompt evaluation when there is any significant injury or concern about the person's condition.

When calling 911, provide:

  • The exact location
  • The roadway or intersection
  • The direction of travel
  • Number of vehicles involved
  • Whether the pedestrian is conscious
  • Apparent injuries
  • Whether the person is trapped or unable to move
  • Whether traffic lanes are blocked
  • Whether fire, smoke, fuel, or another hazard is present

Accurate location information can help dispatchers send appropriate emergency resources.

NHTSA identifies expedited access to emergency medical care as part of its post-crash-care approach for vulnerable road users.

3. Follow Emergency Medical Instructions

Until EMS personnel arrive, bystanders should follow dispatcher instructions and avoid taking actions beyond their training.

If the injured person is conscious, remaining calm and providing reassurance may be useful while waiting for professional assistance.

Avoid giving the person food, drink, or medication unless instructed by qualified medical personnel.

If the person appears seriously injured, unnecessary movement should generally be avoided unless immediate danger requires relocation.

Once EMS arrives, provide responders with any information you observed about the incident and the person's condition.

4. Obtain a Medical Evaluation

Some injuries following a pedestrian collision may not be immediately obvious.

Depending on the circumstances, injuries can involve:

  • Head or brain trauma
  • Neck or spinal injuries
  • Broken bones
  • Internal injuries
  • Soft-tissue injuries
  • Cuts and abrasions
  • Joint injuries
  • Psychological effects

Symptoms can change after the initial collision. A person who initially feels relatively well may later develop pain, dizziness, weakness, confusion, or other symptoms.

Medical professionals should determine the appropriate evaluation and treatment.

Medical and EMS records can also become part of the documentation associated with a collision. NHTSA notes that hospital and EMS data can provide information about injury severity and the nature of injuries that may not appear in police crash records.

5. Do Not Prioritize Photographs Over Medical Care

Photographs can be useful evidence, but they should never delay emergency treatment.

A pedestrian who is injured should focus on medical care first. A bystander, responding officer, investigator, or another person may later document the scene.

If the injured person is able to document anything personally, this should only occur when doing so is safe and does not interfere with treatment or emergency operations.

6. Document the Scene When It Is Safe

Once immediate medical and safety concerns are addressed, photographs can help preserve information that may change quickly.

Potential subjects include:

  • The roadway
  • Crosswalks
  • Traffic signals
  • Pedestrian signals
  • Stop signs
  • Lane markings
  • Vehicle positions
  • Vehicle damage
  • Debris
  • Skid or tire marks
  • Road surface conditions
  • Nearby lighting
  • Obstructions to visibility
  • Construction areas
  • Relevant surrounding buildings

Take both close-up photographs and wider photographs showing the relationship between the evidence and the surrounding environment.

NHTSA's crash-investigation work includes detailed collection and analysis of vehicle-to-pedestrian crash information, including scene and injury data.

7. Document the Pedestrian's Location and Crossing Conditions

The precise circumstances surrounding a pedestrian's location can be important.

When safe and appropriate, document:

  • The crosswalk
  • The intersection
  • Curb ramps
  • Pedestrian signals
  • Walk or don't-walk indicators
  • Traffic signals
  • Nearby bus stops
  • Median areas
  • Sidewalks
  • Lighting
  • Obstructions
  • The approximate direction the pedestrian was traveling

Do not attempt to recreate the collision by standing in traffic or placing yourself in a dangerous position.

The objective is to preserve existing conditions rather than alter the scene.

8. Photograph Vehicle Damage

The involved vehicle should be documented from multiple angles when circumstances permit.

Photographs may include:

  • Front-end damage
  • Hood damage
  • Windshield damage
  • Mirrors
  • Headlights
  • Bumpers
  • Side panels
  • Paint transfer
  • Broken parts
  • Damage to other vehicles or objects

A wider photograph can show where the vehicle was located, while close-ups can document specific damage.

Do not interfere with police investigation or move evidence simply to obtain a better photograph.

9. Identify Witnesses

People who saw the incident may have information about events immediately before and after the collision.

If witnesses are willing to provide contact information, record:

  • Name
  • Telephone number
  • Email address
  • General location from which they observed the incident

Avoid coaching witnesses or asking them to agree with a particular interpretation of the collision.

Witnesses may have different perspectives, and investigators can evaluate their accounts alongside other evidence.

10. Look for Video Evidence

Video recordings can sometimes provide valuable information about a pedestrian collision.

Potential sources include:

  • Traffic cameras
  • Business security cameras
  • Residential security systems
  • Parking-lot cameras
  • Dashcams
  • Nearby vehicle cameras
  • Body-worn cameras operated by law enforcement

If a camera appears to cover the location, record where it is located and, when possible, identify the property or organization operating it.

Video systems may overwrite older recordings automatically. Identifying potentially relevant footage promptly can therefore be important.

11. Preserve Digital Information

Smartphones and other devices may contain time-stamped information relevant to an incident.

Depending on the circumstances, potentially useful material can include:

  • Photographs
  • Videos
  • Location information
  • Navigation records
  • Messages
  • Calls
  • Time-stamped applications
  • Wearable-device information

Original files should be preserved where possible.

If photographs or videos need to be shared, keep the original files separately rather than relying only on edited or compressed copies.

12. Record Roadway and Environmental Conditions

Conditions at the scene can change quickly.

If it is safe to do so, document:

  • Weather
  • Lighting
  • Rain or standing water
  • Snow or ice
  • Road debris
  • Potholes
  • Construction
  • Temporary barriers
  • Visibility obstructions
  • Street lighting
  • Traffic volume

The time and approximate location of photographs can also be recorded.

These details may help investigators understand the environment at the time of the collision.

13. Obtain Police Report Information

When law enforcement responds, ask how to obtain the collision report and record the relevant report number or agency information.

A police crash report may document:

  • People involved
  • Vehicles
  • Location
  • Roadway characteristics
  • Officer observations
  • Statements
  • Reported injuries
  • Other crash information

NHTSA's national crash databases rely on information from state documents, including police crash reports, EMS reports, medical records, and other sources.

A police report is one source of evidence, however, and does not necessarily resolve every question concerning fault or civil liability.

14. Notify the Appropriate Insurance Companies

A pedestrian involved in a collision may need to determine what insurance coverage applies.

Depending on the circumstances, potentially relevant insurance can include:

  • The driver's automobile liability insurance
  • The pedestrian's own automobile insurance, if applicable
  • Other potentially applicable coverage

Insurance requirements and available coverage vary by state and by the facts of the incident.

When communicating with an insurer, provide accurate factual information and preserve copies of correspondence and documents.

15. Keep Medical and Financial Records

After the incident, maintain an organized file containing relevant documentation.

This can include:

  • Emergency medical records
  • Hospital records
  • Physician notes
  • Diagnostic results
  • Prescription records
  • Medical bills
  • Therapy records
  • Transportation expenses
  • Lost-work documentation
  • Property-damage records
  • Insurance correspondence
  • Police reports

Keeping records together can make it easier to understand the sequence of events and respond to requests from insurers or other parties.

16. Preserve Damaged Personal Property

A pedestrian may have personal items damaged during a collision.

Examples include:

  • Clothing
  • Shoes
  • Eyeglasses
  • Watches
  • Phones
  • Bags
  • Bicycles or scooters
  • Other personal equipment

Photograph damaged items before disposing of or repairing them when practical.

Receipts, photographs, repair estimates, or replacement records may help document the nature and value of property damage.

17. Avoid Altering or Destroying Potential Evidence

Important evidence can disappear through ordinary cleanup, repairs, or disposal.

Where practical, preserve:

  • Original photographs
  • Original videos
  • Damaged clothing
  • Damaged personal property
  • Relevant correspondence
  • Medical records
  • Repair estimates
  • Witness information
  • Insurance documents

Evidence should be preserved without interfering with legitimate emergency operations or official investigations.

18. Be Careful When Discussing Fault

A pedestrian collision can involve complicated factual and legal questions.

At the scene, focus on what was actually observed rather than attempting to reach a legal conclusion.

For example, someone may describe:

  • Which direction the vehicle was traveling
  • Whether a traffic signal was visible
  • Where the pedestrian was located
  • What the person observed immediately before impact
  • What happened immediately afterward

Those observations can later be evaluated alongside photographs, video, witness statements, police records, and other evidence.

Traffic rules differ among jurisdictions, and a particular traffic violation does not necessarily resolve every issue involved in a civil claim.

19. Keep Social-Media Posts Limited

Posts, photographs, and comments made after a collision can potentially become part of a later dispute.

It is generally prudent to avoid posting speculative accusations, detailed theories about fault, or statements that could be misunderstood without the surrounding context.

Private communications and social-media activity should not be altered or destroyed simply because a collision occurred. If litigation is reasonably anticipated, preservation obligations may become relevant.

20. Understand That Documentation Is Only One Part of a Claim

Evidence gathered after a pedestrian collision can be important, but no single photograph, police report, witness statement, or medical record necessarily determines legal responsibility.

Depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, a claim may involve questions concerning:

  • Negligence
  • Traffic laws
  • Pedestrian and driver conduct
  • Comparative fault
  • Insurance coverage
  • Injury causation
  • Medical expenses
  • Lost income
  • Property damage
  • Applicable filing deadlines

State law can differ significantly, particularly concerning comparative negligence, insurance coverage, governmental entities, and deadlines for bringing claims.

Final Takeaway

After a pedestrian incident, the appropriate sequence is generally safety first, emergency medical care second, and documentation once the immediate danger has been addressed.

Useful documentation may include photographs of the roadway and vehicles, information about traffic signals and crossing conditions, witness contacts, available video sources, medical records, police-report information, insurance communications, and records of related expenses.

NHTSA's pedestrian-safety framework specifically emphasizes post-crash care and preventing secondary crashes, while its crash-data programs recognize police, EMS, medical, and other records as important sources of information about pedestrian collisions.

For significant injuries, disputed circumstances, or questions about insurance and legal deadlines, the applicable state law and facts of the incident should be reviewed carefully. An attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction can explain how those rules may apply.
