Venture Formation: Essential Legal Checklists for Startup Founders

Launching a startup involves more than developing a product or attracting customers. Founders must also establish a legal and organizational framework that defines ownership, management authority, intellectual property rights, financial responsibilities, and relationships with employees and investors.

The legal structure selected during formation can affect taxation, fundraising, governance, liability, and the ability to transfer ownership. While startup requirements vary by jurisdiction and business model, a structured legal checklist can help founders identify important issues before the company begins significant operations.

Choosing a Business Structure

One of the first legal decisions is selecting an appropriate business structure. Common structures in the United States include sole proprietorships, partnerships, limited liability companies, and corporations.

For venture-backed startups, a corporation is often considered because it can facilitate equity issuance and investment transactions. Delaware corporations are frequently used by U.S. startups, although the appropriate jurisdiction depends on the company's circumstances.

The U.S. Small Business Administration explains that business structure affects registration requirements, taxes, personal liability, and other legal considerations. (sba.gov)

Founders should consider factors such as:

  • Number of founders
  • Ownership arrangements
  • Liability protection
  • Tax treatment
  • Planned fundraising
  • Employee equity
  • Administrative requirements
  • Geographic location of operations

Registering the Business

Once a structure is selected, the company generally needs to be formally created under applicable state or national law.

For a corporation, formation commonly involves filing organizational documents with the relevant state authority. The company may also need a registered agent, business address, governing documents, and other state-level registrations.

Depending on where the company operates, additional registrations, licenses, permits, or foreign qualification may be required.

Preparing Founders' Agreements

When multiple people establish a startup, founders should document their relationship early.

A founders' agreement can address:

  • Ownership percentages
  • Initial capital contributions
  • Responsibilities
  • Decision-making authority
  • Intellectual property ownership
  • Founder departures
  • Equity vesting
  • Confidentiality
  • Dispute resolution
  • Restrictions on transferring shares

Informal assumptions can become difficult to resolve after the company grows. Written agreements help establish expectations before significant value develops.

Establishing Ownership and Equity

Founders should maintain accurate records showing who owns the business and how ownership may change.

A capitalization table, commonly called a cap table, can track:

  • Founder shares
  • Investor shares
  • Employee equity
  • Options
  • Warrants
  • Convertible instruments
  • Reserved equity pools

Ownership records should be updated after significant equity transactions.

For corporations issuing securities, founders should also consider applicable federal and state securities laws. The Securities and Exchange Commission explains that offers and sales of securities generally must either be registered or qualify for an exemption from registration. (sec.gov)

Addressing Founder Vesting

Founder equity may be subject to vesting arrangements designed to protect the company if a founder leaves.

A typical arrangement may provide that shares vest over a specified period, potentially with an initial cliff. The precise structure should be documented carefully because vesting affects ownership, taxation, and the treatment of departing founders.

Founders should understand the distinction between shares that have already vested and shares subject to future vesting conditions.

Protecting Intellectual Property

Intellectual property can represent a startup's most valuable asset. Founders should establish who owns software, inventions, designs, trademarks, written materials, databases, and other intellectual property created for the business.

A startup should determine whether intellectual property was created:

  • Before incorporation
  • By a founder
  • By an employee
  • By an independent contractor
  • By another company
  • Using third-party technology

Written assignment agreements can help establish ownership where appropriate.

The U.S. Patent and Trademark Office provides resources concerning patents, trademarks, and other intellectual property protections. (uspto.gov)

Registering and Protecting Trademarks

A startup should consider whether its company name, product names, logos, or other branding elements conflict with existing trademarks.

Before investing heavily in a brand, founders can conduct appropriate searches and evaluate whether registration is appropriate.

Trademark protection is distinct from company registration. Registering a business name with a state does not necessarily establish comprehensive trademark rights.

The USPTO provides federal trademark search and application resources for businesses considering trademark protection. (uspto.gov)

Establishing Confidentiality Protections

Startups routinely handle confidential information, including product plans, source code, customer information, pricing strategies, financial data, and business plans.

Confidentiality agreements can establish obligations concerning how protected information may be used and disclosed.

Founders should also consider internal security practices, access controls, document permissions, and procedures for departing employees and contractors.

Creating Employment and Contractor Agreements

As a startup begins hiring, written agreements can clarify the relationship between the company and its workers.

Depending on the role and jurisdiction, agreements may address:

  • Compensation
  • Duties
  • Confidentiality
  • Intellectual property
  • Equity compensation
  • Termination
  • Benefits
  • Restrictive provisions where legally permitted

Worker classification is also important. Calling someone an independent contractor does not necessarily determine their legal classification. Federal and state requirements may apply based on the actual nature of the working relationship.

The U.S. Department of Labor provides guidance concerning employee and independent-contractor classification under federal labor law. (dol.gov)

Obtaining an Employer Identification Number

A U.S. business may need an Employer Identification Number (EIN) for federal tax and administrative purposes.

The Internal Revenue Service provides EIN application and eligibility information and identifies circumstances in which businesses may need an EIN. (irs.gov)

An EIN may be needed for matters such as hiring employees, filing certain tax returns, or operating particular business structures.

Opening Business Financial Accounts

Startup finances should generally be separated from founders' personal finances.

The company should establish appropriate business banking and accounting arrangements and maintain records of:

  • Founder contributions
  • Business expenses
  • Revenue
  • Loans
  • Investments
  • Payroll
  • Taxes
  • Equity transactions

Maintaining accurate financial records can simplify tax reporting, investor due diligence, and future financing transactions.

Understanding Fundraising and Securities Laws

Raising money from investors involves legal considerations beyond negotiating valuation.

Depending on the transaction, a startup may issue common stock, preferred stock, convertible notes, or other securities. Federal and state securities laws may apply to these transactions.

Private offerings can sometimes qualify for exemptions from registration, including exemptions under Regulation D. The applicable requirements depend on the offering structure and circumstances. (sec.gov)

Founders should maintain appropriate records concerning investors, securities issued, offering documents, and required filings.

Preparing for Due Diligence

Investors and potential business partners may conduct legal and financial due diligence before completing a transaction.

A startup can prepare by maintaining an organized repository containing documents such as:

  • Formation documents
  • Governing agreements
  • Cap tables
  • Founder agreements
  • Intellectual property assignments
  • Employment agreements
  • Contractor agreements
  • Material customer contracts
  • Financing documents
  • Tax records
  • Licenses and permits
  • Privacy and security documentation

Organized records can make later financing or acquisition processes more efficient.

Reviewing Customer and Supplier Contracts

Startups often begin with relatively simple commercial relationships, but contracts can become increasingly important as the business scales.

Customer and supplier agreements should clearly establish pricing, deliverables, payment terms, warranties, intellectual property rights, confidentiality, liability limitations, termination rights, and dispute-resolution procedures where relevant.

Founders should also identify which agreements require approval from the company's board or investors.

Addressing Privacy and Data Protection

Startups that collect personal information should determine which privacy and data-protection requirements apply to their activities.

The legal requirements can depend on factors such as:

  • Type of information collected
  • Location of customers
  • Industry
  • Business model
  • Children's data
  • Health or financial information
  • Cross-border data transfers

The Federal Trade Commission provides guidance concerning privacy, data security, and businesses' obligations when handling consumer information. (ftc.gov)

Creating a Governance Framework

A startup should establish procedures for making significant corporate decisions.

Depending on the entity structure, governance documents may address:

  • Board composition
  • Officer appointments
  • Voting rights
  • Shareholder approvals
  • Equity issuances
  • Major transactions
  • Conflicts of interest
  • Meeting procedures

Good corporate records should reflect significant decisions and maintain consistency with the company's governing documents.

Maintaining Ongoing Compliance

Formation is only the beginning of a startup's legal obligations.

Companies may need to monitor annual reports, taxes, licenses, employment requirements, securities filings, intellectual property registrations, privacy obligations, and industry-specific regulations.

Founders should establish a compliance calendar so that recurring requirements are not overlooked as the company grows.

Startup Legal Checklist

Before beginning substantial operations, founders can review the following checklist:

Formation

  • Select an appropriate business structure.
  • Register the entity.
  • Establish governing documents.
  • Obtain required identification numbers.
  • Confirm applicable licenses and registrations.

Ownership

  • Document founder ownership.
  • Establish a capitalization table.
  • Address vesting arrangements.
  • Record equity issuances properly.

Intellectual Property

  • Identify existing intellectual property.
  • Execute appropriate assignment agreements.
  • Evaluate trademark protection.
  • Protect confidential information.

Employment

  • Prepare appropriate employee and contractor agreements.
  • Review worker-classification requirements.
  • Establish payroll and employment compliance procedures.

Fundraising

  • Determine the proposed financing structure.
  • Review applicable securities laws.
  • Maintain investor documentation.
  • Update the capitalization table.

Operations

  • Separate business and personal finances.
  • Review customer and supplier contracts.
  • Establish privacy and security practices.
  • Maintain corporate records.
  • Track recurring compliance deadlines.

Conclusion

Startup formation creates the legal foundation on which a venture will operate, raise capital, hire workers, develop intellectual property, and enter commercial relationships. Addressing ownership, governance, intellectual property, employment, financing, contracts, and regulatory compliance early can reduce uncertainty as the company develops.

The appropriate legal structure depends on the startup's business model, ownership, financing plans, location, and applicable laws. Founders should therefore treat a startup checklist as a framework for identifying issues rather than a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal and tax advice.
