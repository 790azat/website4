A Financial History of Contingency Fee Structures in Civil Law

Contingency fees are one of the most distinctive ways that legal services can be financed. Instead of requiring a client to pay an attorney entirely according to hours worked or through an upfront fixed fee, a contingency arrangement generally links the lawyer's compensation to the financial outcome of a case.

The model has an important economic function: it can allow a person with a potentially valuable legal claim but limited funds to obtain representation without paying the lawyer's full fees at the beginning of the dispute.

At the same time, contingency fees create a different allocation of risk. The lawyer may invest substantial time and money in a case without receiving an attorney fee if there is no recovery. Because of that risk, legal systems have developed rules governing when contingency arrangements are permitted, how they must be documented, and what safeguards apply.

The history of contingency fees is therefore also a history of access to civil justice, professional ethics, litigation finance, and the changing economics of legal practice.

What Is a Contingency Fee?

A contingency fee is an arrangement in which an attorney's compensation depends, at least in part, on the outcome of a legal matter.

The lawyer commonly receives an agreed percentage of a monetary recovery if the client obtains compensation.

The arrangement may specify different percentages depending on how the case ends. For example, the agreement may distinguish between:

  • Settlement before litigation
  • Settlement after a lawsuit is filed
  • Trial
  • Appeal

The treatment of litigation expenses can also vary.

The American Bar Association's Model Rule 1.5 requires contingency agreements to explain how the fee will be calculated, including applicable percentages and whether litigation expenses are deducted before or after calculating the attorney's fee.

The exact requirements vary by jurisdiction.

The Economic Problem Contingency Fees Address

Civil litigation can be expensive.

A claimant may have suffered significant financial losses but lack the resources to pay an attorney by the hour for months or years.

Without alternative payment arrangements, the cost of pursuing a claim could prevent some people from bringing otherwise viable cases.

Contingency fees change that economic structure.

Instead of paying the lawyer entirely in advance, the client and lawyer effectively share the financial risk of litigation. The lawyer contributes professional time and may advance or incur certain case expenses, while compensation depends on obtaining a recovery.

This model can therefore function as a form of risk allocation within the attorney-client relationship.

Early American Recognition

Contingency-fee arrangements have a long history in American law.

The U.S. Supreme Court recognized contingent-fee contracts in cases dating back to the nineteenth century. In Ball v. Halsell, the Court explained that federal courts had upheld arrangements under which attorneys representing claims against the United States would receive a percentage of the recovery if successful and nothing as an attorney fee if the claim failed.

The Court identified an economic justification for these arrangements: people with limited resources could otherwise face substantial difficulties pursuing legitimate claims.

This demonstrates that contingency fees were not simply a modern response to personal-injury litigation. They were already part of American legal practice during the nineteenth century.

Nineteenth-Century Development

During the nineteenth century, American courts increasingly recognized contingent compensation arrangements in certain civil matters.

The Supreme Court's historical discussion in Ball v. Halsell identified several nineteenth-century decisions—including cases from 1853, 1875, 1876, and 1883—in which contingency arrangements for certain claims were upheld.

The economic logic was relatively straightforward.

If a claimant could not afford to pay an attorney upfront, an agreement based on the eventual recovery allowed the attorney to assume some of the financial uncertainty.

The arrangement could also provide an incentive for the lawyer to pursue a claim that might otherwise never reach court.

Formal Professional Recognition

The American Bar Association formally addressed contingency fees in its first Canons of Professional Ethics in 1908.

The U.S. Supreme Court later noted that the 1908 ABA Canons recognized the contingency or certainty of compensation as a factor relevant to determining an appropriate fee.

This was an important development because contingency compensation was no longer simply a matter of individual contractual practice. It became part of the profession's formal discussion about reasonable attorney compensation.

The recognition did not mean that every possible contingency arrangement was automatically permissible.

Professional rules continued to impose restrictions and ethical requirements.

Contingency Fees and Access to Justice

One of the strongest arguments historically associated with contingency fees is access to legal representation.

A person injured by another party may face:

  • Medical expenses
  • Lost income
  • Property damage
  • Long-term care costs
  • Reduced earning capacity

At the same time, the person may have little available cash to finance litigation.

A contingency arrangement can allow the attorney to pursue the claim while receiving compensation only if a recovery occurs.

The economic relationship is therefore different from conventional hourly representation.

With hourly billing, the client generally pays as legal work is performed.

With a contingency arrangement, the lawyer accepts a greater risk that substantial work will produce no attorney fee.

The Lawyer's Economic Risk

The contingency model does not eliminate the cost of legal work.

Instead, it changes who carries more of the uncertainty.

A lawyer accepting a contingency matter may spend considerable time on:

  • Case investigation
  • Legal research
  • Discovery
  • Depositions
  • Expert witnesses
  • Negotiations
  • Court proceedings
  • Trial preparation

If the case produces no recovery, the lawyer may receive no contingency fee.

This means that law firms must evaluate potential contingency cases partly as financial investments.

They may consider:

  • Liability evidence
  • Damages
  • Available insurance
  • Defendant resources
  • Litigation costs
  • Expected duration
  • Probability of success
  • Potential recovery

The exact business calculations differ between firms.

Contingency Fees and Case Selection

Because the lawyer's compensation depends on the outcome, contingency arrangements can influence which cases a firm is willing to accept.

A case with substantial potential damages but uncertain liability may be economically different from a case with strong evidence and a clearly identifiable source of compensation.

Similarly, a case requiring extensive expert testimony can involve much greater costs than a dispute that can be resolved through relatively straightforward evidence.

This does not mean that a contingency lawyer simply chooses cases according to financial value. Professional obligations continue to govern the attorney-client relationship.

The economic structure does, however, create a different risk profile from hourly representation.

The Rise of Hourly Billing

Interestingly, contingency fees predated the widespread use of modern hourly billing.

The Supreme Court explained in Gisbrecht v. Barnhart that hourly records were initially used primarily as an internal accounting mechanism. Attorneys historically used multiple methods of determining fees, including annual retainers, fee-for-service arrangements, estimated fees, and contingency fees.

The Court noted that the ABA's 1958 report on the economics of law practice encouraged lawyers to record hours worked, and hourly billing subsequently became increasingly common.

By the early 1970s, hourly billing had become widespread in American legal practice.

This historical sequence is important: contingency billing was not created as a variation of hourly billing. The two models developed along partly independent paths.

Different Economic Models

Modern legal practice can use several fee structures.

Fee model Basic economic structure
Hourly Client pays according to time worked
Fixed fee Client pays an agreed amount for defined services
Retainer Client pays an advance or fee for specified representation or availability
Contingency Lawyer's fee depends on the outcome
Hybrid Combines two or more fee structures

Contingency arrangements are particularly associated with civil claims involving monetary recovery, but their availability depends on applicable professional rules and other law.

The Development of Ethical Restrictions

As contingency fees became established, professional regulators increasingly focused on potential conflicts of interest.

The concern is straightforward: when an attorney's compensation depends on the amount recovered, the lawyer has a financial interest in the outcome.

That interest does not automatically make the arrangement improper. But it can create situations in which the lawyer's financial interests need to be balanced against the client's objectives.

Modern professional rules therefore address both the reasonableness of fees and the disclosure of contingency arrangements.

The Modern ABA Model Rule

ABA Model Rule 1.5 permits contingency fees except where prohibited by the rule or other law.

The rule requires a contingency-fee agreement to be in writing and signed by the client. It must explain how the fee will be calculated, including percentages applicable to settlement, trial, or appeal, as well as the treatment of litigation expenses.

The rule also requires a written statement at the conclusion of the matter showing the outcome and, when there is a recovery, the amount remitted to the client and the method used to calculate it.

These requirements reflect a broader shift from informal fee arrangements toward documented and transparent financial relationships.

Reasonableness of Contingency Fees

A contingency fee is not automatically reasonable simply because the client agreed to it.

ABA Model Rule 1.5 identifies several factors relevant to whether a fee is reasonable, including:

  • Time and labor required
  • Difficulty of the matter
  • Skill required
  • Customary fees in the locality
  • Amount involved
  • Results obtained
  • Time limitations
  • Lawyer's experience and reputation
  • Whether the fee is fixed or contingent

The ABA commentary also notes that applicable law may impose additional restrictions, including percentage ceilings or requirements to offer an alternative fee basis.

State professional-conduct rules can therefore impose requirements beyond the ABA Model Rules.

Why Some Matters Exclude Contingency Fees

Modern ethical rules prohibit contingency arrangements in certain areas because the financial incentive can create conflicts with important legal or public interests.

Under ABA Model Rule 1.5, lawyers may not charge a contingency fee for representing a defendant in a criminal case. The rule also prohibits fees contingent on obtaining a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support, or property settlement in certain domestic-relations matters.

The ABA has explained that these restrictions reflect concerns about conflicts of interest and the difficulty of defining an appropriate successful outcome in such matters.

These are model rules, however. The rules actually governing a lawyer are those adopted in the relevant jurisdiction.

The Difference Between Fee and Expenses

A contingency fee does not necessarily mean that every cost associated with a lawsuit disappears.

Cases can generate expenses for:

  • Filing fees
  • Expert witnesses
  • Medical records
  • Depositions
  • Court reporters
  • Investigators
  • Travel
  • Document preparation
  • Specialized testing

A contingency agreement should explain who is responsible for these expenses and how they affect the final distribution.

Under ABA Model Rule 1.5, the agreement must state which litigation and other expenses will be deducted from the recovery and whether those expenses are deducted before or after calculating the attorney's contingency fee.

That distinction can materially affect the amount ultimately received by the client.

Contingency Fees in Personal-Injury Litigation

Personal-injury cases became one of the most recognizable applications of contingency compensation.

The economic structure can be particularly relevant because an injured person may have immediate expenses while being unable to work.

A contingency arrangement can allow the claimant to obtain legal representation without paying an hourly fee throughout the case.

If the claim produces a recovery, the attorney receives the agreed compensation according to the contract.

If there is no recovery, the attorney may not receive the contingency fee, subject to the agreement and applicable rules concerning expenses.

Settlement and Trial Percentages

Contingency agreements sometimes use different percentages depending on how far the case progresses.

A contract might provide one percentage for a pre-litigation settlement and another after litigation begins.

The rationale is that later stages can require substantially more attorney time and expense.

ABA Model Rule 1.5 expressly requires the agreement to identify percentages applicable to settlement, trial, or appeal when those different percentages are part of the arrangement.

Clients should therefore read the entire fee agreement rather than focusing only on the headline percentage.

Contingency Fees and Settlement Decisions

Settlement creates an important economic issue.

The client generally decides whether to accept a settlement, subject to the lawyer's professional responsibilities and applicable law.

A lawyer's fee may depend on the amount recovered, which means the lawyer has a financial interest in the size of the settlement.

This makes clear communication important.

The lawyer must represent the client's interests rather than treating the client's claim as merely a financial investment.

Professional rules concerning conflicts of interest, communication, and reasonable fees continue to apply.

Modern Transparency Requirements

Over time, contingency-fee regulation has moved toward greater transparency.

A modern agreement can specify:

  • The percentage charged
  • Different percentages at different stages
  • Litigation expenses
  • How expenses are calculated
  • Whether expenses are deducted before or after the fee
  • Client responsibilities
  • What happens if there is no recovery

The ABA Model Rule also requires a final written accounting when there is a recovery.

These requirements help the client understand the economic consequences of the arrangement before entering into it.

Contingency Fees Outside the United States

Contingency-fee structures are not identical across countries.

Legal systems differ substantially in how they regulate attorney compensation, fee sharing, litigation funding, court costs, and recovery of legal expenses.

Some jurisdictions have historically restricted percentage-based fees more heavily than the United States. Others permit forms of success-based compensation subject to professional or statutory safeguards.

The existence of a contingency model in one country therefore does not mean that the same percentage structure is lawful elsewhere.

Comparative research must examine the rules of the particular jurisdiction.

The Relationship With Litigation Funding

Modern civil litigation has also produced additional financing models.

Third-party litigation funding can involve an outside financier providing money to support litigation in exchange for a financial return if the case succeeds.

This differs from a traditional contingency fee because the financier is not necessarily the claimant's lawyer.

The arrangement can raise separate questions involving disclosure, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, control over litigation, and applicable regulations.

The legal treatment varies by jurisdiction.

The Continuing Economic Role of Contingency Fees

Contingency arrangements remain an important part of the economics of civil litigation because they connect legal representation with the potential value of a claim.

For clients, the arrangement can reduce the need for substantial upfront attorney payments.

For lawyers, it creates financial exposure because significant work may produce no fee.

For the civil-justice system, the model can provide a mechanism through which people without substantial litigation resources can pursue monetary claims.

At the same time, the arrangement requires safeguards because attorney compensation is tied to the outcome.

Final Takeaway

Contingency fees have a long history in American civil litigation. Nineteenth-century U.S. Supreme Court decisions recognized arrangements in which lawyers would receive a percentage of a successful recovery, and the ABA formally addressed contingency compensation in its professional ethics rules in 1908.

The model developed partly as a way of allocating litigation risk. Instead of requiring clients to pay lawyers entirely according to time worked, contingency arrangements allow attorneys to receive compensation from a successful recovery while accepting the possibility that substantial work may produce no attorney fee.

The model later developed alongside modern hourly billing, which became widespread in the United States by the early 1970s.

Today, contingency fees are regulated through professional-conduct rules and, in some jurisdictions, additional statutes. ABA Model Rule 1.5 requires written, signed contingency agreements with specific information about percentages, expenses, and the calculation of the final fee.

The economic appeal of contingency representation remains closely connected to access to civil litigation, but the arrangement also creates financial and ethical considerations for both lawyer and client. Because fee restrictions and requirements vary by jurisdiction and type of case, anyone considering a contingency arrangement should review the written agreement carefully and consider advice from an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction.
