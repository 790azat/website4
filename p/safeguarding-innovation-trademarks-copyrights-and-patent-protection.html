Safeguarding Innovation: Trademarks, Copyrights, and Patent Protection

Innovation can become one of a company's most valuable assets. New technologies, product designs, software, written materials, visual content, brand names, logos, and other creative developments can distinguish a business from competitors and generate significant commercial value.

Intellectual property law provides several mechanisms for protecting these assets. Trademarks generally protect signs that distinguish goods or services, copyrights protect original creative expression, and patents protect qualifying inventions. These forms of protection serve different purposes and should not be treated as interchangeable. The U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) specifically distinguishes trademarks, patents, and copyrights according to the type of intellectual property they protect.

Because intellectual property rights are largely territorial, businesses operating internationally must also consider the laws and registration systems of the countries where protection is sought.

Understanding the Main Forms of Intellectual Property

The first step in developing an intellectual property strategy is identifying what the business actually owns or creates.

A trademark can protect a name, phrase, symbol, design, or other sign used to distinguish goods or services. A copyright generally protects original works of authorship, such as literary works, music, photographs, films, artwork, and software. A patent can protect qualifying inventions, including certain new and useful processes, machines, manufactured articles, or compositions of matter.

A single commercial product may involve several forms of intellectual property simultaneously. For example, a technology company could hold a patent covering an invention, a trademark covering its product name, and copyright protection for software, advertising materials, and website content.

Understanding these distinctions helps businesses avoid relying on the wrong type of protection.

Protecting Brand Identity Through Trademarks

A company's name and visual identity can become valuable commercial assets as customers associate them with particular products or services.

Trademark protection focuses on distinguishing one business's goods or services from those of others. WIPO explains that a trademark is a sign capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one enterprise from those of another. Trademark protection is generally obtained through registration at the national or regional level, although some jurisdictions also recognize rights arising from use.

Businesses should consider conducting trademark searches before investing heavily in a new brand. Searching existing marks can identify potentially conflicting names or designs and may reduce the risk of adopting a brand that creates legal disputes.

The relevant search should consider the goods and services associated with the proposed mark, not merely identical names. Similar marks can create problems when they are used for related goods or services, depending on the applicable legal standard.

Trademark Registration and Maintenance

Registration can provide important legal advantages, but the precise scope of those advantages varies by jurisdiction.

WIPO notes that trademark registration generally provides an exclusive right to use the registered mark and can strengthen the owner's legal position in disputes. Trademark owners must also comply with applicable maintenance and renewal requirements.

A corporate trademark program can therefore include:

  • Searching proposed names before adoption
  • Identifying relevant goods and services
  • Registering important marks
  • Monitoring potentially conflicting uses
  • Maintaining registrations
  • Recording ownership changes
  • Controlling how the mark is used by licensees and partners

Companies operating internationally may need protection in multiple jurisdictions. WIPO's Madrid System provides an international filing mechanism through which eligible applicants can seek trademark protection in multiple participating jurisdictions.

Copyright Protection for Creative Works

Copyright can protect a broad range of original creative expression. Examples include written articles, photographs, illustrations, videos, music, advertisements, software, and other qualifying works.

An important distinction is that copyright protects expression rather than the underlying idea itself. The USPTO explains that copyright does not protect ideas, procedures, processes, systems, methods of operation, concepts, principles, or discoveries.

For businesses, copyright management can therefore cover substantially more than traditional artistic works. Software code, marketing materials, product documentation, website content, graphics, training materials, and audiovisual content may all require appropriate ownership and licensing arrangements.

Companies should maintain records identifying who created important works and under what contractual terms.

Ownership of Employee and Contractor Creations

Businesses frequently rely on employees, freelancers, agencies, developers, designers, consultants, and other contractors to create intellectual property.

The existence of a business relationship does not necessarily answer every question about ownership. Employment agreements, contractor agreements, copyright statutes, patent laws, and local rules can determine who owns particular rights.

For this reason, companies can benefit from addressing intellectual property ownership directly in written agreements. Relevant provisions may address:

  • Assignment of intellectual property rights
  • Confidentiality
  • Work product
  • Pre-existing intellectual property
  • Moral rights where applicable
  • Licensing
  • Inventions and discoveries
  • Cooperation with registration procedures

The exact language and legal effect of these provisions vary by jurisdiction, making local legal review important for cross-border operations.

Patent Protection for Technical Innovation

Patents are designed to protect qualifying inventions rather than general business concepts.

The USPTO explains that a U.S. patent gives its owner the right to exclude others from making, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing the patented invention in the United States for the applicable patent term. Patent rights are territorial and do not automatically provide worldwide protection.

Patent eligibility and requirements differ according to the jurisdiction and type of patent. In the United States, for example, utility, design, and plant patents cover different categories of subject matter.

Before filing, businesses commonly conduct searches for existing patents and publicly available technology. The USPTO notes that an invention that is already publicly available generally cannot receive a patent.

Preserving Confidentiality Before Patent Filing

Confidentiality can be particularly important during the early stages of an invention.

Public disclosure can affect patent rights under the laws of some jurisdictions. Because international patent systems can impose different requirements and deadlines, inventors should consider patent strategy before publicly releasing technical information.

Businesses can use confidentiality agreements, access restrictions, internal security controls, and controlled disclosure procedures while evaluating whether an invention should be patented.

The decision may involve more than whether an invention appears technically innovative. Companies can also consider commercial value, development costs, competitive advantages, enforcement considerations, and whether maintaining information as a trade secret is more appropriate.

Creating an Intellectual Property Inventory

An IP inventory can help businesses understand what they own, what they are developing, and where protection may be incomplete.

An inventory can categorize assets such as:

Brand assets: Company names, product names, logos, slogans, and other marks.

Creative assets: Articles, photographs, videos, software, graphics, manuals, and marketing materials.

Technical assets: Inventions, processes, designs, prototypes, and technical improvements.

Confidential assets: Proprietary business information, formulas, methods, customer information, and other confidential know-how.

For each asset, the company can record ownership, creation date, registration status, relevant jurisdictions, contractual restrictions, renewal deadlines, and licensing arrangements.

Managing Intellectual Property Licenses

Companies frequently use intellectual property belonging to other organizations. Software licenses, photographs, fonts, technology platforms, patents, trademarks, and copyrighted content can all involve licensing arrangements.

A licensing agreement can define who may use the intellectual property, for what purposes, in which territories, and for how long. It may also address royalties, sublicensing, quality controls, confidentiality, termination, and enforcement.

Businesses should maintain records of licenses and monitor their expiration dates and usage restrictions. Using intellectual property beyond the scope of a license can create contractual and intellectual property risks.

Monitoring and Enforcing Intellectual Property Rights

Obtaining intellectual property rights is only one part of protection. Companies may also need systems for identifying unauthorized use.

Monitoring can include:

  • Trademark searches
  • Online marketplace monitoring
  • Website and domain-name monitoring
  • Software and content monitoring
  • Patent landscape reviews
  • Review of competitor products
  • Internal reporting mechanisms

When suspected infringement is identified, the appropriate response depends on the facts and applicable law. Possible responses can include direct communication, licensing discussions, platform complaints, administrative procedures, negotiation, arbitration, or litigation.

A company should establish an evidence-preservation process before taking significant enforcement action. Relevant evidence may include screenshots, product samples, URLs, advertising materials, registration records, transaction information, and records showing ownership.

Considering International Protection

Intellectual property rights generally operate within territorial legal systems. A company with international operations therefore needs to determine where protection is commercially important.

WIPO provides international systems and services that can assist applicants seeking protection across multiple jurisdictions. Its resources include international systems for trademarks and patent-related filings, as well as databases that can help businesses research existing intellectual property.

International protection can nevertheless involve significant costs and procedural requirements. Companies may therefore prioritize jurisdictions based on manufacturing locations, major markets, competitors, licensing opportunities, and anticipated commercial activity.

Integrating Intellectual Property Into Corporate Strategy

Intellectual property protection is most effective when incorporated into broader business planning.

Before launching a new product or service, a company can consider whether its name is available for trademark protection, whether its technology warrants patent analysis, whether its content requires copyright ownership documentation, and whether certain information should remain confidential.

During acquisitions and investments, intellectual property can also form part of due diligence. Companies may need to verify ownership, registrations, licenses, infringement disputes, renewal obligations, and contractual restrictions.

These processes can help identify intellectual property risks before they become significant commercial problems.

Conclusion

Trademarks, copyrights, and patents protect different categories of intellectual property and should be incorporated into a coordinated business strategy. Trademarks can help protect brand identity, copyrights can protect original creative expression, and patents can protect qualifying technical inventions.

Businesses can strengthen their intellectual property management by maintaining accurate ownership records, conducting searches before adopting new assets, using appropriate contracts, preserving confidentiality, monitoring unauthorized use, and evaluating protection in the jurisdictions that matter commercially.

Because intellectual property laws and registration requirements vary between countries, businesses developing significant intellectual property portfolios should obtain advice concerning the specific jurisdictions, assets, and commercial arrangements involved.
