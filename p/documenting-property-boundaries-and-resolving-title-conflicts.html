Documenting Property Boundaries and Resolving Title Conflicts

Property boundaries provide the physical framework for determining what land is included within an owner's rights, but the legal boundaries of real property are not always obvious from fences, driveways, buildings, or other visible features. Deeds, recorded plats, surveys, easements, historical conveyances, and other instruments may describe the same parcel in different ways.

Boundary questions can become particularly complicated in commercial real estate transactions, development projects, refinancing, and disputes between neighboring owners. A discrepancy between a deed and an existing survey may affect financing, construction plans, access rights, or the marketability of title. A structured process for documenting the property and identifying competing interests can help reveal these issues before they become more costly.

Establishing the Legal Description

The first step in documenting property boundaries is identifying the property's legal description. Depending on the jurisdiction and property, a deed may describe land by lot and block, metes and bounds, a recorded subdivision plat, or another legally recognized method.

A title examination generally involves reviewing the vesting deed and tracing prior conveyances. The American Bar Association notes that a legal description can be compared against tax maps, GIS information, recorded plats, surveys, and other available drawings, although mapping resources should not automatically be treated as substitutes for the controlling legal instruments.

Metes-and-bounds descriptions can require particular care. Distances, bearings, monuments, adjoining parcels, and calls to natural or artificial features may all be relevant to understanding the intended boundaries.

Why a Professional Survey Matters

A survey provides a visual representation of the parcel and can help identify discrepancies between the legal description and conditions observed on the ground. Depending on its scope, a survey may show boundary lines, improvements, easements, encroachments, access features, and other matters.

The ABA explains that surveys can help confirm boundaries and identify issues such as encroachments, easements, access concerns, and inconsistencies involving legal descriptions.

Commercial transactions may require a more comprehensive survey than a basic residential transaction. The ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey standards establish minimum requirements for surveys used in many commercial real estate transactions and were updated in 2026, with the revised standards becoming effective February 23, 2026.

A survey should therefore be ordered with the intended transaction and due-diligence objectives in mind rather than simply treated as a standard closing formality.

Comparing Surveys, Deeds, and Recorded Plats

Boundary conflicts often become apparent when several sources of information are compared.

A useful review may include:

  • The current deed;
  • Prior deeds in the chain of title;
  • Recorded subdivision plats;
  • Existing surveys;
  • Title commitments or title reports;
  • Easements and rights-of-way;
  • Tax maps and GIS information;
  • Building and site plans;
  • Recorded boundary agreements; and
  • Relevant governmental records.

The purpose is not simply to identify which document appears most convenient. Different documents may serve different legal functions, and the governing law determines how conflicts between descriptions, monuments, surveys, and other evidence are resolved.

For this reason, a surveyor and real estate attorney may need to work together when the records do not align.

Common Boundary Problems

Several recurring issues can produce uncertainty about property boundaries.

Overlapping Legal Descriptions

Two deeds may contain descriptions that appear to overlap. This can result from drafting errors, historical surveying practices, inaccurate references to neighboring parcels, or changes in subdivision configurations.

An overlap does not necessarily mean that one owner automatically owns the disputed area. Determining the controlling boundary may require examination of earlier conveyances and applicable property law.

Encroachments

An encroachment occurs when an improvement or other physical feature extends across a property boundary. Examples can include fences, walls, buildings, driveways, retaining structures, utility installations, or landscaping.

The commercial consequences can be significant if an encroachment interferes with development plans or creates an access or maintenance dispute.

Easements

An easement gives one party certain rights concerning land owned by another. Utility corridors, shared driveways, drainage systems, access routes, and other arrangements may be established through recorded instruments or, depending on applicable law, through other legal mechanisms.

A survey can help show the physical location of an easement, but determining the precise scope of the legal right generally requires reviewing the instrument that created it and the applicable law.

When a Fence Is Not the Legal Boundary

Physical markers can provide useful evidence, but a fence or other enclosure does not necessarily establish the legally controlling property line.

Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute notes that an enclosure can be relevant to adverse-possession claims because it may demonstrate the area being occupied, but the legal requirements for adverse possession vary by jurisdiction.

Consequently, owners should avoid assuming that a longstanding fence automatically changes the legal boundary. Depending on the jurisdiction, doctrines involving adverse possession, boundary by acquiescence, prescription, estoppel, or boundary agreements may become relevant.

Documenting a Potential Title Conflict

When a discrepancy is discovered, documentation should begin before physical changes are made to the property.

A useful record can include:

  1. Copies of relevant deeds and recorded instruments;
  2. The current and historical surveys;
  3. Photographs of fences, structures, driveways, and markers;
  4. A written chronology of ownership and improvements;
  5. Correspondence concerning the disputed area;
  6. Contractor or construction records where improvements are involved;
  7. Municipal or county records;
  8. Title insurance documentation; and
  9. Statements or agreements previously exchanged between neighboring owners.

Preserving the historical record can be important because later alterations to fences or structures may make it more difficult to establish what existed when the dispute began.

Negotiating a Boundary Resolution

Not every boundary disagreement requires litigation. Depending on the circumstances, neighboring owners may be able to resolve the issue through a written boundary agreement, corrective conveyance, easement, lot-line adjustment, or other legally recognized instrument.

A negotiated resolution should be documented carefully and, where required, recorded with the appropriate land-recording authority. The document should clearly identify the affected parcels and explain the rights being established, released, transferred, or confirmed.

The parties should also determine whether lenders, title insurers, governmental authorities, or other interested parties must consent.

Title Insurance and Boundary Conflicts

Title insurance may provide protection against certain covered title risks, but coverage depends on the policy's terms, exclusions, exceptions, endorsements, and applicable law.

Title companies commonly use surveys to evaluate boundary lines, encroachments, easements, access, and discrepancies between recorded legal descriptions and physical conditions. The ABA notes that survey-related issues can delay closings and potentially affect property value or future use.

An owner should therefore review the actual title policy and exceptions rather than assuming that every boundary problem is insured. ALTA's title and escrow materials emphasize that title insurance disputes can involve issues such as easements, chain of title, priority of interests, constructive notice, and other aspects of property law.

Litigation and Quiet Title Proceedings

When negotiations fail, litigation may become necessary. Depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, possible proceedings can include quiet title actions, declaratory proceedings, boundary actions, adverse-possession claims, or other real property litigation.

A quiet title proceeding generally seeks judicial determination of competing claims or interests in property. The precise procedure, required parties, evidentiary standards, and available remedies differ substantially among jurisdictions.

Real estate litigation can also involve related claims concerning easements, access, possession, damages, or improvements.

Because property disputes can affect both present ownership and future transactions, parties should evaluate the potential consequences before selecting litigation as a resolution strategy.

A Practical Boundary-Due-Diligence Checklist

For a commercial property or significant real estate transaction, the following sequence can provide a useful framework:

  • Obtain the current deed and legal description.
  • Review prior conveyances and the chain of title.
  • Obtain a current professional survey appropriate for the transaction.
  • Compare the survey with recorded plats and legal descriptions.
  • Identify easements, rights-of-way, restrictions, and encroachments.
  • Investigate apparent overlaps or gaps in descriptions.
  • Review title commitments and exceptions.
  • Document physical conditions with photographs and supporting records.
  • Determine whether neighboring owners assert competing claims.
  • Consult applicable state or local law concerning boundary doctrines.
  • Consider negotiated documentation before a dispute escalates.
  • Record corrective instruments when legally appropriate.
  • Confirm whether lenders or title insurers require additional documentation.

Conclusion

Documenting property boundaries requires coordination between physical evidence and legal records. A current survey can reveal conditions that are difficult to identify from deeds alone, while title research can uncover historical interests that are not apparent from the property's present appearance.

When a conflict emerges, the appropriate response depends on the nature of the discrepancy. Some matters can be resolved through clarification, surveying, or recorded agreements; others may require title-curative work, negotiation, or litigation.

Because property and recording laws vary by jurisdiction, boundary questions should be evaluated using the law applicable to the property rather than relying solely on general real estate practices. Careful documentation and early professional review can help reduce uncertainty and protect the property's future marketability and use.
