Evaluating Professional Services: What to Look for in Legal Consultants

Choosing a legal professional can be difficult when a person or business is facing an unfamiliar legal issue. Terms such as legal consultant, legal advisor, lawyer, and attorney can describe different types of professional services, and the services that a particular person may legally provide depend on their qualifications, jurisdiction, and role.

For matters requiring legal representation or the practice of law, licensing and professional-regulation requirements are especially important. The American Bar Association's Model Rules state that competent representation requires the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation reasonably necessary for the matter. Individual states adopt their own professional-conduct rules, which may differ from the ABA Model Rules.

A careful evaluation therefore involves more than comparing prices or reading online reviews. Relevant experience, professional status, scope of services, communication practices, conflicts of interest, fees, and the terms of the engagement can all matter.

What Is a Legal Consultant?

"Legal consultant" is not a single, universally defined professional category in the United States.

A person using that title might provide services such as:

  • Legal research;
  • Contract analysis;
  • Regulatory research;
  • Compliance support;
  • Litigation support;
  • Document review;
  • Legal operations assistance; or
  • Advice within a particular professional role.

A licensed attorney may also describe certain advisory work as consulting.

The important question is therefore not simply what title appears on a website or business card. It is what services the professional is offering and whether the person is authorized and qualified to provide those services in the relevant jurisdiction.

If a matter requires representation before a court, legal advice concerning a particular person's rights, or another activity constituting the practice of law, the professional's licensing status should be verified.

Legal Consultant vs. Attorney

An attorney is a lawyer who is licensed to practice law in at least one jurisdiction.

The FTC recommends checking the relevant state bar association when selecting a lawyer. State bar records can generally provide information about whether an attorney is active and, depending on the jurisdiction, whether disciplinary action has occurred.

A consultant who is not licensed as an attorney may have valuable knowledge in a particular field, but that does not necessarily authorize the person to perform every type of legal service.

This distinction becomes particularly important when a matter involves:

  • Court representation;
  • Legal opinions;
  • Negotiating legal rights on another person's behalf;
  • Litigation;
  • Formal legal filings; or
  • Activities regulated as the practice of law.

Before engaging someone, it is useful to establish exactly what role that person will perform.

Verify Professional Credentials

Credentials should be one of the first things to verify.

For an attorney, useful checks can include:

  • State bar admission;
  • Current licensing status;
  • Jurisdictions in which the lawyer is admitted;
  • Disciplinary history where publicly available;
  • Relevant professional certifications;
  • Court experience; and
  • Practice-area experience.

The FTC specifically advises consumers to check state bar records and look beyond awards or promotional badges when evaluating lawyers.

An award, website ranking, or professional designation does not by itself establish that a particular professional is suitable for a particular matter.

Look for Relevant Experience

Legal experience should be evaluated in relation to the actual problem.

For example, a person dealing with a commercial contract may want to know whether the professional regularly handles:

  • Commercial agreements;
  • Contract disputes;
  • Business transactions;
  • Regulatory requirements; or
  • The particular industry involved.

Likewise, an accident claim may require experience with personal injury law, insurance coverage, evidence, medical documentation, and litigation.

The ABA Model Rule on competence recognizes that the complexity and specialized nature of a matter can affect the knowledge and skill required. It also identifies preparation, training, experience, and the ability to associate or consult with another competent lawyer as relevant considerations.

Relevant experience is therefore generally more informative than simply counting years in practice.

Evaluate the Scope of Services

Before hiring a legal professional, determine exactly what the engagement includes.

Questions can include:

  • What work will be performed?
  • What work is excluded?
  • Who will perform the work?
  • Will another attorney or outside professional be involved?
  • Will the professional communicate with opposing parties?
  • Will the professional appear in court?
  • Who is responsible for filing documents?
  • Who will provide updates?
  • What happens if the matter becomes more complicated?

A narrow consulting engagement may cover document review but not litigation representation.

Similarly, an attorney may agree to handle only a particular phase of a dispute.

The scope should be clear before substantial work begins.

Ask Who Will Actually Handle the Work

The person conducting the initial consultation is not necessarily the person who will perform most of the work.

A law firm may assign matters to:

  • Partners;
  • Associates;
  • Contract attorneys;
  • Paralegals;
  • Legal assistants;
  • Investigators; or
  • Outside specialists.

The FTC specifically recommends asking who will perform most of the work and how each person's fees will be calculated.

This can be particularly important for complex matters where the client's relationship with the day-to-day professional may differ from the relationship established during the initial consultation.

Consider Communication Practices

Communication can be an important part of a professional relationship.

Before engaging a legal professional, it can be useful to establish:

  • The primary contact person;
  • Preferred communication method;
  • Expected response times;
  • Frequency of status updates;
  • Emergency-contact procedures;
  • How documents will be exchanged; and
  • How important decisions will be communicated.

The FTC recommends discussing how and how often a lawyer will provide updates and how the client will exchange information and documents.

A clear communication structure can reduce misunderstandings as the matter develops.

Examine Conflicts of Interest

A legal professional may have obligations concerning conflicts of interest.

Potential conflicts can arise when the professional:

  • Represents another party in the same dispute;
  • Previously represented an opposing party;
  • Has a financial interest that conflicts with the client's interests;
  • Represents multiple parties whose interests may diverge; or
  • Has another professional relationship that creates a conflict.

The ABA Model Rules address conflicts of interest as part of the professional responsibilities governing lawyers.

A prospective client can ask whether the professional has identified any conflict that would prevent or limit the representation.

For significant matters, conflict screening should occur before confidential information is shared unnecessarily.

Understand Confidentiality

People often assume that every conversation with anyone calling themselves a legal consultant is automatically protected by attorney-client privilege.

That assumption can be incorrect.

Attorney-client privilege is a legal doctrine associated with confidential communications made for the purpose of obtaining legal advice from an attorney acting in a professional capacity, subject to applicable law and exceptions.

A non-attorney consultant may not provide the same legal protections.

Before disclosing highly sensitive information, it is therefore important to understand the professional's role and the confidentiality obligations that apply.

Review the Fee Structure

Legal services can be billed in several ways.

Common arrangements include:

  • Hourly billing;
  • Flat fees;
  • Contingency fees;
  • Retainers;
  • Hybrid arrangements; and
  • Project-based consulting fees.

The appropriate structure depends on the type of matter.

The ABA Model Rule 1.5 provides that lawyers should not charge unreasonable fees or expenses and identifies factors relevant to evaluating reasonableness, including the time and labor required, complexity, customary local fees, experience, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent.

State professional-conduct rules may differ from the ABA Model Rules.

Get the Fee Agreement in Writing

A written engagement agreement can reduce misunderstandings.

The agreement can specify:

  • Scope of representation;
  • Hourly rates;
  • Flat fees;
  • Contingency percentages, if applicable;
  • Retainer requirements;
  • Filing fees;
  • Expert costs;
  • Travel expenses;
  • Other reimbursable expenses;
  • Billing intervals;
  • Responsibility for third-party costs; and
  • Procedures for ending the engagement.

The ABA Model Rules provide that the scope of representation and basis or rate of the fee and expenses should generally be communicated to the client in writing. Contingency arrangements have additional written-agreement requirements under the Model Rules.

The FTC likewise recommends getting the fee agreement in writing and reviewing bills so that unclear charges can be addressed promptly.

Be Careful With Contingency Fees

A contingency arrangement generally means the attorney receives a specified portion of a recovery rather than charging the client solely by the hour.

But the percentage is only one part of the agreement.

A client should also understand:

  • Whether litigation expenses are deducted separately;
  • Whether expenses are deducted before or after calculating the attorney's percentage;
  • What happens if the case is unsuccessful;
  • Whether the client remains responsible for certain expenses;
  • Whether the percentage changes if the matter goes to trial; and
  • What happens if the representation ends before resolution.

ABA Model Rule 1.5 provides specific disclosure requirements for contingency agreements, including the method used to calculate the fee and treatment of litigation expenses.

State law may impose additional restrictions.

Evaluate Online Reviews Carefully

Online reviews can provide useful background, but they should not be treated as a substitute for professional verification.

The FTC warns that online reviews can be fabricated or written by people with personal relationships to the business or professional. It also cautions consumers to look beyond seals, badges, and purchased awards when evaluating lawyers.

More useful information may include:

  • Verifiable professional credentials;
  • Relevant case experience;
  • Published legal work;
  • Court records where available;
  • Bar disciplinary records;
  • Professional references; and
  • Clear information about services and fees.

No single source provides a complete picture.

Ask About the Likely Process

A competent professional should generally be able to explain the expected process without guaranteeing a particular outcome.

Depending on the matter, the process could include:

  1. Initial consultation;
  2. Conflict check;
  3. Document collection;
  4. Legal research;
  5. Negotiation;
  6. Administrative proceedings;
  7. Mediation;
  8. Filing a lawsuit;
  9. Discovery;
  10. Settlement negotiations;
  11. Trial; or
  12. Appeal.

The actual sequence can change as new facts emerge.

A useful consultation should therefore focus on the possible paths and uncertainties rather than promises about a specific result.

Be Alert to Guaranteed Results

Legal disputes often involve uncertainty.

A professional should be able to discuss:

  • Strengths of the position;
  • Potential weaknesses;
  • Important factual questions;
  • Applicable deadlines;
  • Possible costs;
  • Procedural risks; and
  • Alternative approaches.

Promises of guaranteed outcomes deserve particular caution.

A lawyer can evaluate legal arguments and available evidence, but the ultimate outcome of litigation can depend on judges, juries, opposing parties, changing facts, and applicable law.

Ask About Use of Outside Professionals

Some matters require expertise beyond traditional legal analysis.

A lawyer may work with:

  • Accident reconstructionists;
  • Accountants;
  • Medical experts;
  • Engineers;
  • Investigators;
  • Appraisers;
  • Technology specialists; or
  • Other consultants.

The ABA's competence guidance recognizes that lawyers may associate or consult with other professionals when necessary to provide competent representation. It also addresses responsibilities when lawyers retain outside lawyers or nonlawyer services.

If outside professionals will be used, the client should understand their role and how their fees will be handled.

Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Legal Professional

A prospective client can prepare a short list of questions before the first meeting:

Experience

  • How much experience do you have with this type of matter?
  • Do you regularly handle cases involving these legal issues?
  • Are you licensed in the jurisdiction involved?

Scope

  • What exactly will you handle?
  • What will I be responsible for?
  • Who will perform most of the work?

Fees

  • What is the billing structure?
  • What expenses are separate from legal fees?
  • Is an advance retainer required?
  • Can the fee arrangement change if the matter becomes more complicated?

Communication

  • Who will be my primary contact?
  • How frequently will I receive updates?
  • How should urgent matters be communicated?

Strategy

  • What legal issues appear most important?
  • What information do you still need?
  • What procedural steps could follow?
  • What uncertainties should I understand?

These questions can help turn an initial consultation into a practical assessment of the proposed professional relationship.

Comparing Several Professionals

For significant matters, speaking with more than one professional can provide useful context.

The FTC recommends consulting more than one lawyer before making a hiring decision.

When comparing professionals, consider using the same questions for each one.

For example:

Factor Questions to Consider
Credentials Is the professional properly licensed or otherwise qualified for the proposed work?
Experience Does the professional regularly handle similar matters?
Scope Is it clear what services are included?
Communication Are communication expectations defined?
Fees Are rates and expenses clearly explained?
Conflicts Has the professional completed an appropriate conflict check?
Resources Can the professional handle the complexity of the matter?
Documentation Will important terms be provided in writing?

This approach allows a prospective client to compare concrete information rather than relying primarily on advertising or impressions.

When a Legal Consultant May Not Be Enough

Some matters involve issues that generally call for licensed legal representation.

Examples can include:

  • Active litigation;
  • Criminal proceedings;
  • Complex personal injury claims;
  • Immigration proceedings;
  • Family-law disputes;
  • Business litigation;
  • Regulatory investigations; or
  • Transactions involving significant legal exposure.

The precise requirements depend on the jurisdiction and matter.

If court representation or legal advice is required, verifying that the professional is authorized to provide those services is particularly important.

Final Takeaway

Evaluating a legal professional involves more than comparing hourly rates or looking for the most impressive website. Relevant qualifications, jurisdictional authorization, experience with similar matters, scope of services, conflicts, communication practices, and fee arrangements can all affect the professional relationship.

The ABA Model Rules identify competence, communication, confidentiality, conflicts, and reasonable fees as important aspects of professional responsibility for lawyers, although the rules actually governing an attorney depend on the jurisdictions in which the attorney practices.

The FTC recommends checking state bar information, considering more than one lawyer, asking about experience and fees, and putting the engagement terms in writing.

For a significant legal matter, the most useful evaluation is usually a structured one: verify the professional, define the work, understand the costs, establish communication expectations, identify potential conflicts, and make sure the engagement terms are documented clearly.
