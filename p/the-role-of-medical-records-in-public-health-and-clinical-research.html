The Role of Medical Records in Public Health and Clinical Research

Medical records are created primarily to support individual patient care, but the information they contain can also contribute to public health surveillance and clinical research. Electronic health records (EHRs), laboratory systems, hospital records, and other clinical data sources can provide information about diseases, treatments, outcomes, and patterns of healthcare use across large populations.

The growing availability of electronic records has made this secondary use of clinical information more practical. CDC research has found that EHR data can supplement traditional surveillance by providing relatively timely, longitudinal information across large patient populations. At the same time, researchers must account for missing information, differences between healthcare systems, and the fact that EHR populations may not represent the broader population.

1. Medical Records Serve Primarily as Clinical Records

The fundamental purpose of a medical record is to document a patient's care.

Depending on the encounter, a record can contain:

  • Symptoms and medical history
  • Physical examination findings
  • Diagnoses
  • Medications
  • Laboratory results
  • Imaging
  • Procedures
  • Treatment plans
  • Clinical communications
  • Follow-up information
  • Patient outcomes

Because these records are generated during ordinary healthcare delivery, they can contain information that would otherwise require separate surveys or research data collection.

This creates an opportunity to use existing clinical information for broader health analysis while maintaining the distinction between the original clinical purpose and any later secondary use.

2. Medical Records and Public Health Surveillance

Public health surveillance involves collecting and analyzing information to identify and monitor health problems and support public health action.

CDC describes electronic health records as an increasingly important source of surveillance information. Clinical information such as symptoms, diagnoses, laboratory results, and other data can help public health authorities identify disease patterns and monitor changes over time.

Unlike a traditional survey conducted periodically, EHR information is generated continuously as people receive healthcare.

This can allow surveillance systems to identify trends relatively quickly when appropriate data infrastructure exists.

3. Electronic Case Reporting

One important connection between clinical records and public health is electronic case reporting (eCR).

CDC defines eCR as the automated exchange of case-report information between electronic health records and public health agencies. When information entered into an EHR indicates a potentially reportable condition, an electronic case report can be generated and sent to the appropriate public health authority when applicable reporting criteria are met.

The process can provide public health agencies with information concerning:

  • Reportable diseases
  • Patient demographics
  • Relevant diagnoses
  • Laboratory information
  • Medications
  • Immunizations
  • Other clinical information

CDC states that eCR can provide faster and more complete information than traditional manual reporting and can support disease tracking, case management, and outbreak response.

4. Syndromic Surveillance

Medical records can also contribute to syndromic surveillance, which focuses on patterns of symptoms, diagnoses, and other indicators that may signal an emerging health problem.

Emergency departments can be particularly valuable because they encounter patients with acute illnesses and injuries.

CDC's National Syndromic Surveillance Program uses near-real-time electronic health information to help detect, characterize, and monitor events of public health concern. As of September 2026, CDC reports that emergency departments in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and Guam voluntarily participate in the program.

Syndromic surveillance can be useful when public health officials need information before complete laboratory confirmation or traditional reporting data become available.

5. Monitoring Chronic Diseases

EHR data are not limited to infectious diseases.

They can also support surveillance of chronic conditions such as:

  • Hypertension
  • Diabetes
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Liver disease
  • Asthma
  • Certain cancers
  • Other long-term conditions

Repeated measurements can provide information about disease prevalence, treatment, and control.

For example, CDC research has examined the use of EHR blood-pressure measurements to identify hypertension and assess patterns of hypertension control.

Longitudinal clinical data can therefore provide information about how a condition changes over time rather than capturing only a single point in a patient's history.

6. Medical Records in Clinical Research

Clinical research uses medical records in several ways.

Researchers may use existing records to:

  • Identify potential study populations
  • Study disease patterns
  • Examine treatment outcomes
  • Compare healthcare interventions
  • Study adverse events
  • Evaluate healthcare utilization
  • Conduct observational studies
  • Develop research hypotheses

EHR-based research can reduce the need to collect every piece of information separately because much of the underlying data already exists.

Research networks such as PCORnet demonstrate how clinical information from multiple healthcare systems can be standardized and analyzed for patient-centered and comparative-effectiveness research. CDC describes PCORnet as a network that harmonizes data from participating healthcare systems into a common data model.

7. Retrospective and Prospective Uses

Medical-record research can generally be divided into different study approaches.

Retrospective research

Researchers examine information that already exists.

For example, investigators might analyze several years of records to determine whether patients receiving a particular treatment experienced different outcomes.

Prospective research

Researchers establish a study framework and collect or follow information going forward.

Existing medical records can still be incorporated into prospective studies when appropriate.

The distinction matters because different research designs can involve different consent, privacy, institutional review, and data-management considerations.

8. Medical Records Can Help Identify Health Trends

One advantage of large clinical datasets is their ability to reveal patterns that may be difficult to detect through individual patient encounters.

Researchers can examine:

  • Disease incidence
  • Disease prevalence
  • Treatment patterns
  • Hospitalization rates
  • Medication use
  • Complications
  • Healthcare utilization
  • Geographic patterns
  • Differences among population groups

CDC's experience with PCORnet illustrates how longitudinal EHR data can support population-level analysis across diverse health conditions.

These analyses can help researchers formulate hypotheses and provide information for public health planning.

9. Medical Records Can Support Outbreak Detection

During an infectious-disease outbreak, speed can be particularly important.

Electronic reporting systems can allow clinical information to move from healthcare organizations to public health agencies without requiring every report to be manually prepared.

CDC explains that eCR can provide real-time or near-real-time exchange of reportable-condition information and can support faster public health responses.

Medical records may therefore contribute to:

  1. Detecting unusual patterns.
  2. Identifying potentially reportable conditions.
  3. Monitoring disease trends.
  4. Supporting case investigations.
  5. Evaluating the spread of an outbreak.
  6. Providing information for public health interventions.

The information remains subject to applicable privacy and reporting requirements.

10. Data Quality Is a Major Consideration

A medical record may be useful clinically without being perfectly suited to research.

EHR systems can contain:

  • Missing information
  • Inconsistent terminology
  • Duplicate records
  • Incorrect values
  • Data-entry errors
  • Differences between healthcare systems
  • Unstructured clinical notes
  • Information recorded differently by different providers

CDC research on EHR-based surveillance has identified problems involving missing data, duplicate or missing patient records, inconsistent data fields, and implausible values.

Researchers therefore need data-validation procedures before drawing conclusions from large clinical datasets.

11. EHR Data May Not Represent the Entire Population

One of the most important limitations of medical-record research is representativeness.

EHRs generally contain information about people who interact with the healthcare system represented by the dataset.

People who rarely seek medical care may be underrepresented.

CDC researchers have noted that EHR-based surveillance can be biased toward populations receiving more healthcare, including people with chronic conditions or particular patterns of insurance coverage or healthcare access.

Consequently, researchers should not automatically assume that findings from one healthcare system or network apply equally to every population.

12. Different Healthcare Systems Record Information Differently

Two hospitals may document the same clinical event differently.

Differences can arise from:

  • EHR software
  • Coding practices
  • Clinical workflows
  • Documentation policies
  • Laboratory systems
  • Data structures
  • Local terminology
  • Patient populations

Large research networks may therefore use standardized data models to make information from different institutions more comparable.

PCORnet, for example, maps clinical information from participating healthcare systems to a standardized common data model.

Standardization can make multi-institution research more practical, although it does not eliminate every data-quality or comparability problem.

13. Privacy and Confidentiality

Medical records contain highly sensitive information.

When clinical data are used outside direct patient care, organizations must consider applicable privacy laws, security controls, research requirements, and data-sharing agreements.

HIPAA can apply to protected health information held by covered entities and their business associates, while research involving human participants can also be subject to additional federal, state, institutional, or study-specific requirements.

Privacy protection can involve:

  • Access controls
  • Secure data storage
  • Authentication
  • Encryption
  • Data-use agreements
  • De-identification where appropriate
  • Limited access to identifiable information
  • Audit controls

Privacy is not simply a technical issue. It also involves governance, oversight, and appropriate decisions about who may use information and for what purpose.

14. De-Identification and Limited Data

Researchers do not always need direct identifiers to answer a research question.

Depending on the project, information may be de-identified or otherwise restricted before being made available to researchers.

However, removing obvious identifiers does not necessarily eliminate every privacy concern. Researchers must consider whether the remaining information could be combined with other information to identify individuals and must follow the rules applicable to the particular dataset and study.

The appropriate approach depends on the research purpose, data structure, legal requirements, and institutional policies.

15. Public Health Surveillance Is Different From Research

Although surveillance and research may use similar data, they are not identical activities.

CDC distinguishes the purposes of the two: public health surveillance is directed toward identifying and controlling health problems or improving public health programs, while research is intended to generate generalizable knowledge.

The distinction can affect:

  • Oversight
  • Reporting requirements
  • Institutional review
  • Consent considerations
  • Data governance

A particular project should therefore be evaluated according to its actual purpose and design rather than simply being labeled “research” or “surveillance.”

16. Medical Records Can Improve Clinical Research Efficiency

Using existing records can reduce some of the time and resources required to assemble study populations.

Researchers may be able to identify eligible patients based on:

  • Diagnoses
  • Laboratory values
  • Medications
  • Procedures
  • Demographic information
  • Healthcare encounters

This can make it possible to study large populations that would be difficult to recruit manually.

However, efficiency does not eliminate the need for careful study design. Researchers still need to determine whether the available information is sufficiently complete and accurate for the research question.

17. Linking Clinical Data With Other Sources

Medical records can sometimes be combined with other datasets.

Potential sources include:

  • Public health registries
  • Laboratory data
  • Vital statistics
  • Census information
  • Environmental data
  • Geographic information
  • Insurance or claims data

CDC researchers have noted that EHR information can be linked with community-level information to support more detailed population analyses.

Data linkage can provide a more comprehensive picture, but it also creates additional privacy, matching, governance, and data-quality considerations.

18. Clinical Documentation Affects Downstream Data Quality

The quality of information used for public health and research purposes begins partly at the point of clinical documentation.

When clinicians accurately record:

  • Symptoms
  • Diagnoses
  • Test results
  • Medications
  • Demographic information
  • Relevant clinical observations

those data may later become useful for purposes beyond the original encounter.

CDC's current Project EMRge specifically seeks to improve collaboration between clinicians and public health professionals and to strengthen clinical documentation so that EHR information can better support public health surveillance.

This does not mean clinicians should document information primarily for researchers. Clinical accuracy and appropriate patient care remain the central objectives.

19. Medical Records Can Support Quality Improvement

Clinical data can also be used to evaluate healthcare delivery.

Organizations may analyze records to examine:

  • Screening rates
  • Medication safety
  • Follow-up care
  • Hospital readmissions
  • Treatment patterns
  • Preventive-care measures
  • Clinical outcomes

Quality-improvement activities may have different purposes and oversight requirements from traditional research.

The distinction between quality improvement, surveillance, and research should be established according to the purpose and applicable rules.

20. The Future of Clinical Data Use

Medical records are becoming increasingly important components of health-information infrastructure.

CDC's electronic case-reporting program continues to expand, and as of August 2026, CDC reported that more than 65,360 healthcare facilities in all 50 states and three territories were actively sending electronic initial case reports through eCR.

At the same time, broader use of EHR data creates ongoing challenges involving interoperability, privacy, data quality, representativeness, standardization, and governance.

Future systems are likely to depend increasingly on standardized electronic data exchange and carefully governed secondary use of clinical information.

Final Takeaway

Medical records have a dual importance: they support individual clinical care while also providing a potential source of information for public health surveillance and clinical research.

EHR data can help public health agencies identify disease trends, support outbreak response, monitor chronic conditions, and improve surveillance. Researchers can use appropriately governed clinical datasets to study treatments, outcomes, disease patterns, and healthcare delivery.

However, medical records are not automatically research-ready datasets. Missing information, inconsistent documentation, differences among healthcare systems, and limitations in population representation can affect conclusions. CDC research has specifically emphasized the need for validation and careful assessment of EHR data quality before using it for surveillance or population-level analysis.

The most useful approach combines accurate clinical documentation, appropriate privacy protections, standardized data practices, sound research methodology, and clear distinctions between patient care, public health surveillance, quality improvement, and research.
