Background Screening Industries and the Impact of Public Records on Employment

Background screening has become an important component of modern employment practices. Employers may review information concerning an applicant's employment history, education, professional credentials, criminal records, financial history, or other publicly available information before making hiring and personnel decisions. Third-party background screening companies have developed into a substantial industry that collects, verifies, organizes, and reports information for employers and other organizations.

Public records can provide useful information for evaluating candidates, but their use also creates legal and practical concerns. Records can be incomplete, outdated, incorrectly matched to an individual, or subject to restrictions on their use. Employment screening therefore operates within a framework of consumer reporting requirements, anti-discrimination laws, privacy rules, and state and local regulations.

The Development of the Background Screening Industry

Employers have long investigated the qualifications and backgrounds of prospective employees. The expansion of computerized databases and digital public records, however, has made it possible for specialized companies to collect information from numerous sources and produce standardized reports.

Modern screening companies can search or compile information from sources such as:

  • Court records
  • Criminal-history databases
  • Employment records
  • Education records
  • Professional licensing databases
  • Credit information
  • Public government records
  • Address histories
  • Other legally obtainable information

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that employment background reports can contain criminal background information, public records such as bankruptcy filings and other court documents, employment history, and credit information. Companies providing these reports may qualify as consumer reporting agencies under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA).

Public Records as a Source of Employment Information

Public records are attractive to employers because they can provide independently maintained information about an individual's history. Court records, licensing records, property records, and other government documents may help employers verify information or identify matters relevant to particular positions.

Criminal court records are one of the most frequently discussed examples. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) notes that criminal-history information may come from courthouse records, federal court systems, and third-party background screening businesses.

However, the existence of a public record does not automatically mean that it provides a complete or accurate picture of an applicant. A database may fail to reflect a later dismissal, expungement, sealing order, reduction in the level of an offense, or other development.

Accuracy and Record Matching

One of the central challenges in the background screening industry is accurately associating records with the correct individual.

People can share names, birth dates, addresses, or other identifying characteristics. Clerical errors can also occur when information is transferred between court systems and private databases.

The EEOC has noted that criminal records can contain errors resulting from factors such as misspellings, incomplete identifying information, or inaccurate database information. It has also recognized that records can continue to appear in private databases after they have been sealed or expunged from official systems.

For this reason, employers should not necessarily treat a database match as conclusive evidence without reviewing the underlying information and considering whether it actually relates to the applicant.

The Fair Credit Reporting Act

The FCRA establishes federal requirements governing consumer reports used for employment purposes when the report is provided by a consumer reporting agency.

The CFPB states that third-party background dossiers and algorithmic scores used for hiring, promotion, reassignment, or retention can fall within the FCRA when they meet the statutory definition of a consumer report. This can include information compiled from public records and employment history.

Employers using covered reports generally have obligations concerning authorization, disclosure, and adverse employment actions. Applicants and employees also have rights when information in a consumer report contributes to an adverse decision.

The specific requirements depend on how the information was obtained and used.

Employer Authorization and Disclosure

When an employer obtains a covered consumer report from a background screening company, federal law generally requires specific procedures before obtaining and using the report.

The joint EEOC and Federal Trade Commission guidance explains that employers using background reporting companies must comply with the FCRA, in addition to federal laws prohibiting employment discrimination.

The applicant should generally receive the required disclosure and provide authorization before the report is obtained. Additional requirements can apply when an employer intends to take adverse action based on information contained in the report.

These requirements are intended to give individuals an opportunity to understand and, where appropriate, challenge information being used in employment decisions.

Adverse Employment Decisions

A background report can become particularly important when an employer is considering rejecting an applicant, terminating an employee, or taking another adverse employment action because of information in the report.

The FCRA establishes procedures designed to provide consumers with notice and an opportunity to address potentially inaccurate information. The CFPB explains that when an employer uses a consumer report in making an adverse employment decision, the individual generally must receive appropriate notices and information concerning the report.

This process is sometimes described in terms of pre-adverse and adverse action procedures. The exact steps depend on the circumstances and applicable law.

Arrest Records Versus Convictions

Criminal background screening raises an important distinction between an arrest and a conviction.

An arrest is not equivalent to a finding that an individual committed a crime. The EEOC's enforcement guidance expressly states that the fact of an arrest does not establish that criminal conduct occurred. Employers may consider information concerning the underlying conduct in appropriate circumstances, but an arrest alone presents different legal considerations from a conviction.

A conviction generally provides stronger evidence that the underlying criminal conduct resulted in a formal adjudication. Even then, employers may need to consider whether the record is accurate, relevant to the position, and sufficiently connected to legitimate employment considerations.

Criminal Records and Employment Discrimination

Criminal history itself is not a protected characteristic under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act. Nevertheless, an employer's use of criminal records can create potential discrimination issues when the policy or practice disproportionately excludes individuals based on a protected characteristic.

The EEOC explains that a criminal-record screening policy can create disparate-impact concerns when it disproportionately excludes members of a protected group and the employer cannot demonstrate that the policy is job-related and consistent with business necessity.

The EEOC therefore recommends individualized consideration in appropriate circumstances rather than relying automatically on broad exclusions based on any criminal record.

Relevance to the Position

A central consideration in responsible background screening is whether information is actually relevant to the job.

For example, the considerations associated with a position involving financial responsibilities may differ from those associated with a job involving physical security, professional licensing, access to vulnerable populations, or highly sensitive information.

The EEOC's guidance discusses evaluating factors including the nature and gravity of the offense, the amount of time that has passed, and the nature of the job in determining whether criminal-history information is relevant.

This approach recognizes that a record's existence does not necessarily answer whether an individual is currently suitable for a particular position.

State and Local Regulation

Federal law is only one part of the legal environment surrounding employment screening.

States and municipalities may establish additional restrictions concerning criminal-history inquiries, credit reports, privacy, data retention, licensing, and the timing of background questions.

Some jurisdictions have adopted laws commonly associated with "fair chance" or "ban-the-box" approaches. These laws can restrict when employers may ask about criminal history or how they must evaluate such information.

The EEOC and FTC jointly advise employers to review state and local requirements in addition to federal law when conducting employment background checks.

Credit and Financial Information

Public-record screening can extend beyond criminal records. Employment reports can contain financial information and public court records such as bankruptcy filings.

The CFPB notes that employers may obtain credit reports for employment purposes when the FCRA's requirements are satisfied, including obtaining the individual's written permission. Additional federal, state, or local restrictions may affect whether and how employers can use such information.

Financial information may be more relevant to certain positions than others, but the legality and appropriateness of its use depend on the applicable legal framework.

Technology and Algorithmic Screening

Technology has changed how background information is collected and evaluated. Some screening companies use automated databases, matching systems, risk indicators, or algorithmic scores to organize information.

The CFPB has specifically addressed background dossiers and algorithmic scores used in employment decisions, explaining that such products can qualify as consumer reports under the FCRA when they meet the statutory definition.

Automation can make screening faster, but it does not eliminate the need for accuracy and legal compliance. An algorithm can potentially reproduce errors contained in source databases or create misleading conclusions from incomplete information.

Employers should therefore understand what information a screening product uses and how its results are generated before relying on it for significant employment decisions.

Rights of Job Applicants and Employees

Individuals subjected to background screening have several potential protections under federal law.

Where the FCRA applies, individuals may have rights concerning:

  • Disclosure and authorization
  • Access to consumer reports
  • Disputing inaccurate information
  • Notice before certain adverse actions
  • Receiving information used in an adverse decision
  • Obtaining information about consumer reporting agencies

The EEOC also explains that individuals are protected against employment discrimination when background information is used in ways that violate federal equal-employment laws.

These rights do not necessarily prevent employers from conducting lawful background checks. Instead, they establish requirements concerning how information is obtained, evaluated, and used.

Responsible Screening Practices

Employers can reduce legal and practical risks by developing consistent screening procedures.

A responsible program may include:

  1. Clearly defining which positions require screening.
  2. Identifying which categories of information are relevant to each position.
  3. Using reliable and legally compliant screening providers.
  4. Confirming potentially matching records through appropriate sources.
  5. Giving applicants required notices and opportunities to respond.
  6. Applying screening criteria consistently.
  7. Maintaining appropriate records of the decision-making process.
  8. Reviewing federal, state, and local requirements regularly.

These practices can help distinguish legitimate employment screening from automatic exclusion based on incomplete or irrelevant information.

Conclusion

The background screening industry has transformed the way employers access information about prospective and current employees. Public records can provide valuable information, but they are not necessarily complete, current, or error-free. The use of these records therefore requires attention to accuracy, relevance, privacy, consumer reporting requirements, and anti-discrimination law.

In the United States, the FCRA regulates many employment reports supplied by third-party consumer reporting agencies, while the EEOC's framework addresses the potential discriminatory effects of using criminal-history information in employment decisions. State and local laws can impose additional requirements.

For employers, effective screening involves more than obtaining a report. It requires understanding the source of the information, verifying potentially significant records, applying relevant criteria consistently, and following required procedural safeguards. For applicants and employees, understanding their rights can make it easier to identify and challenge inaccurate information that affects employment opportunities.
