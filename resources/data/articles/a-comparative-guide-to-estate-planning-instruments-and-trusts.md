Estate planning involves more than deciding who receives property after death. Individuals may need to address property ownership, incapacity, family circumstances, business interests, taxes, charitable objectives, and the administration of assets. Different legal instruments address different parts of this process, and a plan may combine several documents rather than rely on one instrument.

Wills, revocable trusts, irrevocable trusts, powers of attorney, beneficiary designations, and other arrangements operate differently under state and federal law. The appropriate structure depends on the individual's circumstances and the laws governing the relevant property and estate.

## Wills and Testamentary Planning

A will is a fundamental estate-planning document that generally specifies how property subject to the will should be distributed at death. It can also nominate an executor and, where applicable, identify guardians for minor children.

Property passing under a will generally goes through the probate process, although the exact procedures vary among states. The Uniform Law Commission's Uniform Probate Code provides a model framework for probate and related estate administration, but states differ in whether and how they have adopted its provisions.

A will does not necessarily control every asset owned by a person. Property held jointly with survivorship rights, assets with beneficiary designations, and certain trust assets may pass through mechanisms outside the probate estate.

A properly coordinated estate plan therefore requires consideration of how different assets are legally titled.

## Revocable Living Trusts

A revocable living trust is generally created during an individual's lifetime and can typically be amended or revoked by the person who established it, depending on the trust terms and applicable state law.

The American Bar Association notes that revocable trusts can be used for lifetime asset management and may provide mechanisms for managing property if the creator becomes incapacitated. The ABA also cautions that their usefulness varies according to individual circumstances.

A revocable trust does not automatically produce estate-tax savings. Because the creator generally retains substantial control over the trust property, the assets may remain included in the creator's taxable estate for federal estate-tax purposes.

The trust also needs to be properly funded. Simply signing a trust document does not necessarily transfer every asset into the trust.

## Irrevocable Trusts

An irrevocable trust generally places greater restrictions on the creator's ability to amend or revoke the arrangement. The precise degree of flexibility depends on the trust instrument and applicable state law.

The federal tax treatment of an irrevocable trust is not determined solely by the label "irrevocable." The IRS explains that an irrevocable trust may be treated as a grantor, simple, or complex trust depending on the powers and provisions contained in the trust instrument.

Irrevocable trusts can be used for a variety of estate-planning purposes, including certain wealth-transfer, asset-management, charitable, and tax-planning strategies. However, transferring assets into an irrevocable trust can have significant legal and tax consequences.

Because control, ownership, beneficiary rights, and tax treatment can vary substantially, these arrangements generally require careful drafting and ongoing administration.

## Testamentary Trusts

A testamentary trust is established through a will and comes into existence after the testator's death according to the terms of the estate plan.

The IRS distinguishes testamentary trusts from inter vivos trusts, explaining that a testamentary trust is created by a will and begins upon the death of the person making the will.

A testamentary trust can provide a framework for managing property for beneficiaries after death. For example, a will may direct that assets be held and distributed to a beneficiary according to specified conditions or at particular stages.

The arrangement can be useful when immediate outright distribution is not consistent with the estate plan, but its administration is governed by the trust document and applicable state law.

## Pour-Over Wills and Trust Coordination

A pour-over will is designed to transfer certain assets remaining outside an inter vivos trust into that trust at death.

The Uniform Law Commission's Uniform Testamentary Additions to Trusts Act specifically recognizes these provisions, commonly known as "pour-over" provisions. The Act allows property passing through probate to be added to an existing inter vivos trust.

A pour-over will does not eliminate the importance of properly funding a revocable trust during life. Assets that remain outside the trust may still be subject to probate before being transferred according to the will.

## Powers of Attorney

Estate planning also involves decisions that may arise during a person's lifetime.

A financial or property power of attorney allows an individual to designate another person to act on their behalf within the authority granted by the document. A health-care power of attorney or similar advance directive can address medical decision-making, depending on state law.

These documents serve a different function from wills and trusts. A will primarily addresses the distribution of property at death, while powers of attorney can provide authority for decision-making during the individual's lifetime.

The Uniform Law Commission has developed a Uniform Power of Attorney Act as a model for state legislation, although the rules governing powers of attorney remain jurisdiction-specific.

## Beneficiary Designations and Nonprobate Transfers

Certain assets can transfer at death through beneficiary designations rather than through the probate process.

Examples can include life insurance policies, retirement accounts, payable-on-death accounts, and other assets for which a beneficiary has been properly designated.

The Uniform Law Commission also recognizes statutory nonprobate transfer mechanisms, including transfer-on-death registration for certain securities.

Because beneficiary designations can operate independently of a will, they should be reviewed whenever an estate plan is created or updated. An outdated beneficiary designation can produce a distribution inconsistent with the individual's current intentions.

## Estate and Gift Tax Considerations

Estate planning may have federal tax implications for individuals with substantial assets. For people dying in 2026, the federal basic exclusion amount is **$15 million**, according to the IRS's 2026 Form 706 instructions. An estate-tax return may also be required in certain circumstances even when no estate tax is ultimately payable, including when an executor elects portability of a deceased spouse's unused exclusion.

Federal gift-tax rules can also apply to transfers made during life, including certain transfers made through trusts. The IRS explains that the gift tax can apply to transfers of real or personal property, whether tangible or intangible, directly or through a trust.

State estate and inheritance taxes may create additional considerations. Consequently, federal thresholds should not be treated as a complete analysis of an individual's potential transfer-tax exposure.

## Comparing Common Estate Planning Instruments

| Instrument | Primary Function | Typical Timing | Key Consideration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Will | Directs distribution of probate assets | At death | Generally subject to probate |
| Revocable living trust | Manages assets during life and after death | During life and after death | Generally remains revocable during creator's life |
| Irrevocable trust | Holds assets under more permanent trust terms | During life or through a testamentary arrangement | Reduced ability to modify may have legal and tax consequences |
| Testamentary trust | Creates a trust through a will | At death | Operates through the estate plan |
| Power of attorney | Authorizes another person to act during life | During life | Authority depends on document and state law |
| Beneficiary designation | Directs qualifying assets to named beneficiaries | At death | May operate independently of the will |
| Pour-over will | Transfers certain probate assets into an existing trust | At death | Does not eliminate the need for proper trust funding |

## Coordinating the Estate Plan

Estate planning instruments work best when their provisions are coordinated.

For example, an individual might establish a revocable trust, execute a pour-over will, maintain beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, and create powers of attorney for financial and health-care decisions.

The documents should not be reviewed independently. Changes to one document may affect the overall plan.

Ownership of real estate is particularly important. Deeds, titles, joint ownership arrangements, trust ownership, and beneficiary mechanisms can determine how property passes after death.

Business owners may also need separate planning for ownership interests, buy-sell agreements, succession arrangements, and the valuation of closely held businesses.

## Reviewing and Updating Estate Documents

Estate plans should be reviewed when significant circumstances change. Common triggers can include marriage, divorce, birth or adoption of a child, death of a beneficiary or fiduciary, acquisition or sale of substantial property, changes in business ownership, relocation to another state, or major changes in tax law.

Beneficiary designations should be reviewed alongside the estate documents rather than treated as a separate administrative task.

Changes in state law can also affect the operation of existing documents. The Uniform Law Commission's current probate and trust-law materials demonstrate that model statutes and amendments continue to develop over time.

## Conclusion

Estate planning instruments address different legal objectives. Wills provide instructions for property subject to the probate process, trusts can establish ongoing management and distribution structures, powers of attorney address lifetime decision-making, and beneficiary designations can control the transfer of particular assets outside probate.

No single instrument automatically provides the appropriate structure for every estate. The effectiveness of an estate plan depends on the individual's objectives, the nature and ownership of the assets, family circumstances, applicable state law, and relevant federal tax rules.

A comprehensive review should therefore consider the estate plan as an integrated set of documents and asset-transfer arrangements rather than as a single will or trust.
