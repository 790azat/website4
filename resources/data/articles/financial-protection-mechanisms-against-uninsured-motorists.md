A motor vehicle collision can create significant financial losses even when the injured person did nothing to cause the crash. The problem becomes more complicated when the at-fault driver has no liability insurance, carries inadequate limits, or cannot be identified after a hit-and-run.

Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage is designed to address some of this risk. Other forms of insurance, including collision coverage, medical payments coverage, and personal injury protection, may also provide financial protection depending on the policy and state law.

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) reports that 15.4% of U.S. motorists were uninsured in 2023, or approximately one in seven drivers. Rates varied substantially among states.

## 1. What Happens When an At-Fault Driver Has No Insurance?

In a conventional liability claim, an injured person may seek compensation from the insurance policy of the driver who caused the collision.

If that driver has no applicable liability insurance, the injured person may instead need to rely on other financial resources or available first-party insurance coverage.

Potential sources can include:

- Uninsured motorist coverage
- Underinsured motorist coverage
- Collision coverage
- Medical payments coverage
- Personal injury protection
- Health insurance
- The responsible driver's personal assets
- Other applicable insurance policies

The availability and interaction of these sources depend on state law and the individual policy.

## 2. Uninsured Motorist Bodily Injury Coverage

**Uninsured motorist bodily injury (UMBI)** coverage is designed to protect an insured person when an at-fault motorist has no applicable liability insurance.

NAIC explains that uninsured motorist coverage can apply when the responsible driver has no bodily injury liability insurance and can also apply in certain hit-and-run situations.

Depending on the policy and state, UMBI can potentially cover losses associated with:

- Medical expenses
- Lost income
- Certain other injury-related damages

The precise benefits and limitations depend on the policy and applicable state law.

UMBI is particularly relevant because a person can comply with insurance requirements and still be injured by someone who does not have adequate coverage.

## 3. Underinsured Motorist Coverage

An **underinsured motorist (UIM)** claim is different from an uninsured motorist claim.

The other driver has liability insurance, but the available limits may be insufficient to cover the injured person's losses.

For example, suppose a driver has $50,000 in applicable bodily injury liability coverage while the injured person's covered damages substantially exceed that amount. If the requirements for UIM coverage are satisfied, the injured person's own policy may provide additional protection, subject to its limits and applicable law.

NAIC describes UIM coverage as protection for situations in which an at-fault driver's insurance limits are inadequate to pay the full loss.

## 4. Uninsured Motorist Property Damage

Some states and insurers offer **uninsured motorist property damage (UMPD)** coverage.

UMPD can provide protection for vehicle or other covered property damage caused by an uninsured driver, subject to the policy.

NAIC notes that uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage can include both bodily injury and property-damage components, but availability and requirements vary by state.

UMPD should not be confused with UMBI. One addresses covered property damage, while the other addresses covered bodily injury losses.

## 5. Collision Coverage

Collision coverage can provide another layer of protection for damage to the insured vehicle.

Unlike liability insurance, collision coverage generally addresses damage to the policyholder's own vehicle resulting from a collision, subject to the policy's terms and deductible.

NAIC describes collision coverage as paying for physical damage to an insured vehicle resulting from a collision with another vehicle or object.

This can be useful when:

- The other driver is uninsured
- Liability is disputed
- The responsible driver cannot be identified
- The insured needs to repair the vehicle under their own policy

Collision coverage generally does not replace bodily injury coverage.

## 6. Medical Payments Coverage

**Medical payments coverage**, sometimes called MedPay, can help pay certain medical expenses resulting from an automobile accident, subject to the policy's terms and limits.

Depending on the state and policy, it may apply to:

- The policyholder
- Household family members
- Passengers
- Other covered individuals

Unlike a liability claim, medical payments coverage generally does not depend on proving that another driver was legally responsible.

NAIC identifies medical payments coverage as one of the coverages that may help pay medical expenses following an automobile accident.

## 7. Personal Injury Protection

**Personal injury protection (PIP)** is another form of first-party automobile coverage.

PIP is particularly important in states using no-fault insurance systems, where an individual's own insurer generally pays certain covered injury-related losses regardless of who caused the accident, subject to statutory and policy conditions.

Depending on the jurisdiction, PIP may cover:

- Medical expenses
- Rehabilitation expenses
- Lost wages
- Funeral expenses
- Other specified costs

NAIC explains that states use either tort or no-fault systems, and the applicable system affects the types of automobile coverage available.

## 8. Hit-and-Run Protection

A hit-and-run driver can create a problem similar to an uninsured motorist because the responsible driver may never be identified.

Uninsured motorist coverage may apply to certain hit-and-run accidents, depending on state law and policy requirements.

NAIC identifies hit-and-run vehicles among circumstances in which UM coverage can potentially apply.

Some policies may impose specific requirements, such as prompt reporting to police or other procedures for establishing that a hit-and-run occurred.

The exact requirements should be checked against the applicable policy and state law.

## 9. State Requirements Differ

One of the most important considerations is that uninsured and underinsured motorist rules are not uniform across the United States.

NAIC's current automobile insurance data indicates that states differ in whether UM coverage is mandatory, whether it must be offered and may be rejected, and whether UIM coverage is required.

For example, NAIC reports that 23 states require UM coverage, while 19 additional states require the coverage unless it is rejected in writing.

These figures illustrate why consumers should not assume that the requirements in one state apply elsewhere.

## 10. Coverage Limits Matter

Having UM or UIM coverage does not necessarily mean every loss will be fully compensated.

Policies have limits.

For example, an insured might have:

- $50,000 of UM bodily injury coverage per person
- $100,000 per accident

If covered damages exceed the available limits, the policy may not provide enough insurance to cover the entire loss.

The appropriate limits depend on the individual's circumstances, state requirements, other insurance, and policy options.

## 11. Multiple Policies May Be Relevant

In some situations, more than one insurance policy may potentially apply.

For example, coverage could involve:

- The injured person's automobile policy
- A household member's policy
- The other driver's liability policy
- A commercial policy
- Employer-related coverage
- Other applicable insurance

Whether coverage can be combined, or "stacked," depends heavily on state law and policy language.

Some jurisdictions permit certain forms of stacking, while others restrict or prohibit it under particular circumstances.

## 12. The Difference Between Liability and First-Party Coverage

It is useful to distinguish two basic categories.

### Liability coverage

The at-fault driver's insurance generally pays covered losses for which the insured is legally responsible, subject to policy limits and other conditions.

### First-party coverage

The injured person's own insurance may provide certain benefits regardless of whether another driver has adequate insurance.

UM/UIM, collision, MedPay, and PIP can function as forms of first-party protection, although their operation and legal characteristics differ.

NAIC explains that liability coverage generally addresses damage or injury caused to others, while UM/UIM coverage can protect an insured when the responsible driver lacks sufficient insurance.

## 13. Financial Protection for Pedestrians and Passengers

Uninsured-motorist protection is not necessarily limited to the person driving the insured vehicle.

Depending on the policy and state law, UM coverage can potentially protect covered passengers and can apply in certain situations involving pedestrians.

The Insurance Information Institute notes that UM bodily injury coverage may apply when an insured person is struck as a pedestrian by an uninsured driver.

The exact definition of an insured person should always be checked in the applicable policy.

## 14. Evidence Can Affect an Uninsured-Motorist Claim

A UM or UIM claim may still require investigation.

Potential evidence can include:

- Police reports
- Photographs
- Video
- Witness statements
- Medical records
- Vehicle damage
- Repair estimates
- Accident reconstruction
- Proof of lost income
- Insurance information

The fact that another driver lacks insurance does not eliminate the need to establish the circumstances and losses associated with the collision.

A policy may also impose specific notice and cooperation requirements.

## 15. Determining Whether Another Driver Is Truly Uninsured

The apparent absence of an insurance card at the scene does not necessarily establish that no coverage exists.

Insurance status may need to be verified through:

- The driver's insurer
- State insurance databases
- Law enforcement records
- Registration information
- Other official sources

There can also be situations in which an insurer later denies coverage or becomes insolvent. NAIC includes certain coverage-denial and insolvency situations within its description of circumstances potentially addressed by uninsured-motorist protection.

## 16. Deductibles and Coverage Conditions

Some forms of first-party protection may involve deductibles or other conditions.

For example, collision coverage commonly has a deductible.

A policy may also require:

- Prompt notice
- Cooperation with the insurer
- A police report
- Proof of the accident
- Medical documentation
- Notice before settlement with the responsible driver

These requirements vary by policy and jurisdiction.

Failure to follow a contractual or statutory requirement can affect how a claim is handled, although the legal effect depends on the circumstances.

## 17. Health Insurance May Also Be Relevant

An injured person may have health insurance that pays medical expenses following a collision.

Health insurance and automobile coverage can interact in complicated ways.

Questions can include:

- Which policy pays first?
- Is reimbursement required?
- Does a health insurer have a subrogation right?
- Are medical bills covered under PIP or MedPay?
- Can UM/UIM benefits apply to remaining losses?

The answers depend on the policies, applicable state law, and the circumstances of the claim.

## 18. Financial Recovery From the Uninsured Driver

A person injured by an uninsured driver may also have a legal claim directly against that driver.

In theory, a judgment can establish an obligation to pay damages.

The practical issue is whether the responsible person has assets or income from which a judgment can be collected.

This is one reason insurance protection can be important: obtaining a judgment against an uninsured driver does not necessarily guarantee that the judgment will be collectible.

## 19. Insurance Verification Systems

States have also developed mechanisms designed to reduce the number of uninsured vehicles.

NAIC notes that more than half of the states have enacted laws and begun developing or implementing online auto-insurance verification systems.

These systems can help government authorities identify vehicles that may not have required insurance.

Insurance verification is primarily a regulatory mechanism, however, and does not replace the financial protection provided by a consumer's own insurance coverage.

## 20. Why Uninsured-Motorist Coverage Matters Financially

The financial exposure created by an uninsured driver can be substantial.

A serious collision may involve:

- Emergency medical treatment
- Hospitalization
- Rehabilitation
- Lost income
- Vehicle repairs
- Vehicle replacement
- Long-term medical care
- Other economic losses

The responsible driver's lack of insurance does not make those expenses disappear.

UM and UIM coverage can transfer some of that financial risk from the injured person to their own insurance policy, subject to policy limits, exclusions, and applicable law.

## 21. Reviewing a Policy Before a Collision

Insurance protection is easier to evaluate before a loss occurs.

A policyholder can review:

- UM bodily injury limits
- UIM bodily injury limits
- UM property damage availability
- Collision coverage
- Deductibles
- PIP or MedPay limits
- Exclusions
- Definitions of insured persons
- Stacking provisions
- State-specific endorsements

NAIC recommends comparing coverage limits and understanding available UM/UIM options when evaluating automobile insurance.

## 22. Comparing Coverage With Potential Financial Exposure

Minimum legal insurance requirements do not necessarily correspond to the amount of financial risk created by a serious accident.

State minimum liability limits vary substantially, and NAIC's current materials show significant differences among jurisdictions.

A policyholder evaluating financial protection may therefore consider the relationship between:

**Potential Loss → Other Driver's Coverage → Available UM/UIM Limits → Other First-Party Coverage**

The objective is to understand how much of a potential loss could remain uninsured.

## 23. Insurance Does Not Eliminate Every Financial Risk

Even substantial insurance coverage can contain limits and exclusions.

Potential gaps can arise from:

- Policy limits
- Deductibles
- Exclusions
- Uncovered damages
- Coverage disputes
- State-specific restrictions
- Multiple-claim situations
- Insufficient documentation

Insurance is therefore a financial risk-transfer mechanism, not a guarantee that every loss will be reimbursed.

## Final Takeaway

Protection against uninsured motorists can involve several layers of financial protection.

**Uninsured motorist coverage** can protect against certain losses caused by uninsured or hit-and-run drivers. **Underinsured motorist coverage** can provide additional protection when the at-fault driver's liability limits are insufficient. **Collision coverage** can address covered damage to the insured vehicle, while **PIP or MedPay** may help with certain injury-related expenses depending on the state and policy.

The importance of these protections is illustrated by the NAIC's estimate that 15.4% of U.S. motorists were uninsured in 2023.

However, coverage rules vary considerably by state. Requirements concerning UM/UIM coverage, stacking, deductibles, hit-and-run claims, no-fault benefits, and available property-damage protection can all differ.

For that reason, policyholders should review the actual policy language and applicable state requirements rather than assuming that another driver's lack of insurance automatically determines how their own coverage will respond.
