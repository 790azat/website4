Commercial contracts provide the legal framework for business relationships involving the sale of goods, services, licensing, financing, distribution, construction, technology, real estate, and other commercial activities. A well-structured agreement does more than record what the parties have agreed to do. It establishes responsibilities, allocates risks, defines remedies, and creates procedures for addressing circumstances that may arise after signing.

Contract drafting and review therefore involve both legal analysis and practical business planning. The American Bar Association's contract-drafting resources emphasize the importance of clear language, careful organization, and attention to potential ambiguity because poorly drafted provisions can contribute to later disputes.

## Identifying the Commercial Purpose

The starting point for drafting is understanding the transaction itself.

Before preparing contractual language, the parties and their advisers should identify:

- The purpose of the agreement
- The parties involved
- The goods or services being provided
- The expected commercial outcome
- Payment arrangements
- Performance obligations
- Key deadlines
- Allocation of risk
- Potential regulatory requirements
- Conditions for termination
- Procedures for resolving disputes

A contract that accurately reflects the commercial arrangement is easier to administer than one assembled primarily from generic provisions.

The ABA has emphasized that effective contract work begins before drafting, with lawyers identifying client objectives, strengths and weaknesses, performance requirements, and potential future events that may require contractual protection.

## Defining the Parties and Their Authority

A commercial agreement should clearly identify each contracting party and its legal capacity.

Depending on the transaction, the contract may identify:

- Legal entity names
- Entity types
- Jurisdictions of formation
- Principal addresses
- Authorized representatives
- Affiliates involved in performance
- Guarantors
- Parent companies

The drafter should also determine whether the person signing the agreement has appropriate authority.

This becomes particularly important in transactions involving subsidiaries, joint ventures, partnerships, or agents. The identity of the actual contracting party can affect payment obligations, liability, enforcement, and the availability of remedies.

## Establishing Definitions

Definitions can provide a common vocabulary for the agreement.

Important commercial terms may include definitions for:

- Products
- Services
- Deliverables
- Confidential Information
- Intellectual Property
- Business Day
- Applicable Law
- Taxes
- Change Orders
- Term
- Material Breach

Definitions should serve a practical purpose. Overly broad or complicated definitions can introduce ambiguity rather than eliminate it.

Defined terms should also be used consistently throughout the agreement. If a term is defined in one section but used differently elsewhere, the resulting inconsistency can create interpretation problems.

## Structuring the Scope of Performance

The operative provisions should explain what each party is required to do.

For a services agreement, this may include descriptions of deliverables, service levels, staffing, reporting, acceptance procedures, and performance standards. For a sales agreement, the provisions may address products, quantities, delivery, inspection, acceptance, warranties, and payment.

The level of specificity should correspond to the transaction.

Vague language such as "provide appropriate services" may leave significant questions unanswered. More precise drafting can establish measurable obligations while allowing appropriate flexibility where circumstances may change.

The ABA's contract-drafting materials emphasize clarity and precision in expressing contractual obligations and avoiding unnecessary ambiguity.

## Addressing Price and Payment

Payment provisions are among the most commercially important sections of an agreement.

They can address:

- Contract price
- Currency
- Invoicing
- Payment deadlines
- Taxes
- Late-payment charges
- Reimbursement of expenses
- Deposits
- Milestone payments
- Retainage
- Disputed invoices
- Refunds and credits

The agreement should also identify what documentation is required before payment becomes due.

For transactions involving recurring services or variable pricing, the contract may establish a formula for calculating charges rather than specifying one fixed amount.

## Representations and Warranties

Representations generally address factual statements made by the parties, while warranties can allocate responsibility for specified conditions or characteristics. The distinction and legal effect depend on the governing law and contractual language.

Commercial agreements may contain representations concerning:

- Authority to enter the agreement
- Ownership of assets
- Compliance with law
- Intellectual-property rights
- Financial information
- Absence of specified litigation
- Corporate organization

Warranties can address the quality, performance, conformity, or characteristics of goods and services.

The provisions should be connected to the transaction's actual risks rather than copied mechanically from unrelated agreements.

## Covenants and Ongoing Obligations

Some contractual responsibilities continue throughout the relationship.

These obligations, often expressed as covenants, can require a party to:

- Maintain insurance
- Protect confidential information
- Comply with applicable laws
- Maintain licenses
- Meet reporting requirements
- Obtain approvals
- Maintain specified standards
- Avoid particular conduct
- Cooperate with audits

Ongoing obligations should be reviewed alongside termination provisions so the parties understand which requirements survive termination and which end when the contractual relationship ends.

## Conditions and Contingencies

Some commercial transactions should not proceed unless specified conditions are satisfied.

Conditions can concern:

- Regulatory approvals
- Financing
- Delivery of required documents
- Completion of due diligence
- Third-party consents
- Insurance
- Licensing
- Corporate approvals

The contract should identify whether a condition must be satisfied before the agreement becomes effective, before a particular obligation arises, or before closing.

Clear drafting can reduce disputes over whether a party was actually required to perform.

## Limitation of Liability and Indemnification

Risk-allocation provisions can become particularly important when a transaction involves substantial financial exposure.

A limitation-of-liability clause may establish a maximum amount recoverable for specified claims. It may also exclude particular categories of damages, subject to applicable law.

Indemnification provisions operate differently. They can require one party to assume responsibility for specified claims, losses, liabilities, or expenses arising from defined circumstances.

The relationship between indemnification and liability limitations should be reviewed carefully. A contract can create uncertainty if one provision appears to cap liability while another creates an apparently unlimited indemnification obligation.

## Confidentiality and Intellectual Property

Commercial transactions frequently involve confidential business information and intellectual property.

Confidentiality provisions can establish:

- What information is protected
- Permitted uses
- Authorized recipients
- Security requirements
- Exceptions
- Return or destruction obligations
- Duration of confidentiality

Intellectual-property provisions should separately address ownership and licensing.

For example, a services agreement may need to distinguish between intellectual property that existed before the relationship and material created specifically during the engagement.

The contract should also address whether either party receives continuing rights to use materials after termination.

## Term, Renewal, and Termination

Every commercial agreement should establish when the relationship begins and how it ends.

Term provisions can provide for:

- A fixed duration
- Automatic renewal
- Renewal by mutual agreement
- Termination for convenience
- Termination for material breach
- Termination following insolvency
- Termination following specified regulatory events

Termination provisions should be coordinated with post-termination obligations.

These can include final payments, return of property, data transfer, confidentiality, intellectual-property rights, transition assistance, and dispute-resolution provisions.

## Reviewing Boilerplate Provisions

Provisions commonly placed near the end of commercial agreements should not be dismissed as insignificant.

The ABA has specifically noted that so-called boilerplate can determine important issues including governing law, dispute forum, assignment, third-party rights, waiver, interpretation, and contractual notices.

Common provisions include:

- Governing law
- Venue
- Dispute resolution
- Assignment
- Notices
- Force majeure
- Waiver
- Severability
- Entire agreement
- Amendment
- Counterparts
- Electronic signatures
- Third-party beneficiaries
- Order of precedence

These clauses can become especially important when a dispute occurs. A governing-law clause may determine which legal rules apply, while a forum-selection provision can determine where litigation must occur.

## Dispute Resolution

Dispute provisions should be considered before a disagreement develops.

A contract can establish litigation, arbitration, mediation, or another dispute-resolution process. It may also specify the applicable rules, location, governing law, procedural requirements, and allocation of certain costs.

The ABA recognizes that dispute-management provisions can be drafted in advance to address issues such as forum, procedure, discovery, timing, and applicable rules.

The appropriate mechanism depends on the transaction, jurisdictions involved, commercial objectives, and nature of potential disputes.

## Reviewing the Contract From Multiple Perspectives

Contract review should not be limited to checking individual clauses for grammatical or legal errors.

A comprehensive review can ask:

1. Does the agreement accurately reflect the commercial deal?
2. Are the parties correctly identified?
3. Are performance obligations measurable?
4. Are payment terms complete?
5. Are important risks allocated clearly?
6. Are representations and warranties appropriate?
7. Do indemnification and liability provisions work together?
8. Are termination rights practical?
9. Are intellectual-property rights addressed?
10. Are governing law and dispute provisions appropriate?
11. Do the schedules and exhibits match the main agreement?
12. Are defined terms used consistently?

Reviewing the contract as an integrated document can reveal problems that may not be visible when individual clauses are examined separately.

## Comparing Drafts and Managing Negotiations

Commercial contracts frequently go through multiple versions.

Each revision should be compared against the previous draft so that material changes are identified and understood. Particular attention should be given to modifications involving price, liability, warranties, termination, intellectual property, confidentiality, dispute resolution, and governing law.

Negotiation should also distinguish between essential business requirements and provisions where compromise is possible.

The ABA's negotiation guidance emphasizes preparation, including understanding the client's interests, alternatives, negotiation objectives, and potential positions before entering negotiations.

This preparation can help ensure that contractual concessions are deliberate rather than accidental.

## Managing Exhibits and Incorporated Documents

Commercial agreements often incorporate schedules, statements of work, specifications, policies, technical documents, or purchase orders.

These materials should be reviewed together with the principal agreement.

The contract should establish an order of precedence when incorporated documents could conflict. For example, the agreement may specify whether the master contract controls over a statement of work or whether a later amendment controls over an earlier schedule.

Failing to address document hierarchy can create uncertainty when different contractual materials contain inconsistent obligations.

## Final Execution and Contract Administration

Drafting does not end when the parties sign.

The final executed version should be stored securely, together with amendments, schedules, exhibits, certificates, and related approvals.

Businesses can also maintain a contract-management system tracking:

- Renewal dates
- Notice deadlines
- Payment obligations
- Insurance requirements
- Reporting requirements
- Performance milestones
- Termination rights
- Compliance obligations

This allows the company to manage the agreement as an ongoing business instrument rather than simply an archived legal document.

## Conclusion

Commercial contract drafting and review involve a sequence of interconnected tasks: understanding the transaction, identifying the parties, defining obligations, allocating risk, establishing payment and performance requirements, addressing intellectual property and confidentiality, and creating procedures for termination and dispute resolution.

Clear drafting can reduce ambiguity, but effective contract review requires attention to the agreement as a whole. Even provisions commonly described as boilerplate can affect governing law, enforcement, assignment, notice, dispute resolution, and other fundamental issues.

A disciplined process of planning, drafting, negotiation, revision, execution, and contract administration can help businesses create agreements that more accurately reflect their commercial objectives and provide clearer mechanisms for addressing problems when circumstances change.

Because contract law varies by jurisdiction and transaction type, significant commercial agreements should be reviewed under the law applicable to the parties and the particular transaction.
