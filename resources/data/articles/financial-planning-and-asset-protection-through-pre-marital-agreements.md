Marriage can combine two people's financial lives in ways that extend beyond their personal relationship. Property, income, business interests, investments, debts, inheritances, and future financial obligations may become relevant during a marriage or if the relationship later ends.

A pre-marital agreement, commonly called a prenuptial agreement or prenup, can provide a framework for addressing certain financial matters before marriage. Rather than functioning solely as a document for divorce, a carefully prepared agreement can be part of broader financial and estate planning.

The legal effect of a pre-marital agreement depends heavily on the jurisdiction. Different states and countries apply different rules concerning enforceability, disclosure, independent legal advice, timing, and the subjects that may legally be addressed.

## What Is a Pre-Marital Agreement?

A pre-marital agreement is a contract entered into by people who intend to marry and that generally becomes effective upon marriage.

The Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act defines a premarital agreement as an agreement between prospective spouses concerning rights and obligations during marriage or upon separation, divorce, or death. The Act has been adopted in different forms by some U.S. jurisdictions.

Pre-marital agreements are primarily concerned with financial and property matters. They may address how certain property will be classified, how debts will be handled, whether particular assets will remain separate, and how certain financial issues will be treated if the marriage ends.

However, a prenup is not a document that can simply override every rule of family law. Certain provisions may be restricted or unenforceable, particularly provisions concerning children's rights.

## Identifying Separate and Marital Property

One of the principal functions of a pre-marital agreement is to establish how property will be characterized.

A person may enter a marriage owning a home, investment account, business, retirement assets, intellectual property, or other property acquired before the relationship. Without a clear agreement, the treatment of those assets may depend on the property laws of the applicable jurisdiction.

A prenup can establish whether certain assets are intended to remain separate property and can address how income or appreciation associated with those assets will be treated.

This can be particularly relevant when one person owns a business before marriage. The American Bar Association notes that a properly structured premarital agreement may help establish how a business interest is treated and can potentially reduce disputes concerning ownership if the marriage later ends.

The precise legal effect depends on applicable state or national law.

## Business Interests and Professional Assets

Business owners may have additional reasons to consider a pre-marital agreement.

A business can increase in value during a marriage, and the involvement of either spouse may affect questions concerning ownership or financial claims. A business may also involve other owners, employees, investors, or contractual obligations that make uncertainty about ownership particularly significant.

A pre-marital agreement can establish provisions concerning the characterization of an existing business interest and, depending on local law, address how increases in value or future contributions will be treated.

The agreement should not be viewed as a substitute for appropriate business documentation. Operating agreements, shareholder agreements, trusts, corporate records, and estate-planning documents may also be necessary.

## Debt Management

Financial planning before marriage should address liabilities as well as assets.

One prospective spouse may enter the marriage with student loans, credit-card balances, business obligations, tax liabilities, or other debts. A pre-marital agreement can potentially establish how certain debts are intended to be treated between the spouses.

This can help clarify whether particular obligations are intended to remain associated with the spouse who incurred them.

However, a private agreement between spouses does not necessarily eliminate a creditor's rights. A creditor that is not a party to the agreement may still have rights under applicable law.

For that reason, debt provisions should be drafted with an understanding of both family law and relevant creditor rules.

## Financial Disclosure Before Signing

Financial disclosure is one of the most important elements of preparing a pre-marital agreement.

Both parties should have a clear understanding of the financial circumstances being addressed. Depending on applicable law, this can involve disclosure of assets, debts, income, business interests, investments, and other financial information.

The American Bar Association identifies full financial disclosure as an important consideration in the enforceability of premarital agreements and notes that requirements vary by jurisdiction.

Recent case law also demonstrates why disclosure can matter. For example, a 2026 Georgia Court of Appeals decision discussed requirements involving full and fair disclosure and voluntary execution with an opportunity for independent counsel.

Because requirements differ, parties should not assume that disclosure practices acceptable in one jurisdiction will necessarily satisfy another jurisdiction's rules.

## Independent Legal Representation

Independent legal advice can help each person understand the rights they may be giving up or preserving.

A prenup is an agreement between two parties whose financial interests may not always be identical. Having separate attorneys allows each person to receive advice concerning their own interests and the consequences of the proposed terms.

The American Bar Association notes that independent counsel is an important consideration in the preparation of premarital agreements and that specific requirements differ among jurisdictions.

Independent representation can also make the negotiation process more transparent. Each person has an opportunity to ask questions, propose changes, and understand the agreement before signing.

## Timing and Voluntary Agreement

The timing of a pre-marital agreement can also be significant.

An agreement signed immediately before a wedding may raise different legal and practical questions from one negotiated well in advance. Some jurisdictions have specific requirements or legal standards concerning voluntariness, timing, or circumstances surrounding execution.

A person should have sufficient opportunity to review the agreement and obtain independent advice rather than being presented with a final document immediately before the wedding.

The objective is to establish a genuine agreement rather than create circumstances in which one party feels compelled to sign without meaningful opportunity for review.

## Estate Planning and Inheritance

Pre-marital agreements can also interact with estate planning.

People entering a later marriage may have children from a previous relationship, existing trusts, substantial assets, or established inheritance plans. They may want to clarify how those interests relate to their future marriage.

A prenup can form one component of this planning, but it generally should not be treated as a replacement for a will, trust, beneficiary designation, power of attorney, or other estate-planning documents.

The American Bar Association recommends reviewing estate plans after significant life events, including marriage, and emphasizes the importance of ensuring that estate-planning documents are consistent with applicable marital agreements.

This coordination can be particularly important where a person wants to preserve assets for children from a previous relationship while also providing for a future spouse.

## Spousal Support and Financial Obligations

Depending on local law, a pre-marital agreement may address certain rights to spousal support.

The extent to which prospective spouses can waive, limit, or establish support obligations varies by jurisdiction. Courts may also review provisions under standards concerning fairness, disclosure, voluntariness, or public policy.

The American Bar Association notes that spousal support and property division are among the financial subjects commonly addressed in premarital agreements.

Because these provisions can have significant financial consequences, they should be evaluated under the law that is likely to govern the agreement.

## What a Prenup Cannot Generally Decide

A pre-marital agreement is not an unlimited contract.

For example, parties generally cannot use a prenup to predetermine a child's best interests or permanently control future custody and parenting-time decisions in a way that overrides applicable family law.

The American Bar Association explains that premarital agreements cannot adversely affect children's rights, including rights concerning child support and custodial responsibilities.

The same principle applies to other matters that a jurisdiction treats as outside the permissible scope of a premarital contract.

Understanding these limitations is essential when drafting an agreement.

## Protecting Future Financial Interests

A well-structured agreement can address not only assets that exist before marriage but, where permitted, certain financial arrangements concerning property acquired during the marriage.

For example, parties may establish rules concerning:

- Premarital property
- Business interests
- Investment accounts
- Real estate
- Inheritances
- Certain debts
- Retirement interests
- Income and earnings
- Property acquired during marriage
- Spousal support
- Financial obligations following separation or divorce

The precise provisions available depend on applicable law.

The agreement should therefore be designed around the couple's actual financial circumstances rather than relying on a generic template.

## Reviewing the Agreement Over Time

Financial circumstances can change significantly after marriage.

A person may start a business, receive an inheritance, acquire real estate, have children, experience a substantial change in income, or move to another jurisdiction.

These changes may affect whether the original financial plan remains appropriate.

The American Bar Association recommends reviewing estate-planning arrangements periodically and following significant life changes.

Depending on the circumstances, spouses may also consider whether a post-marital agreement or amendment is appropriate. Any modification should comply with the applicable legal requirements.

## Coordinating a Prenup With the Overall Financial Plan

A pre-marital agreement is most effective when considered as part of a broader financial plan.

Before marriage, couples may benefit from discussing:

- Existing assets and liabilities
- Income and employment
- Business ownership
- Retirement planning
- Investment goals
- Housing
- Estate planning
- Inheritance expectations
- Insurance
- Financial responsibilities during marriage
- Long-term family goals

These discussions can provide a clearer picture of how the couple intends to organize their financial lives.

The American Bar Association describes premarital negotiations as an opportunity for prospective spouses to discuss how they intend to manage earnings, build assets, and approach financial goals during marriage.

## Conclusion

Pre-marital agreements can serve as useful financial-planning instruments when they are carefully prepared and consistent with applicable law. They can clarify the treatment of existing assets, address certain debts and business interests, establish financial expectations, and complement estate-planning arrangements.

Their effectiveness depends on more than simply signing a document. Financial disclosure, voluntary participation, appropriate timing, independent legal advice, and compliance with jurisdiction-specific requirements can all affect enforceability.

A prenup also has defined limits. It cannot simply determine every aspect of a future divorce or override legal protections concerning children and other matters governed by public policy.

For couples considering a pre-marital agreement, the most useful approach is generally to view it as one part of a broader conversation about financial planning, property, estate planning, and long-term responsibilities. Because family and property laws vary substantially by jurisdiction, individualized legal advice is important before relying on any particular provision.
