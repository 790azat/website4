Workplace safety extends beyond physical conditions. Employees also need an environment where they can perform their responsibilities without being subjected to harassment, intimidation, discrimination, or other inappropriate conduct. Effective workplace harassment prevention combines clear standards, accessible reporting procedures, management accountability, employee education, and timely responses to complaints.

Although legal definitions and employer obligations differ among jurisdictions, international labor standards increasingly recognize freedom from violence and harassment as an important component of decent work. The International Labour Organization (ILO) Convention No. 190 establishes an international framework addressing violence and harassment in the world of work, including gender-based violence and harassment.

## Understanding Workplace Harassment

Workplace harassment can involve unwelcome conduct that affects an employee's dignity, safety, employment conditions, or ability to perform their job. Depending on applicable law, harassment may include verbal abuse, unwanted sexual conduct, discriminatory treatment, threats, intimidation, offensive communications, or other inappropriate behavior.

Not every unpleasant workplace interaction necessarily meets the legal definition of harassment. Legal standards can depend on factors such as the nature of the conduct, its frequency, its severity, whether it is connected to a protected characteristic, and whether it affects an employment decision or working conditions.

The distinction between legally actionable harassment and conduct that violates an employer's internal standards is important. Organizations can establish workplace rules that prohibit inappropriate behavior even when particular conduct does not independently satisfy the legal threshold for unlawful harassment.

## Establishing Clear Workplace Standards

A prevention program should begin with a written policy explaining what behavior is prohibited and how concerns can be reported. A useful policy should address harassment by supervisors, coworkers, contractors, customers, clients, and other individuals who interact with employees when applicable.

An effective policy commonly explains:

- Prohibited conduct and examples of inappropriate behavior
- Available reporting channels
- How complaints are investigated
- Confidentiality limitations
- Protection against retaliation
- Potential disciplinary measures
- Responsibilities of managers and employees
- Procedures for urgent safety concerns

The ILO's framework emphasizes workplace policies, risk assessment, employee information and training, and mechanisms for prevention and response.

## Leadership and Organizational Accountability

Policies are unlikely to be effective if employees believe management will ignore violations. Leadership therefore plays an important role in establishing expectations and demonstrating that workplace standards apply across organizational levels.

The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) has identified several practices associated with effective harassment prevention, including committed leadership, consistent accountability, comprehensive policies, accessible complaint procedures, and regular interactive training.

Managers should understand that their responsibilities extend beyond reacting to formal complaints. They may need to recognize warning signs, respond appropriately to concerns, preserve relevant information, and escalate matters to human resources or other designated personnel.

## Creating Accessible Reporting Channels

Employees may hesitate to report harassment when they believe reporting will damage their careers, relationships with colleagues, or reputation within the organization. Multiple reporting options can make it easier for employees to raise concerns.

Depending on the organization, reporting channels may include:

1. A direct supervisor
2. Human resources personnel
3. Another designated manager
4. An ethics or compliance department
5. An independent reporting mechanism

The reporting process should be understandable and reasonably accessible. Employees should know where to go, what information may be requested, and what happens after a report is made.

The EEOC similarly advises employees to review their employer's anti-harassment policy and follow established reporting procedures, while recognizing that alternative supervisory channels may be available when an employee is uncomfortable approaching a particular person.

## Investigating Complaints Fairly

A workplace complaint should be treated seriously without assuming the outcome before relevant facts have been reviewed. An investigation may involve interviewing the complainant, the person accused of misconduct, and relevant witnesses.

Investigators may also need to examine:

- Emails and workplace messages
- Employment records
- Scheduling information
- Relevant policies
- Previous complaints or documented incidents
- Physical or digital evidence
- Statements from witnesses

Investigations should be handled by individuals with appropriate training and sufficient independence for the circumstances. Organizations should also avoid unnecessary disclosure of sensitive information.

Confidentiality should not be presented as an absolute guarantee when an investigation requires information to be shared with people responsible for addressing the matter.

## Preventing Retaliation

Fear of retaliation can discourage employees from reporting workplace problems. Retaliation can include adverse treatment because an employee made a complaint, participated in an investigation, or otherwise exercised a protected workplace right.

Employers should therefore communicate that retaliation is prohibited and explain how employees can report suspected retaliation separately from the underlying complaint.

Protection against retaliation also supports the integrity of workplace investigations because witnesses and employees may be more willing to cooperate when they understand that retaliatory conduct is not tolerated.

## Training Employees and Managers

Training can help employees recognize inappropriate behavior before problems become more serious. However, training is most useful when it is practical rather than limited to a presentation about legal terminology.

Employees can be taught how to:

- Recognize common forms of harassment
- Understand workplace reporting procedures
- Respond appropriately when witnessing misconduct
- Preserve relevant evidence
- Avoid retaliatory behavior
- Raise concerns safely

Managers require additional training because they may receive complaints directly or become aware of conduct that requires escalation.

## Addressing Digital and Remote Workplaces

Modern workplace harassment can occur through digital communication as well as in physical workplaces. Email, workplace messaging platforms, video conferences, collaboration software, and other technologies can create additional opportunities for inappropriate conduct.

The ILO's Convention No. 190 specifically recognizes work-related communications enabled by information and communication technologies within its scope.

Organizations should therefore ensure that workplace conduct policies apply to relevant digital communications. Employees working remotely should still have access to reporting channels, investigations, managerial support, and applicable workplace protections.

## Assessing Workplace Risks

Prevention is more effective when organizations identify circumstances that may increase the risk of harassment or violence. Certain workplaces may involve heightened exposure because of isolated working conditions, public interaction, late-night operations, customer-facing roles, or other organizational characteristics.

The ILO recommends identifying hazards and assessing risks associated with violence and harassment, including psychosocial risks, and taking appropriate preventive measures.

For workplaces where harassment overlaps with physical violence or threats, occupational safety programs can also be relevant. OSHA recommends workplace violence prevention programs that can include hazard assessment, prevention measures, training, and procedures for responding to incidents.

## Building a Culture of Respect

A written policy is only one component of a safe workplace. Organizations also need everyday practices that reinforce professional conduct.

This can include consistent enforcement of workplace rules, respectful communication from managers, fair disciplinary processes, opportunities for employees to raise concerns, and periodic reviews of workplace policies.

Employers can also examine complaint patterns and employee feedback to identify recurring problems. If multiple complaints involve the same department, management practice, communication channel, or working arrangement, that pattern may warrant additional preventive measures.

## Conclusion

Preventing workplace harassment requires more than responding after an incident occurs. Organizations can reduce risks by establishing clear behavioral standards, providing accessible reporting procedures, training employees and managers, investigating concerns fairly, protecting against retaliation, and regularly evaluating workplace risks.

The broader international framework also demonstrates that workplace harassment is not limited to a particular industry or employment arrangement. ILO Convention No. 190 takes a broad approach to violence and harassment in the world of work, covering conduct connected with work and recognizing the importance of prevention, protection, remedies, and enforcement.

Ultimately, a safe workplace depends on both formal systems and everyday organizational practices. Clear expectations, credible accountability, and accessible avenues for raising concerns can help create working environments where employees can perform their roles with greater security and dignity.
