Internal communications and employment records can become important evidence in workplace disputes, regulatory investigations, administrative proceedings, and litigation. Emails, text messages, personnel files, performance reviews, payroll records, policies, meeting notes, and electronic documents may help establish what happened, when decisions were made, and who participated in particular events.

Preserving these materials requires more than simply keeping copies of important emails. Employers and employees may have different legal obligations concerning recordkeeping, privacy, confidentiality, retention periods, and litigation holds. The appropriate process therefore depends on the nature of the records, the applicable law, and whether a legal dispute is anticipated or already underway.

## What Constitutes Employment Documentation?

Employment documentation includes records created or maintained in connection with the employment relationship. Some records are formal personnel documents, while others may be ordinary business communications that later become relevant to a dispute.

Examples include:

- Employment contracts and amendments
- Job descriptions
- Payroll and compensation records
- Time and attendance records
- Performance evaluations
- Disciplinary records
- Leave documentation
- Workplace policies
- Training records
- Workplace complaints
- Investigation materials
- Emails
- Text messages
- Internal messaging-platform communications
- Meeting notes
- Business correspondence
- Personnel-related forms

The U.S. Department of Labor identifies payroll and employment records that covered employers must maintain under the Fair Labor Standards Act, including information concerning hours worked and wages paid. ([dol.gov](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/21-flsa-recordkeeping))

## Why Internal Communications Matter

Internal communications can provide a contemporaneous record of workplace decisions and events. Unlike recollections prepared months or years later, an email or business message may show what participants understood at the time.

For example, communications can help establish:

- When a workplace concern was reported
- Who received the complaint
- What management knew
- When a policy was implemented
- Why an employment decision was made
- Whether employees received particular instructions
- Whether a safety concern was previously raised

The significance of a particular communication depends on context. A single message may be incomplete or misunderstood if separated from the conversation surrounding it.

## Identifying Relevant Communication Channels

Modern workplaces use numerous communication systems. Preservation efforts should therefore consider more than corporate email.

Potential sources include:

- Email accounts
- Internal messaging platforms
- Text messages used for business purposes
- Collaboration applications
- Video-conference records
- Shared drives
- Cloud-storage systems
- HR information systems
- Payroll platforms
- Applicant-tracking systems
- Project-management software
- Personal devices used for authorized business communications

The exact sources depend on how an organization operates.

A preservation process should identify where relevant information is stored before automated deletion, account closure, device replacement, or system migration causes potentially important information to disappear.

## Ordinary Record Retention

Organizations commonly establish retention schedules specifying how long different categories of records should be maintained.

Retention periods can vary according to the type of record and applicable law. For example, the Department of Labor explains that employers covered by the FLSA generally must maintain payroll records for at least three years and certain records supporting wage calculations for at least two years. ([dol.gov](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/21-flsa-recordkeeping))

Other employment laws establish different requirements. Employers should therefore avoid using a single universal retention period for every employment document.

A retention schedule can categorize records according to legal requirements, operational needs, and the organization's risk-management policies.

## Litigation Holds

The preservation analysis changes when litigation is reasonably anticipated.

A litigation hold is a directive to preserve potentially relevant information so that ordinary deletion or destruction does not eliminate evidence. It can suspend routine document-destruction procedures for information potentially relevant to the dispute.

The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure address electronically stored information and impose preservation-related obligations when litigation is anticipated. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) provides a framework for situations in which electronically stored information that should have been preserved is lost because reasonable steps were not taken and cannot be restored or replaced through additional discovery. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_37))

A litigation hold should generally identify the dispute, the categories of information that may be relevant, and the custodians or systems that may contain the information.

## Identifying Custodians

Preservation frequently begins by identifying the people and systems most likely to possess relevant information.

Potential custodians can include:

- The employee involved in the dispute
- Supervisors
- Human resources personnel
- Executives
- Compliance personnel
- Investigators
- Payroll staff
- Information-technology personnel
- Witnesses
- Other employees involved in relevant decisions

Identifying custodians early can reduce the risk that relevant communications will be overlooked.

## Suspending Automatic Deletion

Many electronic systems automatically delete or overwrite information according to predefined schedules. Email archives, messaging platforms, backup systems, mobile devices, and collaboration software can all operate differently.

Once a legal preservation obligation arises, an organization may need to suspend ordinary deletion practices for information within the scope of the obligation.

This does not necessarily mean preserving every document indefinitely. Preservation should be tailored to information that may be relevant to the anticipated or existing dispute and should follow applicable legal requirements.

## Personal Devices and Messaging Applications

Workplace communications increasingly occur on smartphones and other personal devices. Employees may use text messages or messaging applications for legitimate business purposes.

When relevant business communications exist on personal devices, organizations may need to consider how those communications can be preserved while respecting privacy and other legal restrictions.

The appropriate process can depend on employer policies, the jurisdiction, the nature of the information, and whether litigation or another formal proceeding exists.

Employees should also avoid deleting relevant business communications simply because they were created on a personal device.

## Preserving Original Information

When evidence is important, maintaining the original information or an appropriately preserved copy can be critical.

Preservation may involve retaining:

- Original files
- Metadata
- Email headers
- Attachments
- Message threads
- File creation and modification information
- Relevant versions of documents

Changing or rewriting a document can make it more difficult to determine its original content and context.

For organizations involved in litigation, legal and technical professionals may establish procedures for collecting and preserving electronically stored information while maintaining relevant metadata and chain-of-custody information.

## Employment Records and Government Requirements

Employment documentation may be subject to multiple recordkeeping regimes.

For example, federal Equal Employment Opportunity laws establish recordkeeping requirements for certain employers. The EEOC states that covered employers generally must retain personnel or employment records for specified periods and may have additional obligations when a charge has been filed. ([eeoc.gov](https://www.eeoc.gov/employers/recordkeeping-requirements))

Different rules may apply to payroll records, medical information, immigration documentation, safety records, and other categories.

Employers should therefore maintain a recordkeeping program that accounts for the requirements applicable to their workforce and industry.

## Confidentiality and Privacy

Preservation does not eliminate privacy obligations.

Employment records can contain sensitive personal information, including medical information, financial information, identification documents, compensation data, and private communications.

An organization should limit access to preserved information to individuals with a legitimate business or legal need and follow applicable privacy and security requirements.

The ADA, for example, imposes confidentiality requirements concerning medical information obtained from employees or applicants in covered circumstances. ([eeoc.gov](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/job-applicants-and-employees-enforcement-guidance-how-ada-applies))

Preserving information therefore requires balancing evidence-management requirements with confidentiality and data-protection obligations.

## Avoiding Alteration or Destruction

When an employee or employer becomes aware of a potential legal dispute, deleting, altering, or concealing relevant information can create serious consequences.

Employees should generally avoid:

- Deleting relevant emails
- Erasing text messages concerning the dispute
- Altering documents
- Rewriting records to change their apparent meaning
- Destroying notes
- Removing files from shared systems
- Attempting to conceal relevant communications

Employers should likewise ensure that routine document-destruction practices do not continue after a legal preservation obligation arises.

The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure permit courts to impose remedies in appropriate cases involving lost electronically stored information, including measures designed to cure prejudice and, under specified circumstances, stronger sanctions. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_37))

## Organizing Preserved Records

Preservation is more effective when records remain organized and searchable.

Organizations can establish systems that identify:

- Document type
- Date
- Author or sender
- Recipient
- Relevant employee
- Related matter
- Storage location
- Retention period
- Legal hold status

A structured system can make it easier to respond to discovery requests, regulatory inquiries, audits, or internal investigations.

It can also reduce the amount of time required to determine which records are relevant.

## Documenting the Preservation Process

An organization should consider maintaining a record of its preservation efforts.

Documentation may include:

- Date a potential dispute was identified
- Date a litigation hold was issued
- People who received preservation instructions
- Systems placed on hold
- Categories of information preserved
- Follow-up reminders
- Changes to custodians or systems
- Release of the litigation hold

This documentation can help demonstrate that reasonable preservation procedures were implemented.

## Releasing a Litigation Hold

A litigation hold should generally remain in place while the information remains potentially relevant to the legal matter.

Once the underlying obligation ends, the organization can determine whether the ordinary retention schedule should resume.

Releasing a hold should itself be documented so that employees and information-technology personnel understand when ordinary retention practices may restart.

## Practical Preservation Checklist

When a workplace dispute becomes likely, an organization can consider the following steps:

1. **Identify the dispute.**
2. **Determine what information may be relevant.**
3. **Identify relevant custodians.**
4. **Identify email, messaging, cloud, and physical records.**
5. **Suspend applicable automatic deletion practices.**
6. **Issue appropriate preservation instructions.**
7. **Preserve relevant originals and metadata.**
8. **Protect confidential and sensitive information.**
9. **Document the preservation process.**
10. **Review the hold periodically as the dispute develops.**

The specific steps should be adapted to the legal requirements and circumstances of the matter.

## Conclusion

Employment documentation and internal communications can become significant evidence when workplace disputes develop into administrative proceedings, investigations, or litigation. Effective preservation requires organizations and individuals to identify relevant records, understand where those records are stored, and prevent routine deletion from eliminating information that may need to be retained.

The preservation process must also account for legal retention requirements, litigation holds, privacy obligations, electronic evidence, and organizational policies. Different categories of employment records may be governed by different rules, making a comprehensive recordkeeping strategy preferable to a single retention period.

When litigation is reasonably anticipated, preservation becomes particularly important. Employers should identify relevant custodians and systems, communicate preservation obligations clearly, and document their efforts. Employees involved in potential disputes should likewise avoid altering or deleting relevant communications.

A disciplined approach to employment documentation can help preserve an accurate record of workplace events while supporting compliance with applicable legal and privacy requirements.
