Financial hardship can make it difficult for homeowners to maintain mortgage payments, particularly after a loss of income, major unexpected expense, change in household circumstances, or increase in other financial obligations. When a borrower begins experiencing difficulty making payments, acting early can provide more time to understand available loss-mitigation options.

Depending on the circumstances, a mortgage servicer may offer options such as a loan modification, repayment plan, forbearance, short sale, or deed-in-lieu of foreclosure. The appropriate option depends on the borrower's financial situation, the loan, the property, applicable requirements, and the servicer's available programs. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) recommends contacting the mortgage servicer as soon as a borrower anticipates difficulty making payments.

## Understanding Loss Mitigation

Loss mitigation refers broadly to measures designed to reduce the financial consequences of mortgage default and foreclosure. Some options are intended to help borrowers remain in their homes, while others provide a structured way to transition out of homeownership.

Potential options can include:

- Loan modification
- Repayment plans
- Forbearance
- Refinancing, when available
- Short sales
- Deeds-in-lieu of foreclosure

The availability of a particular option depends on the mortgage, investor or insurer requirements, borrower circumstances, and applicable law. HUD's housing-counseling materials identify loan modifications, forbearance agreements, and other loss-mitigation arrangements as retention strategies that may be considered for borrowers experiencing payment difficulties.

## Contacting the Mortgage Servicer Early

One of the most important steps is communicating with the mortgage servicer before the situation becomes more difficult.

The CFPB advises borrowers who are having trouble paying their mortgage to contact the servicer promptly and be prepared to explain why payments have become difficult, whether the problem is temporary or permanent, and information about income, expenses, and available assets.

HUD similarly advises homeowners to contact their lender as soon as they recognize that they may have difficulty making payments.

A borrower should keep records of communications, including:

- Dates and times of calls
- Names or identification numbers of representatives
- Documents submitted
- Written requests
- Responses received
- Payment records
- Deadlines provided by the servicer

Maintaining a complete file can become particularly important if the borrower later needs to clarify what information was submitted or what options were discussed.

## Loan Modification

A loan modification changes one or more terms of an existing mortgage.

According to the CFPB, modifications can involve extending the repayment period, reducing the interest rate, or forbearing or reducing principal in certain circumstances. A modification can reduce the monthly payment, but borrowers should evaluate both the immediate and long-term financial effects of the revised terms.

A modification may be relevant when the borrower has experienced a continuing change in financial circumstances and needs a payment structure that is more sustainable.

Before accepting a proposed modification, borrowers should examine:

- New monthly payment
- Interest rate
- Remaining loan term
- New principal balance
- Amount of deferred or capitalized amounts
- Fees or costs
- Escrow changes
- Total amount payable over time
- Consequences of missing a future payment

A lower monthly payment does not necessarily mean the borrower will pay less overall. Extending the repayment period or adding missed payments to the balance can increase the total amount owed over time.

## Preparing a Modification Request

A servicer may request extensive financial documentation when evaluating a loss-mitigation application.

Depending on the program, requested information may include:

- Recent pay statements
- Bank statements
- Tax returns
- Employment information
- Monthly expenses
- Information about other debts
- Documentation of financial hardship
- Mortgage statements
- Information about household income

Borrowers should provide requested information accurately and keep copies of documents submitted.

HUD's housing-counselor training materials specifically identify the need to understand the forms and requirements associated with mortgage loss-mitigation packages.

## Forbearance and Repayment Plans

A borrower whose financial difficulty is temporary may have different needs from someone whose income has permanently changed.

A repayment plan may allow missed amounts to be repaid over a specified period in addition to regular payments. The Federal Trade Commission describes this type of arrangement as potentially useful when a borrower has missed only a limited number of payments and expects to be able to make higher payments going forward.

Forbearance is another potential option. Depending on the arrangement, payments may be temporarily reduced or suspended, with the missed amounts addressed later.

Borrowers should understand how deferred payments will become due. An arrangement that temporarily reduces financial pressure may still create a substantial future obligation.

## Short Sales

A short sale involves selling a property for less than the amount owed on the mortgage, with the lender or servicer agreeing to accept the transaction under specified conditions.

The CFPB defines a short sale as a sale of a home for less than the outstanding mortgage balance and explains that it can serve as an alternative to foreclosure.

A short sale generally requires the lender or servicer's approval. The borrower should not assume that finding a buyer automatically resolves the mortgage debt.

Important issues can include:

- The property's market value
- Outstanding mortgage balance
- Other liens
- Proposed sale price
- Closing costs
- Servicer approval
- Timing requirements
- Treatment of any remaining deficiency

State law can affect whether a lender can pursue a deficiency after a short sale. The CFPB recommends that borrowers in states where deficiency liability may apply ask the lender to waive the deficiency and obtain that waiver in writing.

## Deed-in-Lieu of Foreclosure

A deed-in-lieu arrangement generally involves voluntarily transferring ownership of the property to the lender in exchange for an agreement addressing the mortgage debt.

HUD identifies deed-in-lieu as a potential disposition option when a borrower cannot maintain the home even with available retention assistance. For FHA-insured mortgages, HUD explains that an eligible borrower may be able to voluntarily transfer the property to HUD in exchange for a release from mortgage obligations under applicable program requirements.

The precise consequences depend on the mortgage and applicable program. Borrowers should obtain written documentation explaining whether the arrangement releases the entire remaining debt and whether other liens or obligations remain.

## Understanding Foreclosure Proceedings

Foreclosure is the legal process through which a lender or other authorized party may enforce its rights against mortgaged property after a borrower defaults.

Procedures vary substantially among jurisdictions. Some states primarily use judicial foreclosure, while others permit nonjudicial procedures under specified circumstances.

Because foreclosure involves deadlines and legal rights, borrowers who have received formal legal notices should review them carefully and consider obtaining advice from a qualified attorney in the relevant jurisdiction.

The CFPB specifically advises homeowners who are facing imminent foreclosure or have been served with legal papers to consider consulting an attorney.

## Comparing Major Options

| Option | General purpose | Home ownership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loan modification | Change mortgage terms to make payments more manageable | Usually retained |
| Repayment plan | Spread missed payments over a defined period | Usually retained |
| Forbearance | Temporarily reduce or suspend payments under an agreement | Usually retained |
| Short sale | Sell property for less than the mortgage balance with lender approval | Lost through sale |
| Deed-in-lieu | Transfer property to lender under an agreed arrangement | Transferred |
| Foreclosure | Enforce mortgage rights through the applicable foreclosure process | Generally lost |

These descriptions are general. Eligibility, documentation, deficiency liability, tax consequences, and credit effects can vary according to the loan, state law, investor requirements, and individual circumstances.

## Considering Professional and Housing Counseling Assistance

Homeowners do not necessarily need to hire a private company to explore foreclosure-prevention options.

HUD maintains a network of participating housing counseling agencies and certified counselors. HUD states that foreclosure-prevention counseling services are available without charge.

A housing counselor may help a borrower organize financial information, understand available options, and prepare for discussions with the mortgage servicer.

An attorney may be particularly relevant when the borrower has received foreclosure papers, disputes the debt, believes servicing errors have occurred, faces a title issue, or needs advice concerning state-specific foreclosure rights.

## Avoiding Mortgage Relief Scams

Financial distress can make homeowners particularly vulnerable to companies promising immediate foreclosure relief.

The CFPB and Federal Trade Commission identify warning signs that can indicate a mortgage-relief scam, including demands for upfront fees, guarantees that a loan will be modified or foreclosure stopped, instructions to stop communicating with the lender, requests to transfer the deed, or demands to send mortgage payments to someone other than the servicer.

The FTC's Mortgage Assistance Relief Services Rule restricts covered providers from collecting fees before providing the required results and disclosures.

Homeowners should be especially cautious when a company claims to have a special government connection or guarantees a particular outcome.

## Creating a Foreclosure-Prevention Action Plan

A structured response can help homeowners keep track of rapidly changing circumstances.

A basic action plan can include:

1. Contact the mortgage servicer immediately.
2. Determine whether the financial problem is temporary or ongoing.
3. Gather income, expense, debt, and asset information.
4. Request information about available loss-mitigation options.
5. Review the requirements and deadlines for each option.
6. Consult a HUD-approved housing counselor when appropriate.
7. Obtain legal advice if foreclosure litigation or significant legal questions arise.
8. Keep copies of every document submitted or received.
9. Avoid companies demanding prohibited upfront fees or making guaranteed promises.
10. Obtain important agreements, including deficiency waivers or releases, in writing.

## Conclusion

Loan modification, short sale, forbearance, repayment arrangements, deed-in-lieu transactions, and foreclosure are different mechanisms with different consequences. The appropriate path depends on the borrower's financial circumstances, the mortgage terms, the property, applicable state law, and the requirements of the relevant lender, investor, or insurer.

Early communication is important because available options can become more limited as a mortgage moves further into default.

Homeowners facing financial hardship should therefore focus on understanding their available options, meeting documented deadlines, preserving records, and obtaining assistance from legitimate housing counselors or qualified legal professionals when appropriate.
