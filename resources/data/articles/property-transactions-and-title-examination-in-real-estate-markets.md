Real estate transactions involve more than negotiating a purchase price and signing a sales agreement. Buyers, sellers, lenders, investors, and developers must also consider ownership rights, liens, easements, restrictions, taxes, mortgages, recording requirements, and other interests that may affect a property's legal status.

Title examination is one of the principal processes used to investigate these issues. A title examination reviews ownership records and other documents affecting real property to determine whether the seller can convey the expected interest and whether outstanding claims or restrictions require attention before closing. The American Bar Association describes title examination as a study of records concerning a property's ownership history and other matters affecting ownership interests.

Because property and recording laws vary by jurisdiction, the precise procedure for examining and transferring title differs between real estate markets.

## Understanding Property Title

Title refers to the legal rights and interests associated with ownership of property. Those rights can be affected by mortgages, easements, liens, leases, restrictive covenants, judgments, tax claims, and other recorded or legally recognized interests.

A buyer may therefore need to determine more than whether the seller's name appears on a recent deed. The relevant question is whether the seller has the legal authority to convey the intended interest and whether other claims could affect the buyer's ownership.

The scope of a title examination depends on the transaction and jurisdiction. Some examinations may focus on a specific issue, while a comprehensive examination can investigate a property's historical chain of ownership and multiple categories of potential encumbrances.

## Examining the Chain of Title

The chain of title is the sequence of documents showing successive transfers of an interest in the property.

A title examiner generally traces ownership backward through recorded instruments and then examines the relevant documents affecting the identified owners and property. The American Bar Association notes that the examination can involve identifying a "root of title" and following the chain of recorded transactions forward through the relevant period.

The exact period that must be examined depends on local law and the purpose of the search. Some jurisdictions impose statutory requirements concerning marketable title or establish particular periods for examining historical records.

A break in the chain can require additional investigation. For example, an apparently missing conveyance, inconsistent legal description, unresolved estate, or improperly recorded transfer can create questions about whether ownership was transferred correctly.

## Searching Public Records

Title examination commonly involves reviewing public records maintained by recording offices and other governmental authorities.

Depending on the jurisdiction and transaction, relevant records can include:

- Deeds
- Mortgages
- Releases and satisfactions
- Easements
- Restrictive covenants
- Leases
- Tax records
- Judgment liens
- Court records
- Bankruptcy records
- Probate records
- Other recorded instruments affecting the property

Federal regulations concerning certain real estate developments illustrate the breadth of a title search by identifying recorder-of-deeds records, tax liens, court records, bankruptcy proceedings, and other public records as potential sources of information affecting title.

The precise sources required in an ordinary transaction depend on applicable local law rather than this particular federal regulation.

## Identifying Liens and Encumbrances

A property can be subject to interests that do not necessarily prevent a sale but can affect the buyer's rights or the property's value.

A **lien** is a legal claim that can secure payment of an obligation. Mortgages are a common example, but tax liens, judgment liens, and other statutory or contractual claims may also affect property.

An **easement** can give another person or entity a right to use part of the property for a particular purpose. Utility easements, access rights, and shared-driveway arrangements are common examples.

Other restrictions can limit development, construction, use, or alteration of the property.

The title examination should identify relevant interests so that the parties can determine whether they should be released, satisfied, modified, accepted, or treated as exceptions to the title being conveyed.

## Reviewing the Legal Description

The property's legal description is another important component of a transaction.

A deed may identify property using a metes-and-bounds description, lot and block information, a subdivision reference, or another legally recognized method. The description should correspond with the property being transferred and with the applicable recorded documents.

Errors in legal descriptions can create uncertainty about the boundaries or extent of the interest being conveyed. Survey information may be necessary when questions arise concerning boundaries, encroachments, easements, or physical improvements.

A title examination and a property survey serve different purposes. The title examination primarily investigates legal records and interests, while a survey can provide information about physical boundaries and improvements.

## Reviewing Taxes and Assessments

Property taxes and other assessments can affect a transaction and may need to be addressed before or at closing.

A title review may therefore include examination of current tax status and relevant payment history. The American Bar Association notes that current tax records and recent payment history can be important components of a comprehensive title examination.

The parties should determine which taxes, assessments, association charges, or other property-related obligations must be paid, prorated, transferred, or otherwise addressed under the purchase agreement and applicable law.

## Title Commitments and Title Insurance

In jurisdictions where title insurance is commonly used, a title company may conduct a search and issue a title commitment identifying the proposed coverage, requirements, and exceptions.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes title services as including title searches, examinations, preparation and issuance of title commitments, clearance of underwriting objections, and issuance of title insurance policies.

Title insurance generally protects the insured against specified covered losses associated with title defects or claims, subject to the policy's terms, exclusions, exceptions, and conditions. The American Bar Association explains that an owner's title insurance policy can protect against financial loss related to title and the cost of defending the insured's title in court.

A title insurance policy is therefore not identical to a title examination. The examination investigates the records, while the insurance policy provides contractual protection against specified risks.

## Resolving Title Defects Before Closing

A title examination can identify issues that need to be resolved before a transaction can proceed.

Examples include:

- An unreleased mortgage
- An outstanding judgment
- An unpaid tax claim
- An incorrect legal description
- A missing conveyance
- An unresolved estate
- An improperly executed deed
- An unidentified easement
- A conflicting ownership claim

The appropriate solution depends on the defect. A mortgage may need to be paid and released, while an estate-related issue may require probate documentation. A defective deed may require corrective documentation or another legally valid conveyance.

The purchase agreement can also determine which party is responsible for resolving particular title objections and whether the buyer has a right to terminate the transaction if an acceptable title cannot be delivered.

## Recording the Transaction

Closing does not necessarily complete every legal step associated with a real estate transfer. Recording the deed and other documents can be an important part of establishing public notice and protecting the parties' interests under applicable recording laws.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that, in a typical U.S. mortgage closing, the seller signs the deed transferring ownership and the settlement agent or closing attorney prepares supporting documents and records them with the appropriate land-records office.

Recording systems differ significantly among jurisdictions. The legal effect of recording, priority between competing interests, and applicable notice rules are matters of local law.

## Closing Costs and Title-Related Services

Real estate transactions can involve several title-related expenses. These may include title searches, title examinations, title insurance, document preparation, recording fees, settlement services, and attorney fees.

Federal mortgage disclosure rules recognize title services and recording fees as categories of real estate transaction costs.

Buyers should distinguish between the cost of examining title, the cost of obtaining title insurance, and other closing expenses. The precise allocation of these costs between buyer and seller depends on the contract, lender requirements, local customs, and applicable law.

## Digital Records and Title Research

Many land records are now accessible through online databases. Digital access can make title research faster, but it does not eliminate the possibility of incomplete or inaccurate searches.

The American Bar Association notes that online records can contain indexing problems, transcription errors, missing older records, or variations in the way names and documents are entered into databases.

A comprehensive search may therefore require variations in names, review of original documents, examination of multiple indices, or consultation of additional databases. Historical records may also require different research techniques from modern electronically recorded documents.

## Commercial and Investment Property Transactions

Commercial transactions can require particularly extensive title analysis because the property may be subject to leases, development agreements, easements, environmental restrictions, financing arrangements, rights of way, and other interests.

Investors and developers may also need to examine zoning, land-use restrictions, development rights, access, utilities, and other matters that are not necessarily resolved through a basic ownership search.

A title examination can consequently form only one component of broader real estate due diligence.

## Preparing for a Real Estate Closing

A structured closing process can help the parties identify unresolved issues before the transaction becomes final.

A buyer can generally expect the transaction team to coordinate several categories of information, including:

1. Purchase agreement requirements.
2. Title examination or title commitment.
3. Financing documents, if applicable.
4. Survey and property information.
5. Required releases or payoff documents.
6. Taxes and assessments.
7. Closing statements and disclosures.
8. Deed and other transfer documents.
9. Recording requirements.
10. Delivery of funds and possession.

The exact sequence varies according to the jurisdiction and transaction structure.

## Conclusion

Property transactions depend on more than the agreement between buyer and seller. Establishing the status of title, identifying liens and other encumbrances, verifying the chain of ownership, addressing tax and recording issues, and resolving title defects can all affect whether a transaction can proceed as planned.

A title examination provides a structured review of the records affecting ownership, while title insurance can provide contractual protection against specified covered title risks.

Because recording systems, title standards, property laws, and closing practices differ among jurisdictions, buyers and sellers should evaluate title issues under the law applicable to the property. For significant transactions, qualified real estate counsel and appropriate title professionals can help identify and address issues before closing.
