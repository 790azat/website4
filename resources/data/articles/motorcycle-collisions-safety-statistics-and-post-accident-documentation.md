Motorcycle collisions can create significant safety, insurance, and legal issues. Because motorcycles provide less physical protection than passenger vehicles, a crash can result in serious injuries even when the motorcycle itself appears to have relatively limited damage.

Understanding motorcycle safety statistics can provide context, but documenting what actually happened after a collision is equally important. Police reports, photographs, medical records, witness information, vehicle damage, and other evidence can help establish the circumstances of an accident and support an insurance or legal claim.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) reported **6,228 motorcyclists killed in traffic crashes in 2024**, representing 16% of all traffic fatalities that year. NHTSA also reported that, per vehicle miles traveled, motorcyclists were almost 27 times more likely than passenger-car occupants to die in a motor-vehicle crash in 2024.

## Why Motorcycle Collisions Can Be Particularly Serious

Motorcycles generally provide less structural protection than passenger vehicles.

A person riding a motorcycle may be directly exposed to:

- The road surface
- Other vehicles
- Fixed objects
- Debris
- Weather conditions
- The impact forces of a collision

In a passenger vehicle, occupants may have the benefit of a vehicle structure, airbags, seat belts, and other safety systems. Motorcyclists do not have the same type of occupant compartment.

NHTSA's 2024 data illustrates the difference in risk. The agency reported that motorcyclists were almost five times more likely to be injured in a motor-vehicle crash than passenger-car occupants when measured by vehicle miles traveled.

## Motorcycle Safety Statistics

Safety statistics can help explain why motorcycle collisions receive particular attention from transportation-safety agencies.

According to NHTSA:

- 6,228 motorcyclists were killed in traffic crashes in 2024.
- Motorcyclists represented 16% of all traffic fatalities in 2024.
- Motorcyclists were almost 27 times more likely than passenger-car occupants to die in a crash per vehicle mile traveled.
- Motorcyclists were almost five times more likely to be injured per vehicle mile traveled.

NHTSA also reported that **5,950 people operating motorcycles were killed in traffic crashes in 2024**, and 1,529 of those riders had a blood alcohol concentration of at least 0.08 g/dL.

These figures describe national traffic-safety patterns. They do not determine who was responsible for any individual motorcycle collision.

## Helmet Use and Motorcycle Safety

Helmet use is another important part of motorcycle safety.

NHTSA recommends helmets that meet the U.S. Department of Transportation's Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 218 (FMVSS 218). The agency reported that known helmet use among motorcyclists killed in 2023 differed substantially between states with universal helmet laws and states without them.

Helmet requirements themselves vary by state.

For this reason, a motorcycle rider should check the requirements applicable to the state where they ride rather than assuming that one nationwide helmet rule applies everywhere.

Helmet use can also become relevant when an injury claim is evaluated. Whether helmet use affects a particular claim, and how it may affect damages, depends on applicable state law and the circumstances of the accident.

## What to Do Immediately After a Motorcycle Collision

If you are involved in a motorcycle collision, safety should come first.

When possible:

1. Move away from immediate traffic hazards.
2. Call 911 if anyone may be injured.
3. Request appropriate medical assistance.
4. Notify law enforcement when required or appropriate.
5. Exchange information with other involved drivers.
6. Identify witnesses.
7. Photograph the scene if it is safe to do so.
8. Preserve relevant evidence.
9. Notify the appropriate insurance company.
10. Keep records of medical treatment and expenses.

Do not put yourself in danger to obtain photographs or evidence.

## Call Emergency Services When Someone Is Injured

Motorcycle injuries are not always obvious immediately after a crash.

A rider may experience:

- Head injuries
- Broken bones
- Road rash
- Internal injuries
- Spinal injuries
- Joint injuries
- Soft-tissue injuries

A person involved in a collision should seek appropriate medical evaluation when injury is suspected.

Medical records can later provide documentation of the symptoms reported, treatment received, diagnostic testing, and medical expenses.

## Photograph the Motorcycle and Accident Scene

Photographs can provide useful evidence of the physical circumstances of a collision.

If it is safe, photograph:

- Motorcycle damage
- Damage to other vehicles
- The roadway
- Traffic signals
- Lane markings
- Debris
- Skid or tire marks
- Road conditions
- Nearby signs
- Visible injuries
- Protective equipment
- The surrounding intersection or roadway

Take photographs from different distances and angles.

Avoid moving evidence unnecessarily before law enforcement documents the scene unless moving it is necessary for safety.

## Document the Motorcycle's Condition

A motorcycle can sustain damage that is not immediately obvious.

After a collision, document:

- Fairings
- Handlebars
- Wheels
- Forks
- Brakes
- Exhaust components
- Foot controls
- Frame areas
- Mirrors
- Lighting
- Engine or transmission components

A qualified repair professional may identify additional damage during an inspection.

Do not assume that a motorcycle is safe to ride simply because it starts and appears to operate normally.

## Preserve Protective Equipment

Do not automatically throw away damaged safety equipment after a crash.

Potentially relevant items can include:

- Helmet
- Jacket
- Gloves
- Boots
- Protective pants
- Other riding equipment

Photograph damaged equipment and preserve it in its post-accident condition when possible.

A damaged helmet can be particularly important evidence concerning the nature of an impact.

## Collect Information From Other Drivers

Obtain the information required or appropriate under local law from other involved drivers.

This may include:

- Name
- Contact information
- Driver's license information
- License plate number
- Vehicle information
- Insurance company
- Policy information

Do not rely solely on a photograph of the other driver's insurance card if additional information is needed.

Make sure the information is legible and complete.

## Identify Witnesses

Independent witnesses can provide valuable information about how a motorcycle collision occurred.

A witness may have observed:

- Which vehicle entered an intersection first
- Whether a traffic signal was red or green
- A vehicle changing lanes
- A driver failing to yield
- The motorcycle's position
- The direction of travel
- What happened immediately before impact

Record witness names and contact information when possible.

Do not pressure witnesses to adopt a particular version of events.

## Obtain the Police Report

If law enforcement responds to the collision, ask how and when the accident report can be obtained.

A report may contain information such as:

- Driver identities
- Vehicle information
- Witnesses
- Statements
- Officer observations
- Road conditions
- Citations
- Injury information

Police reports can be useful evidence, but they are not necessarily the only evidence considered by an insurer or court.

NHTSA's national Fatality Analysis Reporting System illustrates how crash information can be compiled from multiple sources, including police crash reports, vehicle records, driver records, death certificates, toxicology reports, and emergency medical-service records.

## Document Your Injuries

Keep a record of medical treatment after the accident.

Depending on the circumstances, documentation can include:

- Emergency-room records
- Diagnostic imaging
- Physician records
- Prescription information
- Physical therapy
- Rehabilitation
- Medical bills
- Work restrictions
- Follow-up appointments

It can also be useful to maintain a simple personal record of symptoms and how the injuries affect ordinary activities.

This does not replace medical documentation, but it can help preserve details that might otherwise be forgotten.

## Keep Track of Financial Losses

A motorcycle collision can produce expenses beyond medical treatment.

Depending on the circumstances, these may include:

- Motorcycle repairs
- Towing
- Storage
- Rental transportation
- Medical expenses
- Prescription costs
- Lost wages
- Other accident-related expenses

Keep receipts and other supporting documents.

Whether a particular expense is recoverable depends on the applicable insurance policy and law.

## Be Careful When Discussing Fault

After a collision, people may be asked what happened by police officers, insurers, witnesses, or other parties.

Describe what you personally observed.

Avoid guessing about:

- The other driver's speed
- The exact distance between vehicles
- Whether another driver was distracted
- Whether a driver was impaired
- The precise cause of an impact you did not personally observe

If you do not remember something, it is better to say that you do not remember than to speculate.

## Notify Your Insurance Company

The applicable insurance policy may contain requirements concerning prompt notice of an accident.

When reporting the collision, provide accurate information about:

- Date and location
- Vehicles involved
- Injuries
- Police response
- Damage
- Other insurance information

Keep copies of communications with the insurer.

The coverage available after a motorcycle collision depends on the policy and applicable state law. Potentially relevant coverage can include liability, collision, comprehensive, uninsured-motorist, underinsured-motorist, and medical-payments coverage, depending on the circumstances.

## Preserve Digital Evidence

Modern collisions can generate evidence beyond photographs and paper documents.

Potential sources include:

- Motorcycle cameras
- Helmet cameras
- Dashcams from other vehicles
- Mobile-phone recordings
- Traffic cameras
- Business surveillance systems
- GPS information

If you have video footage, preserve the original file.

Avoid editing or overwriting the original recording.

If you know that a nearby business or residence has surveillance footage, tell law enforcement or your attorney as soon as possible because some systems automatically overwrite older recordings.

## Motorcycle Collision Claims and Liability

A motorcycle collision claim may involve questions about negligence and responsibility.

Depending on the circumstances, investigators may examine:

- Traffic signals
- Right of way
- Speed
- Lane position
- Turning movements
- Following distance
- Visibility
- Road conditions
- Driver behavior
- Motorcycle operation

State laws concerning comparative negligence and other liability rules vary.

A motorcycle rider being injured does not automatically establish that another driver was legally responsible. Likewise, the fact that a motorcycle was involved does not by itself establish rider fault.

The evidence must be evaluated under the law applicable to the collision.

## What If the Other Driver Was Uninsured?

A motorcycle rider may face additional complications if the driver who caused the collision has no insurance or insufficient insurance.

Depending on the policy and state law, uninsured-motorist or underinsured-motorist coverage may provide protection in some circumstances.

The exact requirements can differ significantly between states and policies.

Review the applicable motorcycle insurance policy rather than assuming that coverage available for a passenger vehicle automatically applies in the same way to a motorcycle.

## When Legal Advice May Be Useful

A legal consultation may be appropriate when a motorcycle collision involves:

- Serious or permanent injuries
- A fatality
- Disputed fault
- Multiple vehicles
- An uninsured or underinsured driver
- Significant medical expenses
- Substantial lost income
- A disputed insurance claim
- A potentially defective motorcycle component
- A government entity or roadway issue
- A legal filing deadline

An attorney can evaluate the evidence and explain the legal options available under the law applicable to the accident.

## Final Takeaway

Motorcycle collisions can involve significant safety risks and complicated insurance and liability questions. NHTSA reported 6,228 motorcyclist fatalities in 2024, representing 16% of all traffic fatalities that year.

After a collision, careful documentation can be important. Photographs, police reports, witness information, medical records, damaged protective equipment, repair records, video footage, and financial documents can all help preserve information about what happened.

The most important priority immediately after a crash is safety and appropriate medical care. Once immediate risks have been addressed, preserving reliable evidence and maintaining organized records can make it easier for insurers, investigators, attorneys, or courts to evaluate the incident.

Because motorcycle insurance requirements, helmet laws, comparative-fault rules, and claim procedures vary by state, the legal consequences of a particular collision should be evaluated according to the law applicable to where the accident occurred.
