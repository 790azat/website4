Hiring decisions are generally expected to focus on qualifications, experience, job-related requirements, and legitimate organizational needs. Employment discrimination law also recognizes that discrimination can occur through both intentional decisions and apparently neutral policies that disproportionately disadvantage certain protected groups.

Investigating hiring practices for disparate impact therefore requires more than examining whether a decision-maker intended to discriminate. It can involve reviewing recruitment sources, application requirements, screening tools, interviews, assessments, selection rates, and the relationship between a hiring criterion and the actual requirements of the position.

In the United States, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) identifies race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, disability, and genetic information among characteristics protected under federal employment-discrimination laws, although the specific statutory coverage and standards differ by protected characteristic.

## Protected Characteristics in Employment

Federal employment law protects applicants and employees from discrimination based on several characteristics. Under the laws enforced by the EEOC, these include race, color, religion, sex—including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and transgender status—national origin, age 40 or older, disability, and genetic information.

Other federal statutes can establish additional protections or requirements in particular circumstances. State and local laws may also provide broader protections than federal law.

For hiring investigations, identifying which legal protections apply is an important first step. A policy may raise one type of legal issue when it affects applicants based on race or national origin and a different issue when it concerns disability or age.

## Disparate Treatment Versus Disparate Impact

Two concepts are particularly important when analyzing discriminatory hiring practices: **disparate treatment** and **disparate impact**.

Disparate treatment generally concerns differential treatment because of a protected characteristic. For example, an employer might intentionally reject an otherwise qualified applicant because of race or religion.

Disparate impact is different. It can arise when an apparently neutral employment policy or selection practice disproportionately excludes members of a protected group, even without evidence that the employer intended to discriminate. The EEOC describes disparate impact as focusing on policies that may be neutral in form but discriminatory in operation.

The distinction is significant because an investigation into disparate impact examines the operation and consequences of a policy rather than relying exclusively on evidence of discriminatory motive.

## Hiring Practices That May Require Review

A hiring investigation can examine virtually every stage of the selection process.

Potential areas include:

- Job advertisements
- Recruitment sources
- Application requirements
- Educational requirements
- Experience requirements
- Employment tests
- Skills assessments
- Background checks
- Automated screening systems
- Interview procedures
- Candidate scoring systems
- Referral programs
- Final selection criteria

The EEOC explains that Title VII's disparate-impact framework can apply to recruitment practices, hiring criteria, education and experience requirements, and other employment-selection practices.

A policy does not become unlawful merely because it produces different selection rates. The legal analysis generally requires examining whether the disparity is legally significant and whether the employer can establish the required justification under the applicable statute.

## Measuring Selection Rates

One of the practical foundations of a disparate-impact investigation is comparing selection outcomes among relevant applicant groups.

For example, an employer might examine:

1. The number of applicants from each relevant group.
2. The number who passed an initial screening stage.
3. The number invited to interviews.
4. The number receiving offers.
5. The number ultimately hired.

Comparing these stages can help identify where a disparity emerges.

The EEOC's Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures define adverse impact in terms of substantially different selection rates that disadvantage members of a race, sex, or ethnic group. The Guidelines provide statistical approaches for evaluating selection procedures.

Statistical differences do not automatically establish unlawful discrimination. Sample size, applicant pools, job categories, geographic markets, and other factors can affect the reliability and interpretation of the results.

## Examining Recruitment Sources

Disparate impact can arise before an applicant reaches the formal selection stage.

Recruitment practices may influence who becomes aware of an opening and who applies. If an employer relies heavily on a recruitment source that disproportionately reaches one demographic group, investigators may examine whether the practice unnecessarily limits access to employment opportunities.

The EEOC specifically notes that recruitment methods can violate Title VII when they disproportionately limit employment opportunities based on race and are not related to job requirements or business needs.

This makes recruitment data an important part of a broader hiring review.

## Reviewing Job Requirements

Educational, experience, licensing, testing, and other requirements can affect applicant pools substantially.

An investigation should therefore ask whether each requirement is genuinely connected to the work.

Questions can include:

- What job function does the requirement measure?
- Was the requirement established before the hiring process?
- Is there evidence that it predicts successful job performance?
- Are equivalent qualifications recognized?
- Could the requirement unnecessarily exclude qualified applicants?
- Is a less discriminatory alternative available?

Title VII's disparate-impact provisions require a challenged employment practice to be job-related and consistent with business necessity when the statutory framework applies. The statute also addresses alternative employment practices that would serve the employer's needs with less discriminatory impact.

## Employment Tests and Assessments

Pre-employment tests can provide useful information about candidates, but they can also produce disparities.

An employer using an assessment should understand what the test measures and whether the measured characteristic is actually related to successful performance in the position.

The EEOC states that employment tests must be necessary and related to the job and that employers may not use selection procedures in ways that unlawfully exclude protected groups.

A review may therefore examine validation materials, scoring criteria, testing conditions, accommodation procedures, and selection outcomes.

## Disability and the Hiring Process

Disability discrimination raises additional considerations because employers may have obligations concerning reasonable accommodation during the application process.

The Americans with Disabilities Act prohibits covered entities from discriminating against qualified individuals with disabilities in job application procedures and hiring. It also addresses practices that limit or classify applicants in ways that adversely affect employment opportunities because of disability.

An investigation should therefore distinguish between a legitimate qualification requirement and an unnecessary barrier created by the application or selection process.

For example, if an applicant cannot complete a particular assessment because of a disability, the organization may need to consider whether a reasonable accommodation would allow the applicant to demonstrate the relevant qualification.

## Background Screening and Disparate Impact

Criminal and other background checks can also be examined for disparate impact.

The EEOC states that employers may generally conduct background checks, subject to applicable restrictions, but cannot use background information in a way that unlawfully denies equal employment opportunity through intentional discrimination or unlawful disparate impact.

The issue is not necessarily whether a background check produces different outcomes among groups. Investigators may need to determine whether the screening criterion is job-related, necessary for the position, and consistent with the applicable legal requirements.

## Automated Hiring and Algorithmic Screening

Modern employers increasingly use software to rank applications, identify candidates, administer assessments, or recommend applicants for interviews.

Automation does not remove existing employment-discrimination obligations. A selection algorithm can reproduce disparities present in its underlying data or create new disparities through the variables and assumptions incorporated into its design.

An investigation may therefore examine:

- What data the system uses
- Which variables influence candidate rankings
- How the system was validated
- Whether selection rates differ among groups
- How often humans override automated recommendations
- Whether reasonable accommodations are available
- Whether the employer monitors the system for discriminatory effects

The EEOC's current enforcement materials emphasize that hiring and other employment decisions remain subject to federal anti-discrimination laws, including when organizations use structured programs or decision-making systems.

## Business Necessity and Less Discriminatory Alternatives

When a neutral practice creates a disparate impact under Title VII, the employer may have an opportunity to demonstrate that the practice is job-related and consistent with business necessity. The statute also provides a framework for considering alternative employment practices that would meet the employer's needs with less discriminatory impact.

This makes the investigation more detailed than simply asking whether a disparity exists.

For example, suppose an employer requires a particular credential and the requirement disproportionately excludes a protected group. The analysis may examine whether the credential actually predicts performance, whether it is necessary for the position, and whether another qualification could measure the same capability with less exclusionary effect.

## Documenting the Investigation

A well-organized review should preserve the evidence used to evaluate the hiring process.

Relevant records may include:

- Job descriptions
- Job advertisements
- Recruitment data
- Applicant demographics where lawfully collected
- Screening criteria
- Test results
- Interview records
- Candidate scoring sheets
- Hiring decisions
- Internal communications
- Validation studies
- Background-screening policies
- Automated-system documentation
- Accommodation records

Investigators should also identify the relevant time period and applicant population. Combining unrelated job categories or substantially different hiring periods can produce misleading comparisons.

## Corrective Measures

If an investigation identifies an unnecessary barrier or unexplained disparity, an employer may consider reviewing the underlying practice.

Possible measures include:

- Revising job requirements
- Validating or replacing selection tests
- Expanding recruitment channels
- Standardizing interviews
- Improving accommodation procedures
- Reviewing automated screening systems
- Training hiring personnel
- Monitoring selection rates
- Establishing periodic compliance reviews

Corrective action should be based on the evidence and applicable legal requirements rather than assumptions about the cause of a disparity.

## Conclusion

Investigating protected characteristics and disparate impact in hiring requires an examination of both individual decisions and broader employment systems. A hiring practice can create legal concerns even when it is written in neutral terms if it disproportionately excludes a protected group and does not satisfy the applicable statutory requirements.

Effective review therefore combines legal analysis, applicant-flow data, selection-rate comparisons, examination of job requirements, assessment validation, recruitment analysis, and documentation of decision-making.

The central question is not simply whether different groups experience different hiring outcomes. Investigators must determine whether a particular employment practice caused the disparity, whether the relevant law applies, whether the practice is job-related and justified under that law, and whether a lawful alternative could achieve the employer's legitimate objectives with less discriminatory impact.
