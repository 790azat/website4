Commercial disputes can arise from unpaid invoices, contract breaches, partnership disagreements, failed transactions, intellectual-property issues, construction projects, employment relationships, or allegations that one party failed to perform its contractual obligations. When informal negotiations do not resolve the disagreement, businesses may turn to structured dispute-resolution procedures.

Two important mechanisms are **arbitration and litigation**. Arbitration generally places the dispute before a private arbitrator or panel under an agreed set of rules, while litigation proceeds through a court and is governed by applicable procedural and evidentiary rules. The appropriate process depends on the contract, applicable law, nature of the dispute, available remedies, and the circumstances of the parties.

## Reviewing the Contract Before Choosing a Forum

The first step in many commercial disputes is determining whether the parties previously agreed how disputes would be handled.

Commercial contracts may contain provisions requiring arbitration, selecting a particular arbitration organization, identifying a governing law, establishing a venue, or requiring mediation before litigation or arbitration. The American Arbitration Association (AAA), for example, publishes model commercial arbitration clauses that provide for arbitration administered under its Commercial Arbitration Rules.

A dispute-resolution clause can therefore influence the available forum before the underlying merits of a dispute are even considered.

Businesses should review provisions concerning:

- Arbitration or mediation requirements
- Governing law
- Venue and jurisdiction
- Notice requirements
- Contractual limitations periods
- Attorney-fee provisions
- Available remedies
- Confidentiality obligations
- Choice of arbitration rules
- Requirements for preliminary negotiation or mediation

The precise effect of these provisions depends on the contract and applicable law.

## When Arbitration Becomes Relevant

Arbitration is a private dispute-resolution process in which an arbitrator or panel considers the parties' claims and defenses and ultimately issues a decision.

For U.S. commercial disputes, the Federal Arbitration Act provides an important federal framework for enforcing qualifying arbitration agreements. The Supreme Court has described the Act as establishing a federal policy favoring enforcement of arbitration agreements covered by the statute.

Commercial arbitration may be administered by an organization such as the AAA, or it may proceed under rules and procedures selected by the parties without institutional administration.

Under the AAA's current commercial procedures, a case generally begins with a Demand for Arbitration and the required filing materials. The responding party is given an opportunity to respond, after which the arbitrator or arbitrators are selected and a preliminary hearing establishes the framework and schedule for the proceeding.

## Building the Arbitration Record

Although arbitration can be more streamlined than traditional litigation, parties still need to establish their positions with evidence.

Depending on the applicable rules and the arbitrator's case-management decisions, the process may involve:

- Statements of claims and defenses
- Contract documents
- Financial records
- Correspondence
- Witness statements
- Expert evidence
- Document requests
- Depositions or subpoenas where permitted
- Preliminary hearings
- Evidentiary hearings
- Written submissions

The AAA notes that information exchange in commercial arbitration is generally more limited and streamlined than discovery in litigation.

The parties should nevertheless identify and preserve relevant records as soon as a dispute becomes reasonably foreseeable.

## The Role of the Arbitrator

The arbitrator functions as the decision-maker rather than a judge or jury in a traditional court proceeding.

Depending on the applicable rules and agreement, the arbitrator may manage procedural issues, determine the admissibility or relevance of evidence, establish deadlines, conduct hearings, and issue the final award.

The AAA's commercial rules provide procedures covering filing, arbitrator appointment, information exchange, hearings, and issuance of an award.

The parties' arbitration agreement and selected rules therefore deserve careful review before proceedings begin.

## When Litigation May Be Necessary

Litigation involves presenting a dispute to a court with jurisdiction over the matter. It may become relevant when there is no enforceable arbitration agreement, when the contract permits court proceedings, or when judicial involvement is necessary for a particular remedy or procedural issue.

Federal civil litigation is governed by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure when the case proceeds in a U.S. district court. The federal judiciary explains that these rules govern civil proceedings in U.S. district courts and are designed to facilitate the just, speedy, and inexpensive determination of actions.

A commercial lawsuit may involve:

1. Filing a complaint
2. Service of process
3. An answer or other response
4. Preliminary motions
5. Discovery
6. Expert disclosures where applicable
7. Settlement discussions
8. Pretrial proceedings
9. Trial
10. Judgment
11. Potential post-trial proceedings or appeals

State court procedures differ and may impose additional or different requirements.

## Discovery and Evidence in Litigation

Discovery is often a major component of commercial litigation. Parties may be required to exchange information relevant to the claims and defenses under the applicable procedural rules.

Depending on the case, discovery may include interrogatories, requests for production, requests for admission, depositions, subpoenas, and electronically stored information.

Commercial cases can involve substantial quantities of business records, including emails, accounting records, contracts, internal communications, transaction histories, customer records, and electronic files.

Early preservation of potentially relevant information is therefore important. Destruction or alteration of relevant evidence can create procedural and evidentiary consequences.

## Comparing Arbitration and Litigation

Arbitration and litigation have different procedural characteristics.

| Issue | Arbitration | Litigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision-maker | Arbitrator or panel | Judge, and potentially jury |
| Forum | Private proceeding | Public court system |
| Procedure | Governed by agreement and applicable arbitration rules | Governed by court rules and applicable law |
| Information exchange | Often more limited | Can involve extensive formal discovery |
| Appeals/review | Generally limited | Broader appellate procedures may be available |
| Costs | Filing and arbitrator fees may apply | Court and litigation costs may apply |
| Confidentiality | May depend on agreement and applicable rules | Court filings are generally subject to public-access rules, subject to exceptions |

Neither process is universally applicable to every commercial dispute. The contract, jurisdiction, monetary value, complexity, evidence, desired remedies, and applicable procedural rules all matter.

## Settlement Can Occur During Either Process

Beginning arbitration or litigation does not necessarily mean the parties must proceed through a final hearing or trial.

Commercial disputes may settle at different stages. Parties can negotiate directly, use mediation, participate in judicial settlement procedures, or negotiate through counsel while the formal proceeding continues.

The AAA expressly permits settlement discussions during arbitration and states that parties may attempt to resolve a dispute before an award is issued.

A settlement agreement should clearly identify the obligations being resolved, payment terms, releases, confidentiality provisions where applicable, and procedures for handling any remaining obligations.

## Enforcing an Arbitration Award or Judgment

Obtaining a decision is not always the final practical step.

If an arbitration award is not voluntarily satisfied, the prevailing party may need to seek judicial enforcement. The AAA explains that arbitration awards are generally final and binding and may be enforced through courts under applicable federal or state law, subject to limited grounds for vacating an award.

Court judgments similarly may require enforcement procedures when the losing party does not voluntarily comply.

The availability and procedure for enforcement can depend on the location of assets, applicable law, and whether the decision must be recognized in another jurisdiction.

## Organizing a Commercial Dispute Before Proceedings Begin

Businesses can improve their ability to address disputes by organizing records and identifying the legal basis for their position before filing a claim or responding to one.

Important preparation may include:

- Reviewing the complete contract
- Identifying amendments and related agreements
- Preserving electronic communications
- Calculating claimed losses
- Identifying relevant witnesses
- Collecting financial documentation
- Reviewing applicable dispute-resolution clauses
- Identifying deadlines
- Evaluating possible settlement options
- Determining whether expert evidence may be required

A company should also consider whether continuing a commercial relationship has value. A dispute involving a long-term supplier, customer, business partner, or joint venture may require a strategy that addresses both the immediate disagreement and the parties' broader commercial relationship.

## Obtaining Professional Guidance

Commercial disputes can combine contract interpretation, procedural rules, financial evidence, regulatory requirements, and business considerations. Professional legal guidance can be particularly important when the dispute involves substantial financial exposure, complex contractual provisions, multiple jurisdictions, intellectual property, corporate ownership, or significant business relationships.

The earlier the dispute is evaluated, the more opportunities there may be to preserve evidence, understand contractual obligations, assess available forums, and determine whether negotiation, mediation, arbitration, or litigation is appropriate under the circumstances.

Structured dispute resolution is ultimately about more than choosing between an arbitrator and a courtroom. A sound process begins with understanding the parties' agreement, preserving evidence, identifying the legal and commercial issues, and selecting procedures that comply with the governing rules.
