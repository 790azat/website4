Insurance settlements and risk assessments often involve more than determining the immediate cost of a loss. When payments, medical expenses, lost income, property damage, or other obligations extend into the future, economic assumptions can influence how those amounts are evaluated.

Variables such as inflation, interest rates, expected investment returns, claim severity, loss frequency, wage growth, medical-cost trends, and the timing of future payments can all affect an insurer's financial analysis.

These concepts are particularly important in actuarial work, where insurers estimate future liabilities and evaluate whether premiums, reserves, capital, and assets are sufficient to support expected obligations. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) identifies actuarial modeling, stress testing, reserve calculations, and capital adequacy as important parts of insurance solvency analysis.

## 1. What Is Settlement Valuation?

Settlement valuation is the process of evaluating the financial amount associated with resolving a claim or dispute.

Depending on the situation, the valuation may consider:

- Past economic losses
- Future medical expenses
- Lost earnings
- Property damage
- Insurance limits
- Deductibles
- Expected future costs
- The timing of payments
- Uncertainty surrounding future losses

The economic analysis used by an insurer is not necessarily identical to the legal analysis used by a court.

For example, a court may determine damages under a state's rules, while an insurer may use actuarial or claims models to estimate its expected financial exposure.

## 2. Claim Frequency

**Claim frequency** measures how often claims occur relative to an appropriate exposure base.

For example, an insurer could examine the number of automobile claims per insured vehicle-year.

Frequency is important because a portfolio with many relatively small claims can have a different financial risk profile from a portfolio with fewer but much larger claims.

Actuaries may analyze historical frequency and adjust it for expected changes in:

- Policyholder behavior
- Exposure
- Economic conditions
- Regulation
- Technology
- Claim reporting
- Underwriting practices

Frequency is therefore one component of broader insurance risk assessment.

## 3. Claim Severity

**Claim severity** refers to the financial size of losses.

Severity can be affected by:

- Medical costs
- Repair costs
- Construction costs
- Legal expenses
- Settlement values
- Inflation
- Duration of claims
- Changes in treatment or technology

A rise in average claim severity can materially affect an insurer even when the number of claims remains stable.

NAIC materials concerning unpaid claims recognize that estimated ultimate claim costs can incorporate inflation and other economic and societal factors, with estimates based on historical experience adjusted for current trends and other relevant factors.

## 4. Inflation

Inflation can significantly affect the valuation of future losses.

Suppose an insurer expects a medical procedure to cost $20,000 today. If the procedure is expected to occur several years later, the eventual cost could be higher because of medical inflation.

Similar considerations can apply to:

- Vehicle repairs
- Replacement property
- Construction
- Labor
- Medical equipment
- Prescription drugs
- Professional services

Inflation therefore needs to be distinguished from the underlying change in the quantity or severity of services required.

## 5. Medical-Cost Inflation

Medical claims can be especially sensitive to changes in healthcare costs.

Future medical expenses may depend on:

- Treatment prices
- Hospital costs
- Physician fees
- Prescription prices
- Rehabilitation costs
- Medical technology
- Expected duration of treatment

For a long-term injury claim, the difference between today's medical costs and projected future costs can become substantial.

The appropriate projection depends on the evidence and the assumptions used rather than on a single universal inflation rate.

## 6. Wage Growth and Lost Earnings

Economic damages can sometimes involve income that a claimant allegedly would have earned in the future.

Relevant variables may include:

- Current wages
- Expected wage growth
- Career progression
- Employment history
- Work-life expectancy
- Benefits
- Retirement contributions
- Taxes, where legally relevant
- Ability to return to work

A simple calculation based only on current annual income may therefore fail to capture the economic assumptions involved in a long-term earnings analysis.

The appropriate methodology depends on the jurisdiction and the type of claim.

## 7. Discount Rates

When a valuation involves future payments, analysts may consider the **time value of money**.

A dollar received today is not financially equivalent to a dollar received many years from now because money held today can potentially earn a return.

A discount rate can be used to convert future cash flows into an estimated present value.

For example:

**Present Value = Future Payment ÷ (1 + discount rate)ⁿ**

where **n** represents the number of periods until the payment is expected.

Actual legal and actuarial calculations can be considerably more complicated.

Some courts also have specific rules concerning how future economic damages are converted into present value. For example, current Ninth Circuit model civil jury instructions provide for discounting certain future economic damages to present cash value and instruct juries to consider reasonably expected investment returns and future inflation when supported by evidence.

## 8. Inflation and Discount Rates Interact

Inflation and discounting should not necessarily be treated as independent concepts.

Suppose future medical expenses are projected using an assumed annual inflation rate. A separate discount rate may then be applied to determine their present value.

Using inconsistent assumptions can materially change the resulting valuation.

Analysts therefore need to understand whether their calculations are being performed in:

- Nominal dollars
- Inflation-adjusted dollars
- Real terms

The assumptions must be internally consistent.

## 9. Interest Rates

Interest rates can influence insurance economics in several ways.

For insurers, interest rates can affect:

- Investment income
- Asset values
- Liability valuation
- Reinvestment assumptions
- Liquidity
- Reserve calculations
- Capital requirements

NAIC explains that interest-rate conditions can have significant effects on insurers, particularly life insurers, and that insurers use interest-rate scenarios and cash-flow testing to assess risks associated with asset and liability mismatches.

Interest-rate assumptions can therefore influence an insurer's broader financial risk even when they do not directly determine the amount of an individual claim.

## 10. Investment Returns

Insurance companies collect premiums before many claims are paid. The funds may be invested during that period.

Expected investment returns can therefore be an important component of insurance economics.

Higher or lower returns can affect:

- Profitability
- Reserve adequacy
- Capital
- Product pricing
- Asset-liability management

The relationship varies by insurance product.

For example, NAIC notes that life insurers can be particularly sensitive to the relationship between investment yields and guaranteed rates credited to policyholders.

## 11. Timing of Payments

Two claims with identical nominal amounts can have different economic values if they are paid at different times.

For example:

- Claim A pays $100,000 immediately.
- Claim B pays $20,000 annually for five years.

The total nominal amount is the same, but the economic value and risk profile can differ because the payments occur at different times.

Settlement analysis therefore may consider not only **how much** will be paid but also **when** payment is expected to occur.

## 12. Duration of Claims

Some insurance claims are resolved relatively quickly. Others remain open for years.

Long-duration claims can create additional uncertainty because future costs are affected by:

- Inflation
- Medical developments
- Legal developments
- Claimant recovery
- Wage changes
- Changes in treatment
- Settlement timing

Actuaries may analyze historical claims-development patterns to estimate how reported losses are likely to develop toward their ultimate cost.

This is particularly important for insurers calculating reserves for claims that have already occurred but have not yet been fully settled.

## 13. Reserve Adequacy

An insurer must maintain sufficient resources to meet future obligations.

Reserves represent amounts set aside or recognized for expected future claims and related obligations.

NAIC explains that insurers use actuarial requirements and guidelines to address issues including reserve adequacy, capital adequacy, valuation, and solvency.

For life insurance, modern principle-based reserving can incorporate a range of future economic conditions and insurer-specific experience factors rather than relying exclusively on static assumptions.

Reserve calculations and an individual claimant's settlement value are related but are not the same thing.

## 14. Loss Development

A claim's ultimate value may not be known when it is first reported.

For example, a liability claim might initially involve a relatively small reserve but later develop because:

- Additional medical treatment occurs
- The injury proves more serious
- Additional parties become involved
- Litigation develops
- New evidence emerges
- Settlement negotiations change

Actuaries use loss-development methods to estimate how reported losses may develop over time.

The resulting estimate is generally a portfolio-level or claims-management tool, rather than a guaranteed prediction of the final value of an individual claim.

## 15. Legal and Social Trends

Economic conditions are not the only variables affecting liability claims.

Insurers may also analyze changes in:

- Litigation frequency
- Legal expenses
- Settlement behavior
- Jury awards
- Court procedures
- Legislation
- Judicial decisions

NAIC uses the term **social inflation** to describe increases in liability claims costs above general economic inflation and identifies litigation-related factors among potential contributors.

Because the causes and measurement of social inflation can be debated, it should be treated as an analytical concept rather than a single universally measured economic variable.

## 16. Catastrophe Risk

Some insurance portfolios are exposed to events that produce many claims at once.

Examples include:

- Hurricanes
- Wildfires
- Earthquakes
- Severe storms
- Floods

Catastrophe modeling can evaluate potential losses under different scenarios.

Economic variables may include:

- Property values
- Repair costs
- Construction inflation
- Insurance penetration
- Geographic concentration
- Reinsurance costs
- Expected event frequency

A major catastrophe can affect both individual claims and the insurer's overall capital position.

## 17. Reinsurance Costs

Insurers may transfer some of their risk to reinsurers.

The cost and structure of reinsurance can affect an insurer's financial assessment of a portfolio.

Relevant factors may include:

- Coverage limits
- Attachment points
- Expected losses
- Catastrophe exposure
- Reinsurance pricing
- Counterparty risk

Reinsurance can reduce an insurer's net exposure, but it does not eliminate the underlying economic uncertainty.

## 18. Interest-Rate and Market Stress Testing

Insurers can examine how financial results change under alternative economic scenarios.

Scenarios may include:

- Higher interest rates
- Lower interest rates
- Higher inflation
- Lower investment returns
- Increased claim severity
- Catastrophic losses
- Changes in policyholder behavior

NAIC describes stress testing, financial modeling, reserve calculations, and capital adequacy as important actuarial tools for insurance solvency.

The purpose of stress testing is not to predict exactly what will happen. It is to understand how vulnerable an insurer may be under adverse conditions.

## 19. Risk-Based Capital

Insurance companies must maintain sufficient capital to support the risks they assume.

NAIC's risk-based capital framework recognizes several categories of insurer risk, including:

- Asset risk
- Insurance or underwriting risk
- Interest-rate risk
- Other business and financial risks

Underwriting risk can include the possibility that an insurer underestimates liabilities from business already written or inadequately prices future business.

This demonstrates why settlement valuation is connected to broader risk management: an insurer must evaluate not only individual claims but also the aggregate risk of its portfolio.

## 20. Uncertainty and Scenario Analysis

Economic valuation should not be confused with certainty.

A future loss can be affected by variables that cannot be known precisely at the time of settlement.

Analysts may therefore use:

- Base-case assumptions
- High-cost scenarios
- Low-cost scenarios
- Sensitivity analysis
- Probability distributions
- Stochastic models

For example, a long-term medical-cost projection could be tested under several inflation assumptions.

If a small change in an assumption produces a large change in the valuation, that assumption may represent a significant source of risk.

## 21. Settlement Valuation Is Not the Same as Actuarial Reserving

These concepts overlap but have different purposes.

**Settlement valuation** generally concerns the amount or terms proposed to resolve a particular dispute or claim.

**Actuarial reserving** generally concerns estimating an insurer's future obligations across claims or a portfolio.

An insurer may have a reserve for an individual claim that differs from the amount eventually paid to settle it.

Similarly, an actuarial reserve for a portfolio does not establish what a particular claimant is legally entitled to receive.

## 22. Economic Analysis Does Not Determine Legal Liability

Economic models can estimate financial consequences, but they do not independently establish who is legally responsible.

Legal liability may depend on:

- Applicable statutes
- Case law
- Contract language
- Negligence standards
- Comparative-fault rules
- Causation
- Evidence
- Procedural requirements

Likewise, an actuarial estimate of an insurer's expected claim cost does not establish the legally recoverable damages in an individual case.

The economic and legal analyses should therefore be kept distinct.

## 23. The Importance of Transparent Assumptions

A valuation is only as useful as the assumptions supporting it.

Important assumptions can include:

- Inflation rate
- Discount rate
- Wage growth
- Medical-cost growth
- Claim frequency
- Claim severity
- Investment return
- Expected settlement date
- Life expectancy or duration, where relevant

A well-documented analysis should identify the assumptions and explain why they were selected.

Changing one assumption can materially change the result.

## 24. Why Economic Variables Matter to Risk Assessment

The broader purpose of economic analysis is to understand uncertainty before it becomes a financial problem.

An insurer can use economic variables to evaluate questions such as:

- How much capital may be needed?
- How sensitive are reserves to inflation?
- What happens if claim severity rises?
- How would interest-rate changes affect assets and liabilities?
- How much exposure exists to catastrophic events?
- How much reinsurance may be appropriate?
- Are current pricing assumptions consistent with expected costs?

NAIC's actuarial guidance specifically addresses evolving economic conditions, market risk, reserve adequacy, capital adequacy, and solvency oversight.

## Final Takeaway

Economic variables provide an important framework for evaluating the financial consequences of insurance claims and future risks.

**Inflation, claim frequency, claim severity, wage growth, medical costs, interest rates, discount rates, investment returns, payment timing, loss development, reinsurance, and capital requirements** can all influence an insurer's assessment of risk or the economic analysis surrounding a claim.

The effect of these variables depends heavily on the purpose of the calculation. An actuarial reserve, an insurer's internal claim valuation, a settlement proposal, and a court's calculation of damages are not necessarily based on the same methodology.

Future economic damages may sometimes be converted to present value using an appropriate discount methodology, while insurers use broader actuarial frameworks to estimate liabilities and evaluate solvency. NAIC's current actuarial guidance illustrates how economic scenarios, interest rates, reserve requirements, and capital considerations interact within insurance regulation.

For an individual insurance or civil claim, economic assumptions should be evaluated alongside the applicable policy, evidence, and jurisdiction-specific legal rules. Where substantial future losses are involved, the assumptions behind a valuation can be as important as the arithmetic itself.
