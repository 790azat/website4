Employment separation can occur for many reasons, including resignation, expiration of a contract, layoffs, performance concerns, misconduct, business restructuring, or termination. Regardless of how an employment relationship ends, accurate documentation can be important for both employers and employees.

Documentation helps establish what happened, when decisions were made, what reasons were communicated, and what records support those explanations. In the United States, employment records can also become relevant in discrimination, retaliation, wage, contractual, unemployment, or other employment disputes. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) requires covered employers to retain certain personnel and employment records and imposes additional preservation requirements when a discrimination charge has been filed.

## Identifying the Type of Employment Separation

The first step in evaluating a separation is identifying how the employment relationship ended.

A resignation generally involves the employee voluntarily choosing to leave. A termination involves the employer ending the employment relationship. A layoff may result from economic conditions, restructuring, elimination of a position, or other business circumstances rather than an allegation of employee misconduct.

The distinction can matter because different legal and administrative rules may apply depending on the circumstances. A written separation notice, resignation letter, termination letter, employment agreement, or relevant personnel record may help establish the basic facts.

Documentation should accurately describe the event rather than using terminology that could create confusion about whether the employee resigned or was terminated.

## Recording the Reason for Termination

When an employer terminates an employee, documenting the reason for the decision can be important.

The EEOC advises employers to consider documenting the reasons for disciplinary and termination decisions because such documentation may help defend the business if a discrimination charge or complaint is later filed. The agency also recommends that employers generally apply their disciplinary policies consistently or be able to justify deviations from those policies.

A useful record should identify the factual basis for the decision rather than relying solely on vague descriptions such as “poor fit” or “performance issues.”

For example, where performance is the stated reason, relevant documentation might include performance evaluations, written expectations, prior warnings, attendance records, or communications concerning specific deficiencies.

The objective is not to create a justification after the fact. Documentation is most useful when it reflects information that existed and was considered when the decision was actually made.

## Establishing the Timeline

A chronological record can help clarify how a termination decision developed.

Relevant dates may include:

- The beginning of employment
- Performance reviews
- Complaints or disciplinary events
- Performance warnings
- Investigations
- Requests for workplace accommodations
- Employee complaints
- Management meetings
- Changes in job duties
- The decision to terminate
- The date the employee was notified
- The final date of employment

A timeline can reveal whether the stated reason for termination is consistent with the surrounding events.

For example, if an employer identifies a performance problem as the reason for termination, records created before the termination may help establish whether that concern had previously been raised. Conversely, a sudden change in the stated explanation may warrant additional review.

## Comparing the Stated Reason With Workplace Records

Evaluating termination circumstances requires more than reading the termination letter.

Other employment records may provide relevant context. Performance evaluations, disciplinary records, attendance information, emails, workplace policies, job descriptions, compensation records, and communications with supervisors can help establish the circumstances surrounding the decision.

The EEOC specifically advises employers to consider whether disciplinary and termination decisions are consistent with their policies. If an employer departs from an established policy, the agency recommends being able to justify the different treatment.

This does not mean that every policy deviation is unlawful. Workplaces sometimes encounter circumstances requiring individualized decisions. The important question is whether the employer can explain the decision using legitimate, documented facts.

## Evaluating Potential Discrimination

A termination may raise legal concerns if the decision was based on a protected characteristic covered by applicable law.

Federal employment discrimination laws enforced by the EEOC address discrimination involving characteristics including race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, disability, and genetic information, subject to the coverage requirements of the applicable statute.

When reviewing a termination, relevant information can include how similarly situated employees were treated, whether the employer's stated explanation is supported by records, and whether there were significant changes in the employee's treatment before termination.

An employee's belief that a termination was unfair does not by itself establish unlawful discrimination. A legal assessment requires consideration of the applicable statute, evidence, employer coverage, and surrounding circumstances.

## Evaluating Possible Retaliation

Retaliation is another important issue when reviewing an employment separation.

Federal law prohibits retaliation against employees for certain protected activities, including reporting discrimination, participating in discrimination investigations or proceedings, or opposing discrimination in circumstances protected by law.

The timing of events can therefore be relevant.

For example, an evaluator may examine whether an employee complained about discrimination, requested an accommodation, participated in an investigation, or engaged in another protected activity shortly before an adverse employment action.

Timing alone does not establish retaliation. However, it can be one part of the factual record used to evaluate whether the employer's stated reason is consistent with the circumstances.

The EEOC also explains that protected activity does not give an employee immunity from legitimate discipline or termination for reasons unrelated to the protected activity.

## Considering Protected Workplace Activity

Some terminations may also involve rights protected under federal labor law.

The National Labor Relations Act protects certain concerted activities by employees concerning wages, benefits, working conditions, and other employment-related issues. The National Labor Relations Board states that covered employees generally cannot be discharged, disciplined, threatened, or coercively questioned because of protected concerted activity.

Consequently, a review of an employment separation may need to consider whether the employee was participating in protected activity with or on behalf of other employees.

This area can be fact-specific. Not every workplace complaint is protected concerted activity, and the protections can depend on what the employee did, why the activity occurred, and the circumstances surrounding it.

## Reviewing Consistency Among Employees

Comparing treatment of employees can provide additional context.

If two employees engaged in substantially similar conduct but received substantially different disciplinary treatment, the difference may warrant examination. Relevant factors can include job responsibilities, supervisors, prior disciplinary histories, workplace policies, seriousness of the conduct, and other circumstances.

A comparison should therefore be based on meaningful similarities rather than superficial comparisons.

For example, two employees receiving different consequences does not necessarily demonstrate discrimination if their underlying circumstances were materially different. Conversely, unexplained inconsistencies can raise questions about whether a stated termination reason was applied consistently.

## Preserving Relevant Employment Records

Documentation should be preserved according to applicable legal requirements and organizational policies.

The EEOC states that covered private employers generally must retain personnel and employment records for one year, with specific rules applying to involuntary terminations. Educational institutions and state and local governments generally have longer retention periods under the EEOC's regulations. Additional obligations can apply to payroll, benefits, and other records.

When a discrimination charge has been filed, relevant records generally must be preserved until the charge or related litigation reaches final disposition.

Record preservation is therefore not simply an administrative task. Deleting or altering relevant records after a dispute arises can create additional legal complications.

## Separating Facts From Conclusions

A strong employment record distinguishes factual observations from legal conclusions.

For example, a manager's record might state that an employee missed specified deadlines, received particular warnings, or failed to complete identified assignments. It should avoid unsupported statements about the employee's character or motives.

Similarly, an employee documenting a termination can distinguish between what the employer actually said and the employee's interpretation of why the decision occurred.

This approach makes the record easier to evaluate later and reduces the risk that assumptions will be mistaken for established facts.

## Reviewing the Separation Before Finalizing It

Employers can reduce uncertainty by reviewing termination documentation before communicating the final decision.

The review may consider:

- The stated reason for termination
- Supporting documentation
- Applicable workplace policies
- Treatment of comparable employees
- Recent complaints or protected activity
- Requests for accommodation
- Relevant employment agreements
- Applicable federal, state, and local requirements
- Required record-retention obligations

The EEOC recommends that managers responsible for discipline and termination understand their responsibilities and that employers respond appropriately to discrimination complaints involving disciplinary action.

Legal review may be particularly appropriate when the circumstances involve discrimination allegations, retaliation concerns, protected leave, accommodation requests, whistleblowing, collective workplace activity, contractual disputes, or other potentially significant legal issues.

## Evaluating an Employment Separation Fairly

Documenting an employment separation is not simply about creating a record that supports one side's position. A useful record should provide an accurate account of the events surrounding the decision.

For employers, this means documenting legitimate reasons consistently and preserving relevant records. For employees, it can mean maintaining copies of employment-related communications, evaluations, warnings, complaints, and separation documents that they are lawfully entitled to retain.

The central questions are generally factual: What happened? When did it happen? What reason was given? What records support that reason? Were workplace policies applied consistently? Was there protected activity or another legally significant event preceding the termination?

Answering those questions carefully can help distinguish an ordinary employment separation from circumstances that may require further legal or administrative review.

Because employment law varies considerably by jurisdiction and individual circumstances, documentation alone cannot determine whether a termination was lawful. A qualified employment attorney or appropriate government agency may be needed to evaluate specific claims, deadlines, and available remedies.
