Impaired driving remains a significant public-safety issue, particularly when alcohol or other substances affect a driver's judgment, reaction time, coordination, or ability to operate a vehicle safely. Governments use a combination of laws, enforcement measures, public education, roadside testing, vehicle technology, and treatment programs to reduce the risks associated with impaired driving.

In the United States, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) reported **11,904 deaths in alcohol-impaired driving crashes in 2024**. NHTSA defines an alcohol-impaired-driving crash for statistical purposes as one involving a driver or motorcycle rider with a blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of at least 0.08 grams per deciliter.

Breath-testing technology is an important part of enforcement because it provides a practical method of measuring alcohol concentration without requiring an immediate blood sample. Modern systems range from portable roadside screening devices to evidential instruments used to produce measurements that may be introduced in legal proceedings.

## Understanding Impaired Driving

Impaired driving encompasses driving after consuming alcohol or using drugs or other substances that affect driving ability. Alcohol is particularly well studied because its relationship with driving performance can be measured through BAC.

Alcohol can impair judgment, attention, visual functions, reaction time, and motor coordination. NHTSA notes that impairment can occur even below the legal BAC threshold, meaning that the legal limit should not be interpreted as a point below which alcohol has no effect on driving.

Drug-impaired driving presents additional measurement challenges because different substances can produce different effects and can remain detectable for different periods. The presence of a substance does not necessarily provide the same information about impairment as a contemporaneous alcohol measurement.

## Current Impaired-Driving Statistics

National statistics demonstrate the continuing scale of the problem.

NHTSA recorded **12,429 traffic fatalities in 2023 involving at least one driver with a BAC of 0.08 g/dL or higher**, representing approximately 30% of all U.S. traffic fatalities that year.

The CDC also reports that millions of U.S. residents drive after consuming alcohol or using other substances. Its 2026 impaired-driving overview estimates that **15.5 million U.S. residents aged 16 and older drove under the influence of alcohol during the preceding year**, based on 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health data. The same survey estimated 12.1 million people drove under the influence of cannabis.

These figures come from different data sources and measure different aspects of impaired driving. Fatal-crash statistics count deaths, while survey data estimate self-reported behavior. They should therefore not be treated as interchangeable measures.

## How Alcohol Affects Driving

Alcohol affects the central nervous system. As alcohol concentration increases, impairment generally becomes more pronounced.

At relatively low BAC levels, drivers can experience changes in visual tracking, divided attention, judgment, and information processing. As BAC rises, coordination and reaction ability can deteriorate further.

NHTSA notes that crash risk increases substantially at a BAC of 0.08 g/dL, while also emphasizing that even lower levels can affect driving performance.

Individual responses can vary based on factors such as body composition, food consumption, drinking pattern, tolerance, medications, and other substances. Consequently, people cannot reliably determine whether they are safe to drive simply by estimating how much alcohol they consumed.

## What Is a Breathalyzer?

"Breathalyzer" is commonly used as a general term for a device that measures alcohol in a person's breath. In professional enforcement systems, however, different types of breath-testing equipment have distinct functions.

NHTSA identifies two major categories used by law enforcement:

- **Preliminary breath test devices (PBTs)**, which are generally portable roadside screening instruments.
- **Evidential breath test devices (EBTs)**, which are approved instruments that can provide measurements used as evidence under applicable state law.

NHTSA maintains model specifications and lists of devices that have been tested for conformity with applicable technical requirements.

The distinction matters because a roadside screening device and an evidential instrument may serve different purposes within an impaired-driving investigation.

## How Breath Testing Works

Breath-testing equipment measures alcohol concentration in a breath sample. The result is commonly expressed as breath alcohol concentration (BrAC).

Because alcohol enters the bloodstream and is exchanged in the lungs, alcohol present in deep-lung breath can be used as an indicator of alcohol concentration in the body. Professional instruments are designed to analyze the sample and produce a numerical result.

Modern devices may use different sensing technologies and internal procedures to analyze breath samples. Their reliability depends on appropriate design, calibration, maintenance, operation, and compliance with applicable testing procedures.

NHTSA establishes model specifications for evidential breath alcohol measurement devices, screening devices, calibration units, and breath-alcohol ignition interlocks.

## Roadside Screening and Evidential Testing

Roadside testing is often one part of a broader impaired-driving investigation.

A preliminary breath test can help an officer determine whether alcohol may be present and can contribute to decisions concerning further investigation. NHTSA describes PBTs as handheld screening devices used at the roadside to assist officers in determining probable cause for an arrest.

Evidential breath-testing instruments serve a different function. They are designed to meet applicable technical specifications and may provide measurements that can be used as evidence in court, depending on state law and the circumstances of the test.

The legal significance of a breath result therefore depends not only on the number displayed by the instrument but also on the applicable testing procedures and evidentiary rules.

## Calibration and Quality Control

Accuracy depends on more than the sensor itself. Breath-testing equipment must be maintained and used according to applicable procedures.

NHTSA maintains model specifications for calibration units and publishes lists of devices that conform to its specifications.

Testing programs may also establish requirements concerning operator training, observation periods, maintenance, calibration, record-keeping, and verification. The specific requirements differ among jurisdictions.

These procedures are important because breath-test results can have significant consequences in criminal, administrative, and licensing proceedings.

## Ignition Interlock Technology

Breath-testing technology can also be incorporated directly into vehicles.

An **ignition interlock device** requires a driver to provide a breath sample before the vehicle can start. If the measured alcohol concentration exceeds the device's programmed threshold, the vehicle will not start.

NHTSA reports that ignition interlocks generally use a low preset threshold, commonly around 0.02 g/dL, and may require additional breath tests after the vehicle has started. Devices can also record test results for monitoring purposes.

The technology is therefore different from a conventional roadside breath test. Instead of simply measuring alcohol for enforcement purposes, an ignition interlock is designed to prevent a vehicle from being operated after a failed breath test.

## Evidence on Ignition Interlocks

Research summarized by NHTSA indicates that ignition interlocks can substantially reduce impaired-driving recidivism while they remain installed.

NHTSA's review reports that one analysis of 15 studies found a **75% lower arrest-recidivism rate** among offenders with interlocks compared with those without them. The agency also notes that the reduction generally diminishes after the devices are removed.

NHTSA further reports research finding fewer alcohol-related fatal crashes in states with broader ignition-interlock requirements. One study cited by the agency found that states requiring interlocks for all DWI offenders had 26% fewer alcohol-involved fatal crashes than states without interlock laws.

These findings concern particular studies and policy environments; they should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every individual interlock program will produce identical results.

## Emerging Vehicle-Based Alcohol Detection

Researchers are also developing systems that could detect alcohol without requiring a conventional handheld breath test.

NHTSA's Driver Alcohol Detection System for Safety (DADSS) program has examined technologies capable of detecting alcohol through passive breath measurements or sensors that measure alcohol through skin tissue. The objective is to develop systems that could operate with less interaction from the driver than traditional ignition interlocks.

These technologies are part of a broader effort to incorporate impairment-prevention mechanisms directly into vehicles.

NHTSA reported in 2026 that it continues research into advanced impaired-driving prevention technologies as part of its federal responsibilities concerning roadway safety.

## Data Collection and Public Safety

Reliable data is essential for understanding impaired-driving trends and evaluating safety programs.

NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System collects information concerning BAC results from fatal crashes. However, testing coverage is not identical across all states or all drivers involved in fatal crashes. NHTSA has noted substantial differences in BAC-testing rates among states.

This limitation matters when interpreting statistics. A missing test result does not establish that a driver was sober, nor does it establish impairment. Researchers therefore distinguish between known test results and cases where BAC information is unavailable.

Improving data collection can help governments identify high-risk populations, evaluate enforcement strategies, and determine where additional prevention measures may be appropriate.

## Public-Safety Strategies

Breath testing is only one component of impaired-driving prevention.

Other strategies include:

- Enforcement of impaired-driving laws.
- Administrative license sanctions.
- Public-awareness campaigns.
- Ignition interlocks.
- Treatment and assessment programs.
- Designated-driver initiatives.
- Transportation alternatives.
- Improved data collection.
- Vehicle-based alcohol detection technology.

NHTSA's impaired-driving guidance includes chemical testing, administrative sanctions, ignition interlocks, treatment, monitoring, and other measures within a broader enforcement framework.

Combining these measures can address different stages of the problem, from preventing an impaired person from driving to detecting impairment and preventing repeat offenses.

## Legal and Evidentiary Considerations

Breathalyzer evidence can become important in criminal and administrative proceedings, but its legal treatment varies by jurisdiction.

Questions may include whether the device was approved, whether it was properly maintained and calibrated, whether the operator followed required procedures, whether the test was administered correctly, and whether applicable evidentiary requirements were satisfied.

A numerical breath result should therefore be understood within its legal and technical context rather than considered independently of the testing process.

## Conclusion

Impaired driving remains a substantial public-safety concern. U.S. federal data show that alcohol-impaired crashes continue to account for a significant share of roadway fatalities, while survey research indicates that millions of people report driving after using alcohol or other substances.

Breath-testing technology provides an important tool for identifying alcohol exposure, with roadside screening devices, evidential breath instruments, and ignition interlocks serving different purposes. Technical standards, calibration, proper administration, and reliable record-keeping are important to the accuracy and legal usefulness of these systems.

Public-safety strategies increasingly combine traditional enforcement with vehicle technology and prevention programs. As passive alcohol detection and other advanced systems develop, technology may become an increasingly integrated part of efforts to prevent impaired individuals from operating vehicles.
