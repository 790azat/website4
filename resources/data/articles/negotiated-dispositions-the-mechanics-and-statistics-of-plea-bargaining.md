Plea bargaining is one of the most significant mechanisms through which criminal cases are resolved in the United States. Rather than proceeding to a full trial, a defendant may agree to plead guilty or, in some circumstances, no contest in exchange for an agreed recommendation, dismissal or reduction of charges, or another negotiated outcome.

These agreements can reduce the number of cases requiring trials while allowing prosecutors and defendants to resolve cases with greater predictability. At the same time, plea bargaining raises important questions concerning legal representation, prosecutorial discretion, sentencing, evidentiary strength, and the defendant's ability to make an informed decision.

Federal statistics demonstrate how common negotiated or otherwise guilty-plea dispositions are. The U.S. Sentencing Commission reported that in fiscal year 2025, **98 percent of federally sentenced cases resulted in guilty pleas**, while only 2.3 percent were resolved through trial. The Commission's dataset included 66,662 federal felony and Class A misdemeanor cases involving individuals sentenced during that fiscal year.

## What Is a Negotiated Disposition?

A negotiated disposition occurs when the prosecution and defense reach an agreement that resolves a criminal case without requiring a contested trial on every charge.

The most familiar form is a plea agreement. The defendant agrees to plead guilty, while the prosecution may agree to dismiss certain charges, reduce charges, recommend a particular sentence, or make another concession permitted by law.

The precise structure varies by jurisdiction and case. Some agreements concern the charges themselves, while others concern sentencing recommendations or factual matters relevant to sentencing.

A guilty plea is different from an admission made informally during negotiations. A formal plea requires compliance with applicable procedural rules and judicial requirements.

## The Federal Rule 11 Framework

Federal plea agreements are governed in significant part by Rule 11 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure. Rule 11 permits several forms of agreement between the government and the defendant.

Under Rule 11(c)(1), the parties may agree that the government will dismiss or refrain from bringing other charges, recommend or agree not to oppose a particular sentence or sentencing range, or agree that a particular sentencing provision or factor applies or does not apply. Certain types of agreements may be binding on the court once accepted, while others operate as recommendations.

The distinction matters because a defendant should understand what the prosecution has actually promised and what remains within the court's discretion.

## Charge Bargaining and Sentence Bargaining

Plea negotiations can involve different forms of bargaining.

**Charge bargaining** occurs when the prosecution agrees to reduce, dismiss, or refrain from pursuing particular charges in exchange for a guilty plea.

**Sentence bargaining** involves an agreement or recommendation concerning the punishment associated with the offense. Depending on the jurisdiction and applicable rules, the recommendation may or may not bind the sentencing court.

Other negotiations may concern factual stipulations, dismissal of additional counts, restitution, forfeiture, or sentencing-related recommendations.

The practical consequences can vary substantially. A reduction from one offense to another may affect statutory penalties, sentencing guidelines, collateral consequences, licensing issues, immigration consequences, or eligibility for particular programs.

## The Defendant's Decision

A guilty plea generally involves a waiver of important trial-related rights. Before accepting a guilty plea in federal court, Rule 11 requires the court to address matters including the defendant's understanding of the plea, the rights being waived, and the consequences of the plea.

The defendant's decision should therefore be informed by an assessment of the available evidence, potential defenses, sentencing exposure, negotiated terms, and collateral consequences.

Defense counsel has an important role in this process. Counsel can investigate the government's case, explain the potential consequences of different outcomes, assess the proposed agreement, and advise the defendant concerning available options.

The final decision to accept or reject a plea belongs to the defendant under applicable legal and ethical rules.

## The Prosecutor's Role

Prosecutors exercise substantial discretion when negotiating criminal cases. They may consider the seriousness of the conduct, available evidence, criminal history, cooperation, victim interests, public-safety considerations, and applicable charging policies.

A negotiated resolution can also conserve prosecutorial and judicial resources. A case that would otherwise require jury selection, multiple hearings, witness testimony, expert evidence, and post-trial proceedings may be resolved through a plea hearing and sentencing process.

That efficiency does not eliminate the need for careful review. Prosecutorial decisions remain subject to constitutional requirements, applicable statutes, professional rules, and judicial procedures.

## Judicial Oversight

Although plea negotiations primarily occur between the prosecution and defense, the court retains an important role.

Federal Rule 11 requires the court to address the defendant personally and determine that the plea is voluntary and supported by an adequate factual basis. The court may accept, reject, or defer consideration of certain types of plea agreements according to the rule.

This judicial review helps distinguish a formal plea disposition from a private agreement between the parties. The court must ensure that required procedural safeguards are satisfied before entering judgment based on the plea.

## Plea Bargaining and Sentencing Guidelines

Federal sentencing can add another layer of complexity to plea negotiations. The United States Sentencing Commission collects information about guideline calculations, statutory penalties, plea agreements, and sentences imposed.

The Commission reported 66,662 federal felony and Class A misdemeanor cases involving sentenced individuals in fiscal year 2025. Its Sourcebook includes data concerning conviction type, guilty pleas and trials, guideline applications, sentence types, sentence lengths, fines, restitution, and other sentencing information.

A negotiated plea may therefore interact with the federal sentencing framework in several ways. The parties can negotiate charges or factual issues that affect sentencing exposure, while the court remains responsible for imposing the sentence under applicable law.

## What the Statistics Show

Federal statistics demonstrate the extent to which guilty pleas dominate federal criminal case resolution. In fiscal year 2025, **65,099 of 66,662 sentenced individuals' cases—97.7 percent—were resolved through guilty pleas**, compared with 1,563 cases, or 2.3 percent, resolved through trial.

The proportion varies substantially by offense and jurisdiction. The Sentencing Commission reported that more than 99 percent of federal immigration cases resulted in guilty pleas in fiscal year 2025, while trials occurred more frequently in categories such as murder and individual-rights offenses.

These figures should not be interpreted as statistics for every criminal case in the United States. They concern cases reported to the federal Sentencing Commission involving individuals sentenced during fiscal year 2025. State criminal courts operate under different laws and procedures and are not represented by these federal figures.

## Mandatory Minimums and Plea Negotiations

Mandatory minimum penalties can also influence plea negotiations. In fiscal year 2025, the Sentencing Commission reported that 13,926 of the 66,662 federal cases in its dataset involved an offense carrying a statutory mandatory minimum penalty. The Commission reported that 33 percent of individuals convicted of offenses carrying mandatory minimums received some form of relief from the minimum, including relief associated with the statutory safety valve or substantial assistance.

These figures illustrate why statutory penalties can be significant during negotiations. The potential difference between charges carrying different mandatory penalties can affect the consequences a defendant faces if convicted.

## Advantages and Concerns

Negotiated dispositions can provide several practical benefits. They may reduce court backlogs, limit litigation costs, spare witnesses from testifying, provide defendants with greater predictability, and allow courts to concentrate trial resources on cases that proceed to adjudication.

However, plea bargaining also raises concerns. Defendants may face pressure to accept an agreement because the potential consequences of proceeding to trial are substantially greater. Differences in resources between prosecution and defense can also affect negotiations.

For these reasons, effective defense representation, meaningful disclosure of relevant evidence, judicial oversight, and voluntary decision-making are important safeguards.

## The Importance of Accurate Case Assessment

A plea agreement should be evaluated against the underlying facts rather than simply its headline terms. Important considerations can include the strength of the evidence, admissibility issues, potential defenses, criminal-history consequences, sentencing exposure, restitution, forfeiture, immigration consequences, professional licensing, and future record implications.

The existence of a negotiated offer does not itself establish that the underlying allegations are true or that a particular agreement is appropriate. Each case depends on its facts and governing law.

## Conclusion

Plea bargaining is a central component of modern criminal justice administration in the United States. Through negotiated dispositions, prosecutors and defendants can resolve cases without a full trial, while courts retain responsibility for ensuring that required procedural safeguards are satisfied.

Federal data show the scale of this practice: 97.7 percent of cases involving individuals sentenced in federal court during fiscal year 2025 were resolved through guilty pleas. The statistics, however, describe the federal sentenced population and should not be generalized automatically to state courts or every criminal proceeding.

Understanding negotiated dispositions requires attention to the structure of the plea agreement, the defendant's rights, prosecutorial discretion, judicial review, sentencing rules, and the factual circumstances of the individual case. Because plea procedures and consequences vary by jurisdiction, defendants considering a negotiated resolution should obtain advice from qualified defense counsel before making decisions about a plea.
