After a traffic collision, an insurance claim may involve more than submitting photographs and waiting for a repair estimate. Insurance companies typically investigate the circumstances of a collision, evaluate the damage, review available evidence, and determine how the policy applies to the reported loss.

A **claims adjuster** may inspect the vehicle, review documentation, speak with the people involved, and evaluate the information collected during the investigation. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) explains that adjusters assess damage and determine claim payments, while the specific claims process is affected by state law and the terms of the insurance policy.

Understanding what an adjuster or investigator may examine can help drivers organize their records and provide accurate information after a crash.

## What Is an Insurance Claims Adjuster?

An insurance claims adjuster is a person who investigates an insurance claim and evaluates issues such as coverage, damage, and the amount potentially payable under a policy.

The NAIC identifies several types of adjusters, including company or staff adjusters, independent adjusters, and public adjusters. Staff and independent adjusters generally work for or represent the insurer, while public adjusters represent insured individuals in certain types of claims.

For an auto collision, an adjuster may review:

- Vehicle damage
- Accident circumstances
- Police reports
- Photographs
- Witness information
- Statements from drivers
- Repair estimates
- Insurance coverage
- Medical information when relevant to the claim
- Other documentation

The exact investigation depends on the type and complexity of the claim.

## The First Stage: Reporting the Collision

The investigation generally begins when the insurer receives notice of the accident.

The policy may contain requirements concerning when and how a claim should be reported. The NAIC recommends contacting the insurer as soon as possible after a collision and keeping documentation related to the claim.

During the initial report, the insurer may ask for basic information such as:

- Date and time of the accident
- Location
- Vehicles involved
- Driver information
- Insurance information
- Description of the collision
- Whether anyone was injured
- Whether police responded
- Whether the vehicles are drivable

The initial report does not necessarily resolve questions about fault or coverage.

It provides the insurer with information needed to begin the investigation.

## Reviewing the Accident Account

The insurer may ask each involved person to describe what happened.

Investigators can compare those accounts with other evidence.

For example, one driver might report that they had a green traffic signal, while another driver reports the same thing. Investigators may then look for additional evidence, such as:

- Traffic-camera footage
- Dashcam recordings
- Witness statements
- Vehicle positions
- Damage patterns
- Police documentation
- Road markings
- Photographs

The purpose is to develop a factual understanding of the collision rather than relying exclusively on one person's description.

## Police Reports and Accident Records

A police report may become an important part of an insurance investigation when officers respond to the collision.

The report may contain information about:

- Statements made at the scene
- Driver information
- Witnesses
- Vehicle positions
- Traffic violations
- Injuries reported
- Road conditions
- Officer observations

However, a police report is only one piece of evidence.

An insurer may conduct its own investigation and reach conclusions based on the policy, available evidence, and applicable law.

Drivers should therefore preserve a copy of the report when it becomes available.

## Photographs and Video Evidence

Photographs can help document the physical circumstances of a collision.

Useful photographs may show:

- Damage to each vehicle
- Vehicle positions
- Road signs
- Traffic signals
- Lane markings
- Debris
- Skid marks
- Weather or road conditions
- Nearby property damage

Video can be particularly useful when available.

Potential sources include:

- Dashcams
- Traffic cameras
- Business surveillance systems
- Parking-lot cameras
- Security cameras
- Mobile-phone recordings

The NAIC recommends collecting information at the scene, including photographs or a diagram of the accident and information about witnesses and road conditions.

## Vehicle Damage Inspection

An adjuster may inspect the damaged vehicle personally or use photographs and other inspection methods.

The inspection can document:

- Location of damage
- Severity of damage
- Parts requiring repair
- Possible repair costs
- Whether additional damage may exist
- Whether the vehicle may be a total loss

The adjuster's assessment can then be used as part of the insurer's claim evaluation.

Damage does not necessarily prove exactly how a collision occurred by itself. It is generally considered alongside other available evidence.

## Repair Estimates

If a vehicle can be repaired, the insurer may review the estimated cost of repairs.

The evaluation can include:

- Replacement parts
- Labor
- Paint and refinishing
- Structural repairs
- Related components
- Applicable taxes and fees

The process can vary between insurers and states.

If additional damage is discovered after repairs begin, the repair shop may communicate with the insurer about a supplemental estimate.

## How Insurers Evaluate Fault

Fault is particularly important when liability coverage is involved.

An insurer may consider evidence concerning:

- Right of way
- Traffic signals
- Speed
- Lane position
- Following distance
- Turning movements
- Vehicle locations
- Driver statements
- Witness accounts
- Police observations
- Video evidence

The applicable legal standard can depend on the state where the accident occurred.

Some states use comparative-negligence systems, while other jurisdictions have different approaches to allocating responsibility.

Therefore, an insurer's preliminary assessment should not automatically be treated as a final legal determination of fault.

## Witness Statements

Witnesses can provide information that is independent of the drivers involved.

An investigator may ask a witness:

- Where were you located?
- What did you see?
- Which vehicles were involved?
- What happened immediately before the impact?
- Which direction were the vehicles traveling?
- Did you see a traffic signal?
- Did you see either driver after the collision?

Witnesses should describe what they actually observed rather than speculate about information they could not have known.

The NAIC recommends collecting witnesses' names and contact information following a collision.

## Statements From the Drivers

An insurer may ask drivers to explain what happened.

The questions can cover:

- Where the driver was traveling
- Traffic conditions
- Speed
- Lane position
- Direction of travel
- Actions immediately before impact
- What happened after the collision

It is generally best to provide accurate information and distinguish between what you personally observed and what you are assuming.

If you do not remember something, saying that you do not remember is preferable to guessing.

## Medical Information in Injury Claims

When a collision involves injuries, the investigation may also address medical expenses and the relationship between the accident and the claimed injuries.

Documentation can include:

- Emergency treatment
- Physician records
- Diagnostic testing
- Prescriptions
- Physical therapy
- Medical bills
- Work restrictions

An insurer may review medical documentation when evaluating an injury claim.

The fact that an insurer requests medical information does not by itself mean that the claim will be accepted or denied.

## Reviewing Insurance Coverage

The insurer also has to determine what coverage applies to the particular loss.

Depending on the policy and jurisdiction, an auto policy may contain different forms of coverage, including:

- Bodily injury liability
- Property damage liability
- Collision
- Comprehensive
- Uninsured motorist coverage
- Underinsured motorist coverage
- Medical payments or other applicable coverage

The NAIC explains that collision coverage generally applies to damage caused by a collision, while uninsured-motorist coverage can provide protection in certain situations involving uninsured or hit-and-run drivers.

Coverage cannot be determined from the accident facts alone. The actual policy and applicable state law matter.

## Determining Whether a Vehicle Is a Total Loss

An insurer may determine that repairing a vehicle is not economically appropriate under the applicable rules or policy.

The evaluation can involve:

- Vehicle condition before the accident
- Pre-loss market value
- Estimated repair cost
- Salvage value
- Applicable state requirements

The NAIC notes that an insurer's valuation of a totaled vehicle is generally based on the vehicle's actual value immediately before the crash rather than simply relying on a generic pricing guide.

If the vehicle is declared a total loss, the insurer may explain the valuation methodology and available options.

## Why Some Claims Take Longer

Not every collision claim can be resolved immediately.

An investigation may take longer when:

- Fault is disputed
- Multiple vehicles are involved
- Someone suffered significant injuries
- Police records are unavailable
- Witnesses need to be contacted
- Surveillance footage is being obtained
- Damage is extensive
- Coverage is unclear
- Additional evidence is discovered
- Multiple insurance companies are involved

The NAIC notes that claims requiring special or extended investigation can take longer to resolve.

A delay does not automatically mean that an insurer is acting improperly.

However, claimants can ask for an explanation of what remains outstanding.

## What You Can Do During the Investigation

You can make the process easier by maintaining an organized claim file.

Keep:

- Claim number
- Adjuster's name and contact information
- Police report
- Photographs
- Videos
- Witness information
- Repair estimates
- Medical documentation
- Receipts
- Emails
- Letters
- Notes from telephone conversations

The NAIC recommends keeping records of conversations with agents or adjusters and asking questions when you do not understand a claim decision.

## What If You Disagree With the Adjuster's Assessment?

You do not necessarily have to accept an assessment without asking questions.

If you disagree with a decision, ask the insurer to explain:

- How the decision was reached
- Which policy provision applies
- What evidence was considered
- How the damage estimate was calculated
- What additional documentation could be reviewed

The NAIC recommends asking for written explanations of claim decisions when appropriate. If a dispute cannot be resolved with the insurer, consumers can contact their state insurance department for assistance.

Your policy may also contain an appraisal process for certain disputes involving the value of vehicle damage.

## When Legal Advice May Be Useful

Most minor collision claims do not necessarily require an attorney.

Legal advice may become useful when:

- Serious injuries are involved
- Fault is strongly disputed
- Multiple parties may be responsible
- An insurer denies a significant claim
- A settlement dispute involves substantial damages
- The accident involves a commercial vehicle
- A government vehicle or entity may be involved
- A wrongful-death claim is possible
- A lawsuit has been filed
- A legal deadline is approaching

An attorney can evaluate the particular facts under the law applicable to the accident.

## Final Takeaway

Insurance adjusters and investigators typically evaluate traffic collision claims by considering several categories of information rather than relying on a single piece of evidence.

The investigation may involve the accident report, driver statements, witness accounts, photographs, video, vehicle damage, repair estimates, medical documentation, insurance coverage, and other relevant records.

The NAIC recommends documenting the collision carefully, reporting the claim promptly, cooperating with the adjuster's investigation, and keeping records of communications and supporting documents.

Because insurance rules and claim procedures vary by state and policy, a driver who disagrees with a claim decision should first ask the insurer for an explanation and review the applicable policy. For significant disputes or injuries, professional legal advice may also be appropriate.
