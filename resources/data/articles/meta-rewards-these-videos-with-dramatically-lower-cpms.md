One of the most interesting patterns we discovered while testing creatives on Meta was that **the ads that tried hardest to sell were not necessarily the ads that generated the cheapest traffic.**

In our experience, informational creatives could generate significantly more efficient CPMs when the content was closely aligned with the product, service, or topic on the landing page.

The creative doesn't aggressively push the product.

It doesn't scream *“BIG SALE!”*

It doesn't rely on *“CLEARANCE!”*, *“LIMITED TIME OFFER!”*, or similar promotional language.

Instead, it provides information that is directly relevant to what the user will find after clicking.

That small change in approach can have a significant effect on traffic economics.

## Stop Trying to Sell in the Ad

A common instinct among advertisers is to make the value proposition as aggressive as possible.

They want the user to immediately understand:

**Buy this.**

**Get this discount.**

**Claim this offer.**

**Big sale.**

**Limited availability.**

That approach can work for certain campaigns.

But when the objective is to acquire inexpensive, relevant traffic to informational content, a hard-sell approach isn't always necessary.

Instead of selling the product directly, the creative creates an information gap.

The user sees something relevant to a problem, service, or topic they're already interested in.

They click because they want to **learn more**.

This creates a very different user journey:

**Informational Creative → Relevant User → Click → Relevant Landing Page**

rather than:

**Aggressive Sales Creative → Promotional Click → Landing Page**

For content-driven funnels, the first structure can be significantly more efficient.

## Strong Demand Means You Don't Need to Manufacture Attention

There is another factor that can make informational creatives particularly effective: **the inherent demand for the product or service.**

If people already want the product, service, or information you're advertising, you don't necessarily need to exaggerate the message to get their attention.

Think about the difference between:

**“INSANE DEAL! HUGE DISCOUNT! DON'T MISS OUT!”**

and:

**“What does a roof replacement actually cost?”**

If someone is already researching roof replacement, the second message can be enough to make them stop and click.

The advertiser isn't creating demand from scratch.

**The demand already exists.**

The creative simply needs to connect that existing interest with the information the user is looking for.

This is especially powerful for high-intent verticals. When the underlying topic is already desirable or solves a problem people actively have, aggressive sales language can become unnecessary.

Instead of exaggerating the offer, you can **let the subject matter create the curiosity.**

That's why we pay close attention to the relationship between:

**Existing Demand + Creative Relevance + Landing Page Match**

When all three are strong, the creative doesn't need to scream for attention.

It can simply give the user a compelling reason to learn more.

## Relevance Is More Important Than Hype

The key isn't simply removing words like *“SALE”* or *“DISCOUNT.”*

The entire creative needs to make sense in relation to the landing page.

If the advertisement discusses one topic and the landing page immediately switches to something unrelated, the user experience becomes inconsistent.

But when the two match closely, the transition feels natural.

For example:

**Ad:** Information about the signs that a roof may need replacement.

**Landing page:** An article explaining roof replacement options, costs, and considerations.

The user clicked because the topic was relevant.

The landing page continues the same conversation.

That's very different from an advertisement promising an enormous discount and then sending the user to a general informational article.

## Why Informational Creatives Can Be Powerful

An informational creative doesn't need to convince someone to buy.

It only needs to convince the right person that **the information is worth exploring.**

That distinction is particularly important for traffic arbitrage.

The objective isn't necessarily:

“Convince this person to purchase the product.”

The objective may be:

**“Find people who are genuinely interested in this topic and get them onto relevant content.”**

Once the user reaches the website, the content and related search experience can do the rest.

This is why we often look beyond CTR when evaluating Meta traffic.

A creative with a slightly lower CTR can still be substantially more valuable if it generates:

- Lower CPM
- Lower CPC
- Higher-quality visitors
- Better engagement
- Stronger downstream monetization

## Aggressive Language Can Change the Economics

One of the patterns we've observed is that increasingly promotional language can make the creative less efficient.

Words such as:

**“BIG SALE”**

**“CLEARANCE”**

**“LIMITED TIME”**

**“BUY NOW”**

**“HUGE DISCOUNT”**

can immediately make an advertisement feel like an advertisement.

That isn't inherently bad.

But when the objective is to acquire inexpensive, relevant traffic to informational content, a hard-sell approach may be working against the funnel.

The user isn't necessarily looking to buy.

They may simply be researching a problem.

So instead of trying to force a purchase decision, the creative should match the user's **information-seeking intent**.

## The Creative and Landing Page Should Tell the Same Story

This is perhaps the most important principle.

**Your ad should make the landing page feel like the next logical step.**

If the ad talks about one problem, the landing page should address that problem.

If the ad creates curiosity around a particular service, the landing page should provide information about that service.

If the ad is informational, the landing page should continue being informational.

This creates a consistent path:

**Ad Message → User Expectation → Landing Page → Content → Monetization**

Breaking that chain can hurt performance even when the ad itself looks good.

## Don't Confuse Attention With Relevance

A creative can get attention in many different ways.

You can use exaggerated claims.

You can use shocking language.

You can promise an enormous discount.

You can create urgency.

But attention by itself isn't the objective.

For arbitrage, you need **valuable attention**.

If the user clicks because they genuinely want to learn about the topic, the resulting traffic can behave very differently from a user who clicked simply because an exaggerated headline created a moment of curiosity.

This is why we care about what happens **after the click**, not just what happens inside the ad.

The real question isn't:

**“Did the creative make them click?”**

It's:

**“Did the creative attract the right person?”**

## What We Learned From Testing

The biggest lesson isn't that informational ads are always better.

It's that **creative strategy needs to match the monetization model and the existing demand for the subject.**

If you're selling a product directly, aggressive commercial messaging may make sense.

If you're acquiring traffic for an informational funnel, the objective is different.

You're looking for users who are interested in the topic—not necessarily users who are ready to purchase immediately.

And when the underlying product or service already has strong demand, you don't need to manufacture that desire with exaggerated messaging.

You can simply **connect existing demand with relevant information.**

That changes how you should approach the creative.

Instead of asking:

**“How can I make this ad sell harder?”**

ask:

**“How can I make the right person interested enough to learn more?”**

That shift can completely change the economics of a campaign.
