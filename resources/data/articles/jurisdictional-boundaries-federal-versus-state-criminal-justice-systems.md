The United States operates under a federal system in which criminal laws are enforced through both federal and state governments. Although these systems sometimes address similar conduct, they derive their authority from different sources and operate through distinct courts, prosecutors, law-enforcement agencies, and procedural rules.

Understanding the boundary between federal and state criminal jurisdiction is important because the government prosecuting a case can affect the applicable law, court, sentencing framework, investigative agencies, and potential appellate process. Jurisdiction is not determined simply by the seriousness of an alleged offense. It depends on constitutional authority, federal statutes, state law, the location and circumstances of the conduct, and other legally defined connections to federal jurisdiction.

## The Constitutional Foundation of Federal and State Authority

The federal government does not possess unlimited general criminal-police power. Federal criminal jurisdiction generally depends on authority granted by the Constitution and legislation enacted pursuant to that authority.

Congress has enumerated legislative powers in Article I, including authority over areas such as interstate and foreign commerce, federal property, immigration and naturalization, taxation, and other subjects. The Constitution also establishes federal judicial authority under Article III. ([constitution.congress.gov](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/article-1/))

States, by contrast, retain broad authority to enact and enforce criminal laws within their jurisdictions, subject to the U.S. Constitution and applicable federal law.

The result is a system in which conduct can sometimes violate both state and federal law.

## State Criminal Justice Systems

State governments generally handle the large majority of criminal prosecutions in the United States. State criminal codes cover offenses such as homicide, assault, robbery, burglary, theft, many forms of fraud, and numerous other offenses.

State cases are ordinarily prosecuted by local district attorneys, state's attorneys, county prosecutors, or comparable officials, depending on the state. Law enforcement can involve municipal police departments, county sheriffs, and state agencies.

State courts are organized differently across jurisdictions, but criminal cases commonly proceed through trial-level courts followed by intermediate appellate courts and, in appropriate circumstances, a state's highest court.

The precise terminology and structure vary by state.

## Federal Criminal Jurisdiction

Federal criminal jurisdiction generally arises when Congress has enacted a criminal statute applicable to the conduct and the constitutional basis for federal authority exists.

Federal crimes can include offenses involving federal property, certain forms of interstate or international conduct, federal financial institutions, immigration, customs, federal corruption, certain drug-trafficking offenses, and crimes specifically assigned to federal jurisdiction.

The U.S. Department of Justice explains that federal prosecutors enforce federal criminal laws and that United States Attorneys' Offices prosecute federal offenses within their respective districts. ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/usao))

Federal law enforcement can involve agencies such as the Federal Bureau of Investigation, Drug Enforcement Administration, Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, Homeland Security Investigations, and others, depending on the offense.

## Federal and State Offenses Can Overlap

The same conduct can sometimes violate both federal and state criminal law.

For example, conduct involving fraud may potentially implicate state fraud statutes as well as federal laws addressing particular forms of wire fraud, mail fraud, bank fraud, securities offenses, or other federally regulated activity.

Similarly, drug offenses can be prosecuted under state law, federal law, or both, depending on the circumstances.

The existence of concurrent jurisdiction does not necessarily mean that two governments must prosecute the same conduct. Prosecutorial decisions depend on applicable law, evidence, investigative circumstances, and enforcement priorities.

## The Dual-Sovereignty Principle

One important constitutional doctrine is the dual-sovereignty principle. Under this doctrine, the federal government and a state are separate sovereigns for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause when each derives its authority to prosecute from a separate source of sovereign power.

The Supreme Court reaffirmed the doctrine in *Gamble v. United States*, holding that a state and the federal government may, in appropriate circumstances, prosecute the same conduct under their separate sovereign authority. ([supremecourt.gov](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/18pdf/17-646_7l48.pdf))

The doctrine does not mean that multiple prosecutions are automatically permissible. Its application depends on whether the prosecuting governments qualify as separate sovereigns and whether other constitutional or statutory limitations apply.

## The Role of Federal Courts

Federal criminal cases are generally prosecuted in U.S. District Courts. Federal courts have jurisdiction over cases arising under federal criminal statutes and other matters authorized by federal law.

Federal judges preside over criminal proceedings, while federal prosecutors from the Department of Justice represent the United States.

Federal criminal procedure is governed by constitutional requirements, federal statutes, and the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure. The Federal Rules establish procedures for matters including arraignment, motions, discovery, trial, sentencing, and appeals. ([uscourts.gov](https://www.uscourts.gov/forms-rules/rules-practice-procedure))

## State Courts and State Criminal Procedure

State criminal proceedings operate under state constitutions, statutes, rules of criminal procedure, and applicable federal constitutional requirements.

A state cannot avoid federal constitutional protections simply because a prosecution occurs in state court. Constitutional provisions incorporated against the states can impose requirements concerning counsel, searches and seizures, due process, confrontation, jury trials, and other criminal-procedure protections.

At the same time, states can provide additional protections through their own constitutions and laws.

Consequently, a defendant's rights may depend on both federal constitutional law and the law of the particular state.

## Investigative Jurisdiction

Law-enforcement jurisdiction can also differ from prosecutorial jurisdiction.

A local police department generally operates within legally defined territorial and statutory boundaries. State agencies may possess broader authority within the state, while federal agencies enforce federal law under their respective statutory mandates.

Investigations can nevertheless involve cooperation between agencies. Joint investigations may include federal agents, state investigators, county officials, and local police.

The participation of a federal agency does not automatically mean that a case will ultimately be prosecuted federally. Prosecutorial authority depends on the applicable law and the circumstances of the investigation.

## Interstate and Cross-Border Conduct

Federal jurisdiction can become particularly important when alleged criminal activity crosses state or national boundaries.

Congress has enacted numerous statutes addressing conduct involving interstate commerce, communications, transportation, financial systems, immigration, customs, and international activity.

For example, electronic communications can cross state boundaries even when participants are physically located within different states. Federal statutes may therefore apply where Congress has established the necessary jurisdictional connection.

Cross-border conduct can also involve treaties, extradition procedures, immigration law, and cooperation between governments.

## Federal Sentencing and State Sentencing

Federal and state criminal cases operate under different sentencing structures.

Federal sentencing is governed by federal statutes and, in many cases, the federal sentencing guidelines framework. The U.S. Sentencing Commission publishes the federal guidelines and related sentencing materials. ([ussc.gov](https://www.ussc.gov/guidelines))

States establish their own sentencing laws. Some use sentencing guidelines, some use determinate or indeterminate structures, and others employ combinations of different approaches.

As a result, similar conduct can produce substantially different sentencing consequences depending on the jurisdiction, offense, criminal history, and applicable sentencing law.

## Removal and Immigration Consequences

A criminal prosecution can also have immigration consequences for noncitizens. Federal immigration law can classify certain convictions as grounds for inadmissibility, removal, or other immigration consequences.

This creates an additional layer of complexity when a state criminal case involves a noncitizen. A state court's criminal disposition can interact with federal immigration law even though the underlying prosecution is conducted by state authorities.

The Supreme Court recognized the importance of immigration consequences in *Padilla v. Kentucky*, holding that defense counsel must advise noncitizen defendants about certain potential immigration consequences of a guilty plea. ([supremecourt.gov](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/09pdf/08-6515.pdf))

This does not mean that every criminal conviction automatically creates an immigration consequence. The specific offense, sentence, immigration status, and applicable federal law must be examined.

## Federalism and Allocation of Criminal Authority

The division between federal and state criminal systems reflects the broader constitutional structure of federalism.

States retain broad authority over ordinary criminal-law enforcement, while the federal government exercises specifically authorized powers. Over time, Congress has enacted federal criminal statutes addressing an expanding range of conduct, particularly where interstate or national interests are involved.

The boundary is therefore not completely static. Constitutional interpretation, congressional legislation, Supreme Court decisions, and changes in state law can influence the practical division of criminal jurisdiction.

## Determining Which System Applies

When evaluating a criminal matter, several questions can help identify the relevant jurisdiction:

1. **What law allegedly was violated?** Determine whether the offense is defined under state or federal law.
2. **Where did the conduct occur?** Location can affect territorial jurisdiction.
3. **Did the conduct cross state or national boundaries?** Interstate or international elements can create federal jurisdiction under applicable statutes.
4. **What government investigated the case?** The investigating agency can provide clues but does not alone determine prosecutorial jurisdiction.
5. **Which court issued the charging document?** A federal indictment or information generally indicates a federal prosecution, while state charging documents arise within the state court system.
6. **What sentencing law applies?** Federal and state sentencing systems differ.
7. **Are immigration consequences involved?** Federal immigration law may create consequences even when the underlying criminal case is prosecuted by a state.

These questions can help distinguish jurisdictional issues from other criminal-defense questions.

## Conclusion

Federal and state criminal justice systems operate within the same constitutional framework but possess different sources of authority and institutional structures. State governments generally prosecute offenses under state criminal law, while federal authorities prosecute offenses created by federal law and supported by federal constitutional authority.

Some conduct can fall within both systems, and the dual-sovereignty doctrine recognizes circumstances in which separate sovereigns may prosecute the same conduct. Federal and state systems nevertheless differ in their courts, prosecutors, investigative agencies, procedural rules, sentencing structures, and appellate mechanisms.

For anyone involved in a criminal proceeding, identifying the correct jurisdiction is an important first step. The applicable statute, location of the conduct, charging authority, constitutional protections, and potential collateral consequences all help determine which legal framework governs the case.
