Financial markets operate within a regulatory framework designed to promote orderly trading, protect investors, support market transparency, and establish standards for firms and professionals participating in securities transactions. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) plays a central role in administering and enforcing federal securities laws, while self-regulatory organizations such as FINRA and national securities exchanges operate within the broader regulatory structure.

The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 provides the SEC with authority over significant areas of the securities industry, including broker-dealers, securities exchanges, clearing agencies, transfer agents, and securities self-regulatory organizations. The Act also addresses market conduct, corporate reporting, and other requirements affecting publicly traded securities.

For companies and market participants, securities compliance is therefore not limited to completing registration documents. It can involve trading practices, disclosures, recordkeeping, financial responsibility, market access, reporting, surveillance, and internal controls.

## The Regulatory Structure of Financial Markets

The U.S. securities regulatory system consists of several interconnected participants.

The SEC regulates major securities-market participants, including broker-dealers, national securities exchanges, clearing agencies, transfer agents, and self-regulatory organizations. The SEC's Division of Trading and Markets is responsible for administering rules governing significant aspects of market structure and securities trading.

Self-regulatory organizations also play an important role. National securities exchanges establish and enforce their own rules subject to federal securities regulation, while FINRA regulates its broker-dealer members and performs market-regulation functions.

This structure means that a regulated firm may need to comply simultaneously with federal securities laws, SEC rules, and applicable SRO requirements.

## Securities Exchange Registration

National securities exchanges operate within a formal registration and regulatory framework.

The Exchange Act provides for SEC oversight of securities exchanges and other self-regulatory organizations. SROs must establish rules addressing member conduct and market integrity, while proposed rule changes may be subject to SEC review and public comment depending on the applicable statutory process.

Compliance therefore involves more than operating a marketplace. Exchanges must maintain regulatory programs capable of enforcing applicable rules and supporting fair and orderly markets.

The SEC's 2026 examination priorities specifically state that examinations of national securities exchanges will assess whether exchanges are meeting obligations to enforce compliance with SRO rules and federal securities laws, including obligations associated with National Market System Plans.

## Broker-Dealer Compliance

Broker-dealers are another major component of the securities regulatory system.

The SEC generally requires persons engaged in the business of effecting securities transactions for others or acting as dealers to register unless an applicable exemption applies. The agency's broker-dealer guidance identifies registration, conduct regulation, financial responsibility, books and records, customer protection, and other obligations.

The distinction between a broker and a dealer can be important. A broker generally acts on behalf of others, while a dealer generally buys and sells securities for its own account as part of its business. Certain activities involving securities transactions can also trigger registration requirements even when a business does not describe itself as a traditional brokerage firm.

Companies considering whether their activities require registration should therefore examine the substance of their activities rather than relying solely on business terminology.

## Market Conduct and Antifraud Requirements

Securities laws contain broad restrictions against fraudulent and manipulative conduct.

The SEC explains that federal securities laws prohibit fraudulent activities connected with the offer, purchase, or sale of securities. The Exchange Act also provides the Commission with enforcement authority over prohibited market conduct.

For broker-dealers, compliance can include requirements concerning fair dealing, material disclosures, conflicts of interest, order handling, and other conduct obligations. The SEC's broker-dealer guide explains that broker-dealers must comply with federal antifraud provisions and applicable SRO requirements.

Internal compliance programs should therefore identify activities that could create risks involving misleading statements, manipulation, improper trading, conflicts of interest, or inadequate disclosures.

## Insider Trading Controls

Insider trading compliance is another important component of securities regulation.

The Exchange Act and related federal securities laws prohibit certain transactions involving material nonpublic information when the trader violates an applicable duty to keep that information confidential or refrain from trading.

Companies can address these risks through policies governing confidential information, securities trading by insiders, restricted trading periods, preclearance procedures, and information barriers where appropriate.

A compliance program should also address how sensitive information is handled when employees participate in corporate transactions, financing activities, mergers and acquisitions, financial reporting, or other events that may involve material nonpublic information.

## Corporate Disclosure and Reporting

Public companies have continuing disclosure responsibilities under federal securities laws.

The Exchange Act authorizes the SEC to require periodic reporting by companies with publicly traded securities. Depending on the issuer and security, filings can provide investors with information concerning financial performance, material events, ownership, management, and other matters.

Compliance therefore requires coordination among legal, finance, accounting, investor-relations, and executive personnel.

A disclosure-control system should help identify potentially material information, determine applicable reporting obligations, review filings, and maintain records supporting significant disclosure decisions.

## Financial Responsibility and Customer Protection

Broker-dealer compliance also includes financial-responsibility requirements.

The SEC identifies requirements concerning minimum net capital, customer funds and securities, books and records, and related financial protections. The Customer Protection Rule, for example, addresses possession or control of fully paid and excess-margin securities held for customers.

These requirements are designed to address risks associated with broker-dealer financial distress and the handling of customer assets.

A compliance review should therefore examine not only trading activity but also financial controls and procedures governing customer property.

## Books, Records, and Audit Trails

Accurate records are fundamental to securities compliance.

Broker-dealers are subject to requirements concerning books, records, financial reporting, and other documentation. The SEC's broker-dealer guidance identifies required books and records among the major compliance categories applicable to regulated firms.

Records can help regulators reconstruct transactions, assess compliance, investigate potential misconduct, and evaluate the effectiveness of internal controls.

Firms should therefore establish procedures governing the creation, retention, accessibility, and protection of required records.

## Market Structure and Order Execution

Modern securities markets involve multiple trading venues and complex order-routing arrangements.

Regulation NMS addresses several aspects of U.S. equity-market structure, while SEC rules establish requirements concerning market centers, order handling, and related market practices.

The SEC's 2026 guidance concerning Rule 605 reflects continuing attention to execution-quality information. The amended Rule 605 requirements became subject to a compliance date of August 1, 2026, and require specified market centers to make monthly reports concerning order execution information publicly available.

For firms involved in order routing or execution, compliance may therefore require systems capable of capturing and analyzing transaction-level information accurately.

## Surveillance and Market Integrity

Securities exchanges and other market participants need systems capable of identifying potentially problematic trading activity.

Surveillance programs may examine patterns associated with manipulation, unusual trading, improper use of information, or violations of exchange and securities rules.

The SEC's Division of Trading and Markets states that its mission includes maintaining standards for fair, orderly, and efficient markets and regulating major market participants.

The SEC's examination program also uses a risk-based approach to assess whether firms are complying with federal securities laws and to identify risks affecting investors and markets.

Effective internal surveillance should therefore be aligned with the firm's activities and the risks associated with its particular business model.

## Compliance Examinations

Regulated firms may be subject to examinations by the SEC or applicable self-regulatory organizations.

The SEC's Division of Examinations conducts examinations of investment advisers, investment companies, broker-dealers, self-regulatory organizations, clearing agencies, transfer agents, and other registrants. Its stated objectives include improving compliance, preventing fraud, monitoring risk, and informing regulatory policy.

The SEC also publishes examination priorities and risk alerts to identify areas receiving regulatory attention.

For 2026, the SEC's examination priorities include national securities exchanges and FINRA among the self-regulatory organizations subject to examination. The priorities also address areas such as broker-dealer relationships, disclosures, conflicts, and compliance controls.

## Internal Compliance Programs

An effective securities compliance program generally begins with identifying the organization's regulatory obligations.

A firm can map requirements across areas such as:

- Registration
- Trading activity
- Market access
- Customer protection
- Financial responsibility
- Disclosure
- Insider trading
- Recordkeeping
- Surveillance
- Cybersecurity and operational controls
- Conflicts of interest
- Regulatory reporting

Written policies should be supported by actual procedures, training, monitoring, testing, escalation processes, and documented corrective actions.

A policy that exists only on paper may provide limited protection if employees do not understand it or if the organization cannot demonstrate that it monitors compliance.

## Responding to Regulatory Deficiencies

A compliance audit may identify weaknesses without establishing that unlawful conduct occurred.

For example, a firm may discover incomplete records, inadequate monitoring, inconsistent supervisory procedures, or gaps in employee training. These findings should be evaluated to determine their legal significance and whether corrective action is required.

The SEC publishes risk alerts specifically to communicate observations from examinations and help firms improve their systems, policies, and procedures.

Corrective measures may include updating policies, improving surveillance technology, strengthening supervisory controls, retraining employees, correcting records, or obtaining specialized legal advice.

## Securities Compliance as an Ongoing Process

Financial-market compliance cannot generally be treated as a one-time registration exercise.

Regulatory requirements evolve, market structures change, firms introduce new products and technologies, and regulators periodically update their examination priorities. The SEC's Trading and Markets division currently publishes updated guidance and regulatory materials covering areas such as Regulation SHO, Regulation NMS, Treasury clearing, electronic recordkeeping, and other market-structure issues.

Enterprises operating in securities markets should therefore periodically reassess whether their policies and controls remain appropriate for their current activities.

## Understanding Securities Exchange Compliance

Securities exchange compliance combines several layers of legal and operational responsibility. Exchanges must maintain appropriate regulatory programs, broker-dealers must satisfy registration and conduct requirements, public companies must meet applicable disclosure obligations, and market participants must maintain controls addressing trading, records, customer assets, and market integrity.

The SEC provides the primary federal regulatory framework, while self-regulatory organizations such as FINRA and national securities exchanges contribute additional rules and oversight.

For enterprises operating in financial markets, the practical challenge is translating these requirements into functioning compliance systems. That can involve legal review, documented policies, employee training, transaction monitoring, recordkeeping, internal testing, and continuing communication with regulators.

Because securities regulation is highly fact-specific and can involve federal statutes, SEC rules, SRO requirements, and jurisdiction-specific obligations, companies should obtain qualified securities-law advice when evaluating registration, reporting, trading, or compliance questions.
