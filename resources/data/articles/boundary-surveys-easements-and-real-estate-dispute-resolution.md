Real estate disputes frequently arise from uncertainty about property boundaries, access rights, easements, encroachments, title records, or the location of improvements. These issues can become particularly complicated when the physical characteristics of a property differ from the legal description contained in a deed or when neighboring owners have conflicting interpretations of their respective rights.

Boundary surveys can provide important factual information about the location of property lines and improvements. Easements can establish rights to use another person's land for particular purposes. When disagreements cannot be resolved through negotiation, parties may turn to mediation, arbitration, litigation, or other property-dispute procedures.

Because real property law is primarily governed by state law in the United States, the legal rules concerning boundaries, easements, recording, adverse possession, and dispute resolution can vary considerably between jurisdictions.

## Understanding Boundary Surveys

A boundary survey is used to determine the location of property lines and corners based on the legal description of the property and applicable surveying standards.

The American Bar Association explains that a survey can show boundary lines, improvements, and encroachments affecting the property. A survey can also identify matters such as utilities, shared driveways, and easements.

A boundary survey can therefore provide information that may not be apparent from simply examining a deed or walking the property.

For example, a fence may appear to establish a boundary even though the legally established property line is located several feet away. Similarly, a driveway may cross a neighboring parcel even though the parties have different understandings about whether a legal right of access exists.

## The Importance of the Legal Description

Property boundaries are generally established through legal descriptions contained in deeds and other recorded instruments.

A legal description may use a metes-and-bounds description, a lot and block reference, a recorded subdivision plat, or another legally recognized method.

A surveyor uses the applicable legal description together with relevant records, monuments, measurements, and other evidence to locate the boundaries on the ground.

A discrepancy between a deed description and physical conditions does not automatically mean that the deed is incorrect. The legal significance of a discrepancy depends on the applicable property law and the evidence concerning the property's history.

## Surveys in Real Estate Transactions

Surveys can be particularly important during property purchases and refinancing.

The American Bar Association notes that lenders commonly require surveys because a survey can help confirm that the legal description in the deed corresponds to the property the buyer expects to acquire. Surveys can also identify encroachments, easements, access issues, and other matters that could affect title or property use.

Commercial transactions may require more detailed surveys.

The ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey standards establish minimum requirements for surveys used in many commercial real estate transactions where title companies, lenders, clients, and surveyors need consistent information about property boundaries and title-related matters. The 2026 standards became effective February 23, 2026.

## Identifying Encroachments

An encroachment occurs when an improvement or structure extends across a property boundary or into an area subject to another property interest.

Examples can include:

- A building extending onto neighboring land;
- A fence crossing a property line;
- A driveway extending across a boundary;
- Landscaping or retaining walls occupying another parcel;
- Structures extending into an easement; or
- Improvements constructed over access or utility areas.

Encroachments can create problems during purchases, refinancing, development, or disputes between neighboring owners.

A survey can help identify these conditions before they become more significant.

The American Bar Association notes that survey-related issues such as boundary disputes, unrecorded easements, and encroachments can delay transactions and affect the potential use and value of property.

## Understanding Easements

An easement is a nonpossessory property interest that gives one party the right to use another person's land for a particular purpose.

Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute describes an easement as an interest allowing the holder to use another person's land. Easements can be affirmative, allowing particular uses, or negative, restricting certain uses of the burdened property.

Common examples include easements for:

- Driveways;
- Roads and access;
- Utilities;
- Drainage;
- Pipelines;
- Telecommunications;
- Maintenance; and
- Other specifically authorized uses.

The scope of an easement generally depends on the instrument creating it and the applicable law.

## Dominant and Servient Properties

An easement can involve two parcels with different legal roles.

The property benefiting from an easement is commonly referred to as the dominant estate, while the property burdened by the easement is the servient estate.

For example, if Parcel A has a recorded right to use a driveway crossing Parcel B, Parcel A may be the dominant estate and Parcel B the servient estate.

Some easements are appurtenant, meaning that the right is connected to a particular parcel rather than simply to an individual person. Such rights can generally pass with the property when ownership changes, subject to applicable law and the terms of the easement.

## Recorded and Unrecorded Easements

An easement may be reflected in recorded property documents, but disputes can arise when an alleged easement is not clearly documented in the public record.

The American Bar Association notes that if a survey identifies an easement for which there is no corresponding recorded document, a purchaser may need to investigate where the survey information came from. An unrecorded easement can also become an exception to title-insurance coverage depending on the circumstances and policy terms.

Recording systems are intended to provide notice of property interests, but the rules governing priority and enforceability differ among jurisdictions.

Cornell's Legal Information Institute explains that recording a document does not necessarily guarantee that the underlying claim is legally valid. Recording offices generally do not independently determine whether every recorded property interest is legally correct.

## Easement Scope and Use

A dispute may arise even when the existence of an easement is undisputed.

The parties may disagree about:

- Where the easement is located;
- How wide it is;
- Who may use it;
- What activities are permitted;
- Who must maintain it;
- Whether vehicles may use it;
- Whether improvements can be placed within it; or
- Whether the easement has been modified or abandoned.

The language of the creating instrument is therefore important.

A party generally cannot assume that an easement authorizes every conceivable use of the affected property. The scope of the right depends on the applicable legal rules and the circumstances under which the easement was created.

## Boundary Disputes Between Neighbors

Boundary disputes commonly arise when owners have conflicting understandings about the location of a property line.

A disagreement may involve a fence, driveway, building, landscaping, retaining wall, tree line, or other physical feature.

The parties may each possess documents supporting their respective positions, making professional surveying and title research important.

The first step is often to establish the factual location of the boundary through an appropriate survey and review of the relevant property records.

A survey does not necessarily resolve every legal question. It can establish important physical and technical information, while legal counsel may be needed to determine the legal effect of deeds, easements, adverse possession claims, or other property doctrines.

## Title Research and Chain of Ownership

Property disputes can also require examination of the property's chain of title.

A title search can identify deeds, easements, mortgages, liens, restrictions, and other recorded interests affecting the property.

The recording system is particularly relevant because recorded documents can provide notice to subsequent purchasers. Cornell's Legal Information Institute explains that a title search is generally used to examine the chain of title and identify competing interests affecting ownership.

Where older deeds contain inconsistent descriptions or overlapping legal descriptions, historical title research may be necessary to understand how the present dispute developed.

## Negotiating a Boundary Agreement

Not every boundary disagreement requires litigation.

If neighboring owners are willing to cooperate, they may negotiate an agreement addressing the location or use of the disputed area.

Depending on state law, a boundary agreement may require a survey, written instrument, signatures, notarization, recording, or other formalities.

A properly documented agreement can provide greater certainty for future owners than an informal understanding.

Because recording and property-transfer rules differ by jurisdiction, parties should obtain appropriate legal advice before relying on an informal boundary arrangement.

## Mediation of Real Estate Disputes

Mediation can provide an alternative to litigation when parties are willing to negotiate.

A neutral mediator can help the parties identify the disputed issues, exchange information, and explore potential solutions without acting as a judge.

Possible negotiated solutions may include:

- Relocating a fence;
- Establishing an agreed boundary;
- Granting or modifying an easement;
- Creating a maintenance agreement;
- Establishing access arrangements;
- Removing an encroachment;
- Purchasing a narrow strip of land; or
- Recording a formal settlement agreement.

The suitability of mediation depends on the nature of the dispute and the willingness of the parties to participate.

## Arbitration and Litigation

When negotiation and mediation do not resolve a dispute, arbitration or litigation may become necessary.

The appropriate procedure depends on the jurisdiction, contractual agreements, nature of the property interest, and relief being requested.

Real estate litigation can involve claims concerning boundaries, easements, access, title, adverse possession, encroachments, or other property interests. The American Bar Association identifies boundaries, easements, access, and title among the subjects commonly encountered in real estate litigation.

A court may be asked to determine the parties' rights, establish or confirm a boundary, interpret an easement, determine title, or order other appropriate relief under applicable law.

## Quiet Title Actions

A quiet title proceeding can be used in some jurisdictions when competing claims to property ownership or interests need judicial resolution.

The purpose is generally to obtain a judicial determination concerning title or competing interests in real property.

Quiet title procedures vary substantially by state. The availability of the action, required parties, notice requirements, evidentiary standards, and resulting remedies are governed by applicable state law.

Because a title dispute can affect future transfers and financing, resolving the recorded title issue may be important beyond the immediate disagreement between neighboring owners.

## Adverse Possession and Boundary Claims

Some boundary disputes involve adverse possession or similar doctrines.

Adverse possession laws can allow ownership interests to arise from qualifying possession of land for a statutory period when specified legal requirements are satisfied. The exact requirements vary considerably among jurisdictions.

A fence or other encroachment that has existed for many years therefore may raise legal questions beyond the original location of the deeded boundary.

The existence of long-term physical occupation does not automatically establish adverse possession. The claimant generally must satisfy the requirements of the applicable jurisdiction.

## Title Insurance and Survey Issues

Title insurance can provide protection against certain covered title risks, but it does not automatically cover every boundary or easement dispute.

The American Bar Association explains that title companies may rely on surveys to identify boundary discrepancies, easements, encroachments, and other matters affecting title. Survey-related exceptions and endorsements can vary by policy and jurisdiction.

A purchaser should therefore review both the survey and the title commitment or policy rather than assuming that title insurance eliminates every survey-related risk.

Particular attention may be appropriate for exceptions involving:

- Easements;
- Encroachments;
- Boundary discrepancies;
- Rights of parties in possession;
- Unrecorded interests; and
- Access.

## Resolving a Dispute Before Closing

Boundary and easement issues discovered during a transaction can affect whether the transaction proceeds.

A buyer or lender may require clarification before closing if the survey identifies an encroachment, access problem, conflicting boundary, or easement that affects the intended use of the property.

Possible responses can include obtaining a corrected survey, recording an agreement, obtaining an easement, modifying a title commitment, negotiating with a neighboring owner, or resolving the matter through a formal legal proceeding.

The American Bar Association notes that survey issues can delay or even derail transactions when they affect marketability or intended property use.

## Documenting the Resolution

Once a boundary or easement dispute has been resolved, documentation is critical.

Depending on the agreement and jurisdiction, the parties may need:

- A boundary-line agreement;
- Easement agreement;
- Corrective deed;
- Quitclaim deed;
- Survey or survey plat;
- Release;
- Settlement agreement;
- Court order; or
- Other recorded instrument.

The purpose is to create a reliable record of the parties' rights and reduce the possibility of the same dispute resurfacing after a property transfer.

## Preventing Future Property Disputes

Many real estate disputes can be reduced through careful due diligence before acquisition or construction.

Property owners and purchasers can consider:

1. Obtaining an appropriate survey.
2. Reviewing the legal description carefully.
3. Examining the chain of title.
4. Identifying recorded easements.
5. Investigating apparent unrecorded rights.
6. Checking for encroachments.
7. Confirming legal access.
8. Reviewing title-insurance exceptions.
9. Documenting agreements with neighboring owners.
10. Recording legally significant instruments when required.

The 2026 ALTA/NSPS standards specifically emphasize matters such as boundaries, easements, encroachments, access, and legal descriptions in the context of land-title surveys.

## Conclusion

Boundary surveys and easement documentation provide important tools for identifying and managing real estate rights. A survey can help establish the physical relationship between property lines and improvements, while title research can reveal recorded interests affecting ownership and use.

Easements can create valuable access, utility, maintenance, and other rights, but disputes can arise concerning their location, scope, maintenance, or continued validity. Boundary disagreements can similarly involve both technical surveying questions and legal issues concerning deeds, title history, adverse possession, or other property doctrines.

When disputes arise, parties may be able to resolve them through direct negotiation, professionally prepared agreements, mediation, or other alternative dispute-resolution processes. More complex disputes may require litigation or a judicial determination of title or property rights.

Because real property law is primarily governed by state law, the rules concerning surveys, easements, recording, adverse possession, title, and dispute resolution can differ substantially between jurisdictions. Professional surveying and qualified legal advice can therefore be important when a boundary or easement issue could affect ownership, development, financing, or the future use of real property.
