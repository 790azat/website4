Commercial trucking in the United States is subject to a broad set of federal and state safety requirements. These rules address areas such as driver qualifications, hours of service, vehicle maintenance, cargo securement, insurance, and the safe operation of commercial motor vehicles.

These regulations can also become relevant after a truck accident. Investigators, insurers, attorneys, and regulators may examine whether a driver or motor carrier complied with applicable safety requirements when evaluating what happened.

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) administers many of the federal regulations governing interstate commercial motor carriers. Its regulations cover areas including driver qualifications, commercial-vehicle operation, equipment, hours of service, inspection and maintenance, and hazardous-material transportation.

## What Is a Commercial Motor Vehicle?

The term **commercial motor vehicle (CMV)** covers certain vehicles used in interstate commerce that meet specific size, passenger, or hazardous-material criteria.

In general, FMCSA rules apply to many commercial vehicles that:

- Weigh or have a gross vehicle weight rating of 10,001 pounds or more
- Transport certain numbers of passengers
- Transport hazardous materials requiring placards

The precise definition and applicable exemptions depend on the vehicle and operation.

Not every truck on the road is subject to exactly the same federal requirements. Intrastate operations may also be governed by state regulations.

## Who Is Responsible for Trucking Safety?

Responsibility for compliance can involve several parties.

Depending on the circumstances, these may include:

- The truck driver
- The motor carrier
- A vehicle owner
- A leasing company
- A maintenance provider
- A cargo loader
- A shipper
- Other businesses involved in the transportation operation

The exact allocation of responsibility depends on the regulation involved, the contractual relationships, and the facts of the incident.

A trucking company cannot necessarily avoid regulatory responsibility simply because a driver was the person physically operating the vehicle.

For example, FMCSA guidance states that a motor carrier can be responsible for hours-of-service violations when it had, or should have had, the means to detect those violations.

## Hours-of-Service Requirements

Hours-of-service (HOS) rules are designed to limit the amount of time certain commercial drivers can spend driving or on duty without required rest.

FMCSA describes HOS requirements as rules governing the maximum amount of time drivers may be on duty, including driving time, as well as required rest periods. In general, covered commercial motor vehicle drivers and carriers must comply with 49 CFR Part 395, subject to applicable exceptions.

These rules are intended to address driver fatigue and help ensure that commercial drivers receive appropriate opportunities for rest.

Records relating to a driver's hours can become relevant after a collision if there is a question about fatigue or compliance.

## Electronic Logging Devices

Many commercial drivers use electronic logging devices (ELDs) to record information related to hours of service.

ELDs can provide information about:

- Driving periods
- On-duty time
- Off-duty periods
- Vehicle movement
- Certain changes to duty status

Not every driver or operation is subject to identical ELD requirements, because federal rules contain exceptions.

When a truck accident occurs, investigators may review available records when they are relevant to determining how the vehicle was being operated.

## Driver Qualification Requirements

Commercial motor carriers must also comply with rules concerning driver qualifications.

Depending on the operation, qualification requirements can address matters such as:

- Licensing
- Driving experience
- Medical qualifications
- Driving records
- Required examinations
- Employer records

The purpose is to ensure that drivers operating covered commercial vehicles meet applicable requirements.

A violation of a qualification rule does not automatically establish civil liability for every accident. However, evidence of regulatory noncompliance can become relevant depending on the facts and legal claims involved.

## Vehicle Inspection and Maintenance

Commercial vehicles must be maintained in safe operating condition.

FMCSA's maintenance rules require motor carriers and certain intermodal equipment providers to systematically inspect, repair, and maintain commercial motor vehicles under their control. Parts and accessories that affect safe operation must remain in safe and proper operating condition.

Maintenance can involve components such as:

- Brakes
- Tires
- Steering systems
- Wheels and rims
- Suspension
- Axles
- Frames
- Lighting
- Other safety-related equipment

Maintenance records can become important after a crash when mechanical condition is relevant to the accident.

## Pre-Trip and Inspection Requirements

Commercial vehicles can be subject to inspection requirements designed to identify safety problems.

Drivers and carriers may have responsibilities concerning vehicle inspections and defects.

If a truck has a known mechanical problem that affects safe operation, the circumstances surrounding that defect can become important in an investigation.

For example, investigators may ask:

- When was the vehicle last inspected?
- Was the defect previously documented?
- Was the problem repaired?
- Who performed the repair?
- Was the vehicle operated afterward?
- Did the defect contribute to the collision?

These questions are fact-specific and do not automatically establish liability.

## Cargo Securement Rules

Cargo must be properly loaded and secured.

FMCSA's cargo-securement rules are intended to prevent cargo from shifting within or falling from commercial motor vehicles. The federal requirements include standards concerning securement devices, blocking and bracing, working-load limits, and the number and placement of tiedowns.

Cargo that is not adequately secured can potentially create serious hazards.

An investigation may therefore examine:

- What cargo was being transported
- Who loaded it
- How it was secured
- Whether appropriate securement devices were used
- Whether the cargo shifted
- Whether cargo fell from the vehicle

Different commodities can have additional securement requirements.

## Hazardous Materials

Trucking regulations also contain specialized requirements for vehicles transporting hazardous materials.

Depending on the material and circumstances, requirements can address:

- Packaging
- Marking
- Placarding
- Routing
- Loading
- Vehicle operation
- Emergency procedures

Hazardous-material transportation can involve additional federal requirements beyond the rules applicable to ordinary freight.

## Insurance and Financial Responsibility

Commercial motor carriers may also be subject to federal financial-responsibility requirements.

FMCSA states that entities seeking certain operating authority must maintain specified minimum levels of financial responsibility, with requirements varying according to factors such as the type of entity, operating authority, cargo, and vehicle.

Federal requirements can therefore affect the insurance or financial-security arrangements maintained by certain motor carriers.

The existence of required insurance does not automatically establish that a carrier is legally responsible for a particular collision.

Liability and insurance coverage are separate questions.

## How Regulations Can Become Relevant to an Accident Claim

Suppose a truck crashes into a passenger vehicle.

The investigation may examine the driver's actions and the condition of the truck, but it may also consider regulatory compliance.

Potential questions could include:

- Was the driver properly qualified?
- Were hours-of-service rules followed?
- Was the vehicle properly maintained?
- Were required inspections completed?
- Was cargo secured?
- Was the truck operating within applicable weight restrictions?
- Was the driver complying with applicable traffic rules?
- Did a mechanical defect contribute to the collision?

The answers can provide context for understanding the accident.

However, a regulatory violation and civil liability are not necessarily identical concepts. A lawyer or court may need to determine whether a particular violation is relevant to the legal elements of the claim under the applicable jurisdiction.

## Evidence Investigators May Review

A serious truck accident can generate substantial documentation.

Potential evidence can include:

- Driver logs
- ELD records
- Inspection reports
- Maintenance records
- Repair invoices
- Vehicle photographs
- Dashcam footage
- GPS information
- Dispatch records
- Cargo documents
- Driver qualification records
- Police reports
- Witness statements
- Insurance records

Not every case will involve all of these sources.

The relevant evidence depends on the type of accident and the questions being investigated.

## Can a Regulation Violation Establish Negligence?

Not necessarily.

A violation of a federal trucking regulation may be evidence relevant to a negligence claim, but the legal effect can vary by jurisdiction and by the particular regulation.

Some courts may consider regulatory violations as evidence of negligence, while other circumstances may involve different legal doctrines or standards.

Therefore, it is generally inaccurate to state that **“a trucking violation automatically means the carrier is liable.”**

Liability normally requires analysis of the applicable law and facts.

## Employer and Driver Liability

Another important issue is determining which party may be legally responsible for a driver's conduct.

A truck driver may work directly for a motor carrier, operate under another business arrangement, or be involved in a more complicated commercial relationship.

Potential questions include:

- Who employed the driver?
- Who controlled the vehicle?
- Who maintained the vehicle?
- Who dispatched the driver?
- Who owned or leased the truck?
- Which company held the relevant operating authority?

The answers can affect which parties may need to be investigated.

## What Happens After a Truck Accident?

Following a serious collision, the investigation may involve several organizations.

Law enforcement may investigate the crash itself.

Regulatory authorities may investigate potential safety violations.

Insurance companies may investigate coverage, liability, and damages.

Attorneys may investigate evidence relevant to a civil claim.

These investigations can overlap, but they serve different purposes.

A regulatory investigation does not necessarily determine whether a private party is entitled to compensation in a civil lawsuit.

## Preserving Trucking Evidence

Some trucking records can be especially important because they may exist electronically or be maintained for limited periods.

After a serious collision, relevant parties may need to identify and preserve potentially important evidence.

This can include:

- Electronic logs
- Vehicle data
- Camera footage
- Maintenance records
- Inspection records
- Dispatch communications
- GPS records
- Cargo documentation

In a significant injury case, an attorney may send a preservation or litigation-hold request to relevant parties.

## When Legal Advice May Be Useful

Legal advice may be particularly useful after a commercial truck collision involving:

- Serious injuries
- Fatalities
- Disputed liability
- Multiple potentially responsible parties
- Questions about driver fatigue
- Suspected maintenance problems
- Cargo-related issues
- Unclear insurance coverage
- Significant financial losses
- A government or regulatory investigation
- A potential lawsuit

A lawyer can evaluate the evidence and determine which legal theories and deadlines may apply.

## Final Takeaway

Commercial trucking is governed by a combination of federal and state requirements covering areas such as driver qualifications, hours of service, vehicle maintenance, cargo securement, safe operation, and financial responsibility.

FMCSA regulations provide an important federal framework for interstate commercial motor carriers.

After a truck collision, compliance with those requirements may become part of the factual investigation. Driver logs, maintenance records, inspection documentation, cargo records, vehicle data, and other evidence can help establish what occurred.

However, a safety violation does not automatically determine civil liability. Whether a truck driver, carrier, owner, maintenance provider, or another party is legally responsible depends on the applicable law and the specific evidence.

For anyone involved in a serious commercial-trucking collision, preserving evidence and obtaining advice based on the law of the jurisdiction where the accident occurred can be important.
