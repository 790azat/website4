Comparative fault is a central concept in modern U.S. negligence law. When more than one person may have contributed to an accident or injury, a court or jury may need to determine how much responsibility belongs to each party. In jurisdictions using comparative negligence, the injured person's own share of fault can reduce the damages that may be recovered.

The rules are not uniform across the United States. Some jurisdictions use **pure comparative negligence**, while others use **modified comparative negligence** with a percentage threshold. A small number of jurisdictions continue to use traditional contributory negligence rules.

Understanding these differences is important because the same underlying facts can produce different legal consequences depending on the applicable state law.

## What Is Comparative Fault?

Comparative fault is a method of allocating responsibility among parties whose conduct contributed to an injury.

For example, suppose a jury determines that an injured driver suffered $100,000 in damages. If the driver is found 20% responsible and another driver is found 80% responsible, a comparative negligence system may reduce the injured driver's recovery to $80,000.

The basic concept is that damages are adjusted according to the plaintiff's percentage of responsibility. Cornell's Legal Information Institute describes comparative negligence as a tort principle that reduces recoverable damages according to the degree of negligence attributable to the parties.

Comparative fault generally involves two separate questions:

1. **Did each party act negligently or otherwise contribute to the harm?**
2. **What percentage of responsibility should be assigned to each party?**

The second question becomes particularly important when several people or entities may have contributed to the same injury.

## Pure Comparative Negligence

Under a **pure comparative negligence** system, an injured plaintiff's recovery is reduced according to the plaintiff's percentage of fault, but the plaintiff is not automatically barred from recovery because their percentage is high.

For example:

- Total damages: $200,000
- Plaintiff's fault: 30%
- Defendant's fault: 70%
- Potential recovery: $140,000

Even if the plaintiff were found 80% responsible, a pure comparative negligence system could still allow recovery of the remaining 20%, subject to other applicable rules.

Cornell's Legal Information Institute identifies California, Florida before its 2023 statutory change, and New York as examples of jurisdictions that have used or use pure comparative negligence frameworks. Florida subsequently changed its general negligence standard to modified comparative negligence.

The practical effect is that the plaintiff's percentage of responsibility generally affects **how much can be recovered**, rather than automatically determining whether recovery is possible.

## Modified Comparative Negligence

Modified comparative negligence also reduces damages based on the plaintiff's assigned percentage of fault, but it adds a threshold beyond which recovery is barred.

There are two commonly described versions.

### The 50% Bar

Under a 50% bar system, a plaintiff may be prevented from recovering if the plaintiff is found to be **50% or more at fault**.

For example, if a plaintiff is 49% responsible, the plaintiff may remain eligible to recover the defendant's corresponding share of damages. If the plaintiff is 50% responsible, however, the jurisdiction's rule may bar recovery.

The precise wording of a state's statute or case law matters, particularly around whether the threshold is triggered at 50% or only above 50%.

### The 51% Bar

Under a 51% bar system, the plaintiff generally cannot recover when the plaintiff's fault reaches **51% or more**.

A plaintiff found 50% responsible may therefore still recover, with damages reduced by the plaintiff's percentage. A plaintiff found 51% responsible may be barred.

The American Bar Association notes that states have adopted different comparative-fault approaches and that the effect of a plaintiff being more responsible than the defendant depends on the jurisdiction.

Because state statutes and judicial interpretations differ, the actual rule should be checked in the jurisdiction governing the claim.

## How Fault Is Allocated

Fault allocation generally requires the fact finder to examine the conduct of the parties and determine how that conduct contributed to the injury.

Evidence can include:

- Police or incident reports
- Photographs and videos
- Witness testimony
- Accident reconstruction evidence
- Medical records
- Expert testimony
- Employment or financial records
- Vehicle or property records
- Electronic or digital evidence
- Applicable safety rules and regulations
- Statements made by the parties

A traffic citation, safety violation, or regulatory violation can be relevant evidence, but it does not necessarily resolve the separate civil question of negligence or comparative fault.

Likewise, compliance with a regulation does not automatically eliminate the possibility of civil liability if other evidence establishes that a party acted negligently.

## Comparative Fault and Multiple Defendants

Comparative fault becomes more complicated when several defendants are involved.

Consider an accident in which a plaintiff alleges that a negligent driver, vehicle manufacturer, and property owner all contributed to the injury. A fact finder may need to determine whether each party was responsible and, where applicable, how responsibility should be divided.

The resulting allocation can interact with a state's rules governing **joint and several liability** or **several liability**.

Under pure several liability, for example, a defendant generally is responsible only for the portion of damages attributed to that defendant. Other jurisdictions use various forms of joint and several liability or hybrid approaches.

Consequently, determining percentages of fault is not always the final step. The state's rules concerning collection of the judgment can also affect the amount actually recoverable from each defendant.

## Fault Assigned to Nonparties

Some jurisdictions permit courts or juries to consider the conduct of people or entities that are not defendants in the lawsuit.

For example, a defendant might argue that another person who is not part of the litigation contributed to the accident. Whether and how that person's fault can be considered depends on the governing jurisdiction.

This issue can become important when:

- Another driver left the scene;
- A contractor or subcontractor contributed to an accident;
- A manufacturer or distributor is not named as a defendant;
- An employer may have contributed to an incident;
- A property owner is not part of the lawsuit.

Because nonparty-fault rules vary substantially, the applicable statute and case law should be reviewed before assuming that a particular person's conduct will be included in the allocation.

## Comparative Fault in Settlement Negotiations

Comparative fault can affect settlement discussions even before a case reaches trial.

An insurer or defendant may argue that the plaintiff contributed to the accident or injury. The plaintiff may dispute that assessment and present evidence supporting a different allocation.

For example, in a $150,000 claim, the parties might disagree about whether the plaintiff was:

- 10% responsible;
- 30% responsible; or
- 50% responsible.

The significance of those percentages depends on the jurisdiction's comparative-fault system and other applicable rules.

Settlement discussions may therefore focus not only on the total value of the claimed damages but also on the disputed allocation of responsibility.

## Comparative Fault Is Different From Contributory Negligence

Comparative negligence should not be confused with traditional **contributory negligence**.

Under traditional contributory negligence, a plaintiff's own negligence could completely bar recovery, even when the plaintiff's contribution to the accident was relatively small. Cornell's Legal Information Institute identifies Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, and Virginia as jurisdictions retaining contributory negligence, while noting that the District of Columbia also follows the doctrine.

The distinction can be illustrated simply:

| System | General effect of plaintiff's fault |
| --- | --- |
| Pure comparative negligence | Recovery is reduced according to the plaintiff's fault |
| Modified comparative negligence | Recovery is reduced, but a specified fault threshold can bar recovery |
| Contributory negligence | Plaintiff's negligence can generally bar recovery entirely |

These are broad descriptions rather than substitutes for examining the law of a particular state.

## Why the Applicable Jurisdiction Matters

Comparative-fault rules can differ not only between states but also across different categories of claims.

A state's treatment of automobile accidents may not necessarily be identical to its rules for medical negligence, product liability, premises liability, workers' compensation, or claims against governmental entities.

Other doctrines can also interact with comparative fault, including:

- Assumption of risk;
- Failure to mitigate damages;
- Statutory defenses;
- Special liability statutes;
- Governmental immunity;
- Workers' compensation exclusivity;
- Joint and several liability;
- Contribution among defendants.

For that reason, identifying the governing jurisdiction is an important first step before applying a comparative-fault theory to a specific dispute.

## How Comparative Fault May Affect a Claim

Someone evaluating a negligence claim should generally preserve evidence addressing both the defendant's conduct and their own conduct.

Useful questions include:

- What happened immediately before the incident?
- What actions did each person take?
- Were traffic, workplace, property, or safety rules involved?
- Were there witnesses or recordings?
- Did another person contribute to the event?
- What evidence supports the claimed percentage of fault?
- Which state's law applies?
- Does that jurisdiction use pure or modified comparative negligence?
- Are there special rules governing multiple defendants or nonparties?

These questions can help identify factual disputes that may become important during negotiations, discovery, or trial.

## Final Takeaway

Comparative fault systems attempt to account for the responsibility of multiple parties rather than treating every negligence dispute as an all-or-nothing question. Pure comparative negligence generally permits recovery reduced by the plaintiff's percentage of fault, while modified comparative negligence can impose a threshold that prevents recovery once the plaintiff reaches a specified level of responsibility.

The details matter. Rules concerning the fault threshold, multiple defendants, nonparties, joint and several liability, and particular types of claims vary by jurisdiction. Florida, for example, changed its general negligence framework from pure comparative negligence to modified comparative negligence in 2023, illustrating why older descriptions of state law should be checked against current statutes and case law.

When comparative fault is disputed, the relevant jurisdiction's current law and the evidence concerning each party's conduct are critical to understanding how damages may ultimately be allocated.
