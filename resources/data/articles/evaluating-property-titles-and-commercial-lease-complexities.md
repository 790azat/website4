Commercial real estate transactions often involve two closely connected areas of legal due diligence: determining whether a property can be transferred with acceptable title and evaluating the contractual obligations attached to its lease arrangements. A property may appear commercially attractive while carrying easements, liens, restrictive covenants, unresolved ownership issues, or lease obligations that materially affect its value and usability.

Title examination and lease review therefore serve different but complementary purposes. Title analysis focuses primarily on ownership and interests affecting the property, while lease analysis examines the contractual relationship governing possession, use, payments, maintenance, and other obligations. Both require careful review of documents and applicable jurisdictional rules.

## Understanding the Property Title

A title examination generally begins by tracing the property's ownership history through recorded instruments. Deeds are reviewed to establish the chain of title, while additional searches may identify mortgages, liens, easements, restrictions, leases, and other interests affecting the property.

The American Bar Association describes title examination as a process involving both the reconstruction of the chain of title and a separate review of instruments affecting identified owners and the property. Separating these stages can help reduce the risk of overlooking documents. [1]

Common title issues include:

- Gaps or inconsistencies in the chain of title;
- Unreleased mortgages or other liens;
- Easements affecting access or development;
- Restrictive covenants;
- Encumbrances created by prior owners;
- Probate or estate-related ownership questions;
- Errors in legal descriptions;
- Mechanic's liens and other recorded claims;
- Restrictions on property use; and
- Interests held by parties other than the current record owner.

The significance of any particular issue depends on the property, transaction, financing arrangements, and governing law.

## Reviewing the Title Commitment

In jurisdictions where title insurance is used, a title commitment can identify matters that must be resolved before a policy is issued as well as exceptions that may remain outside coverage.

A title commitment should not automatically be treated as a legal opinion guaranteeing that ownership is free from every possible defect. ALTA explains that a title commitment is an offer to issue a title insurance policy subject to its conditions and requirements, rather than a warranty or independent representation of title. [2]

Accordingly, buyers and their advisers should examine the commitment together with the underlying recorded documents. An exception that appears relatively technical may become commercially significant if it affects access, construction, financing, leasing, or the property's intended use.

## Easements, Restrictions, and Access Rights

Commercial properties frequently depend on rights that extend beyond the boundaries of the parcel itself. Access easements, utility easements, parking arrangements, reciprocal easement agreements, and other recorded instruments can determine how a property functions.

For example, a shopping center may depend on shared access roads and parking areas. An office development may rely on recorded agreements governing maintenance, utilities, signage, or common facilities.

These documents should be reviewed alongside the proposed transaction and lease. The American Bar Association notes that commercial real estate analysis can require examination of leases, easements, covenants, and use restrictions as part of broader due diligence. [3]

An easement that does not prevent ownership may nevertheless limit development or impose continuing obligations.

## The Commercial Lease as a Separate Layer of Risk

A commercial lease creates a contractual framework that can be substantially more complicated than a simple obligation to pay rent. Depending on the property and transaction, the lease may address operating expenses, taxes, insurance, repairs, construction, permitted uses, assignment, subleasing, renewal, termination, casualty, indemnification, and compliance requirements.

The ABA identifies rent, operating expenses, taxes, late charges, delivery obligations, repair and maintenance responsibilities, casualty, insurance, assignment, and subletting among the significant subjects that may require review during commercial lease negotiations. [4]

For a purchaser acquiring leased property, reviewing the lease is also important because the buyer may acquire the property subject to existing tenant rights and landlord obligations.

## Rent, Operating Expenses, and CAM Charges

Commercial leases frequently distinguish between base rent and additional amounts payable for operating expenses. These may include taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, and common-area maintenance, often referred to as CAM charges.

The structure can materially affect the tenant's actual occupancy cost. A lease with comparatively attractive base rent may produce significantly higher expenses if operating-cost provisions are broad or escalation mechanisms are substantial.

Review should therefore address:

1. How operating expenses are defined;
2. Which costs are expressly excluded;
3. How the tenant's share is calculated;
4. Whether controllable expenses are capped;
5. How annual reconciliations operate;
6. Whether the tenant has audit rights; and
7. How expenses are treated during renewal periods.

Historical operating-expense information can provide an important baseline for evaluating potential future obligations. [5]

## Permitted Use and Property Restrictions

The tenant's permitted use should be evaluated against zoning requirements, recorded restrictions, building rules, and other property-level limitations.

A lease can authorize a particular business activity contractually while other legal or recorded restrictions prevent that activity from being conducted at the property. Conversely, a broad permitted-use clause may still be limited by zoning, environmental requirements, licensing rules, or recorded covenants.

This is especially important where the tenant expects to expand operations, change its business model, install specialized equipment, or transfer the lease.

## Assignment and Subleasing

Assignment and subleasing provisions can become critical when a tenant sells its business, restructures its operations, relocates, or seeks to reduce its premises.

An assignment generally transfers the tenant's leasehold interest to another party, while a sublease allows the original tenant to retain its lease relationship while transferring possession of all or part of the premises to a subtenant.

The original tenant may continue to bear obligations under the lease following either arrangement, depending on the contract and applicable law. Commercial lease provisions may also require landlord consent and impose financial or operational conditions on a proposed transferee. [6]

A prospective tenant should therefore consider not only current occupancy needs but also whether the lease provides sufficient flexibility for future corporate or operational changes.

## Maintenance, Insurance, and Indemnification

Commercial leases allocate responsibility for maintaining the premises and protecting against various risks. The allocation can cover structural components, building systems, common areas, utilities, tenant improvements, and compliance with applicable laws.

Insurance provisions should be reviewed together with indemnification and waiver-of-subrogation provisions. The appropriate allocation may vary considerably between office, retail, industrial, and specialized properties.

The lease should also clearly address responsibility following casualty or condemnation. A tenant that has invested heavily in improvements may need to understand what happens if the premises become unusable before the lease expires.

## Default, Remedies, and Termination

Default provisions can create substantial financial consequences. Depending on the lease and applicable law, remedies may include late charges, recovery of attorneys' fees, termination, recovery of possession, application of security deposits, or other contractual remedies.

Recent ABA commentary notes that some commercial leases may provide for security-deposit draws, repayment of incentives, accelerated rent, and other remedies following default, although enforceability and available remedies depend on the governing law and circumstances. [7]

Termination provisions should therefore be evaluated alongside cure periods, notice requirements, casualty provisions, condemnation rights, and any negotiated early-termination rights.

## A Practical Due-Diligence Framework

A structured review can help identify conflicts between title, property operations, and lease obligations. A commercial real estate checklist may include:

- Current title commitment and underlying recorded documents;
- Complete chain-of-title review;
- Mortgages, liens, easements, and restrictions;
- Current survey and legal description;
- Zoning and permitted-use analysis;
- Existing leases and amendments;
- Rent schedules and security deposits;
- Operating-expense and CAM provisions;
- Assignment and sublease rights;
- Renewal and termination options;
- Insurance and indemnification requirements;
- Maintenance and repair responsibilities;
- Construction and tenant-improvement obligations;
- Defaults and remedies;
- Recorded reciprocal or operating agreements; and
- Required consents, releases, or title-curative documents.

ALTA's commercial due-diligence materials similarly identify title commitments, leases, environmental assessments, property-condition reports, zoning information, tax or lien searches, and surveys as important components of commercial transactions. [8]

## Conclusion

Evaluating a commercial property's title and lease arrangements requires more than confirming ownership or reviewing the stated rental rate. Title research establishes the nature of the interests affecting the property, while lease analysis determines how contractual obligations shape its use and economic performance.

The most significant risks often emerge where these two areas overlap—for example, when a recorded restriction conflicts with a tenant's intended use, an easement affects access, or an existing lease imposes obligations that influence the property's value.

Because title standards, landlord-tenant rules, recording requirements, remedies, and commercial leasing practices vary by jurisdiction, transaction participants should have qualified local professionals review documents before relying on them for a purchase, financing, or long-term lease decision.
