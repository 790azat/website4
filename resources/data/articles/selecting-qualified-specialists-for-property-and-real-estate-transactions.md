Real estate transactions often involve more than a buyer, seller, and real estate agent. Depending on the property and transaction, buyers may need assistance from attorneys, title professionals, inspectors, surveyors, appraisers, lenders, insurance professionals, engineers, environmental consultants, accountants, or other specialists.

Selecting the right professionals can help identify legal, financial, physical, and regulatory issues before a transaction closes. The appropriate team depends on the property's characteristics, the transaction structure, financing arrangements, and applicable state and local requirements.

## Understanding the Real Estate Transaction Team

A residential purchase may require only a relatively small group of professionals, while a commercial acquisition can involve specialists across multiple disciplines.

Common participants include:

- Real estate agents or brokers
- Real estate attorneys
- Mortgage lenders or brokers
- Title and settlement professionals
- Home inspectors
- Surveyors
- Appraisers
- Insurance professionals
- Accountants and tax advisers
- Engineers
- Environmental consultants
- Property managers

HUD's homeownership counseling materials identify multiple professionals who may participate in the homebuying and financing process and emphasize that the combination of professionals can vary according to state requirements and individual circumstances.

The goal is not necessarily to hire every type of specialist. Instead, buyers should identify which areas of the transaction require independent expertise.

## Selecting a Real Estate Agent or Broker

A real estate agent can assist with property searches, negotiations, transaction coordination, and communication between the parties.

Before selecting an agent, buyers can investigate:

- State licensing status
- Experience with the relevant property type
- Familiarity with the local market
- Experience with comparable transactions
- Professional references
- Compensation arrangements
- Representation relationships
- Potential conflicts of interest

The CFPB recommends understanding whom an agent represents and how the agent or brokerage is compensated. Depending on the circumstances and state law, an agent may represent the buyer, seller, or potentially both parties under applicable rules.

Buyers should therefore understand the agency relationship before relying on an agent's recommendations or advice.

## Choosing a Real Estate Attorney

A real estate attorney can review contracts, explain legal obligations, identify title or ownership issues, and address transaction-specific legal questions.

Legal assistance may be particularly relevant when a transaction involves:

- Complicated purchase agreements
- Commercial property
- Multiple owners
- Development rights
- Easements
- Boundary disputes
- Unusual financing arrangements
- Leases
- Business acquisitions
- Seller-financing arrangements
- Property held through entities
- Significant title problems

State requirements differ. In some jurisdictions, attorneys play a central role in the closing process, while other jurisdictions commonly use title or escrow companies for settlement services. The CFPB notes that the person conducting a closing varies by state and may be a settlement agent, escrow agent, or closing attorney.

## Evaluating Title and Settlement Professionals

Title professionals investigate ownership records and potential claims affecting property.

Title services can include title searches, title insurance, and other services associated with issuing a title policy. The CFPB explains that buyers can often shop for certain title and closing services rather than automatically accepting a provider recommended by a lender or real estate agent.

When evaluating a title or settlement provider, buyers can ask about:

- Experience with similar transactions
- Title-search procedures
- Title insurance coverage
- Settlement services
- Fees
- Communication procedures
- References
- Licensing or professional credentials where applicable

ALTA maintains a registry that allows consumers and other transaction participants to identify title and settlement agents, including information concerning participating title agents and real estate attorneys.

Title issues can sometimes require substantial research and corrective work before closing. Recent ALTA research describes title production as involving property-record searches, identification of defects, and curative work before a transaction can close.

## Choosing a Property Inspector

For residential purchases, an independent home inspector can provide information about the property's observable physical condition.

The CFPB recommends selecting an inspector with a reputation for being thorough and honest and advises arranging an independent inspection as early as possible so there is time to investigate significant problems.

Buyers should investigate:

- Licensing requirements in the relevant state
- Professional experience
- Inspection scope
- Written report format
- References and reviews
- Insurance coverage
- Whether specialized inspections are available

A general home inspection does not necessarily cover every potential property issue. Additional professionals may be appropriate for structural problems, electrical concerns, plumbing issues, environmental hazards, septic systems, wells, roofing, or other specialized conditions.

## Selecting a Surveyor

A surveyor may be needed when the buyer needs accurate information about property boundaries, improvements, easements, access, encroachments, or other land characteristics.

Survey requirements vary by transaction. Commercial acquisitions, development projects, boundary disputes, and transactions involving complicated parcels may require substantially more detailed surveying work than an ordinary residential purchase.

The 2026 ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey Standards provide nationally recognized minimum requirements for qualifying land title surveys and address matters including boundaries, easements, encroachments, access, and legal descriptions.

When selecting a surveyor, buyers should confirm that the professional is properly licensed in the applicable jurisdiction and has experience with the type of survey required.

## Evaluating an Appraiser

An appraisal serves a different purpose from a property inspection.

An inspector evaluates physical condition, while an appraiser develops an opinion of value using applicable appraisal methods and market information.

An appraisal may be particularly important when:

- Financing is involved
- The property is unusual
- The purchase price differs significantly from comparable properties
- The transaction involves an investment property
- A buyer is evaluating development potential
- A valuation is needed for a business or estate transaction

Buyers should understand who ordered the appraisal, what property characteristics were considered, and whether the appraisal is being used for lending, investment, tax, or another purpose.

## Selecting an Insurance Professional

Property insurance can protect against specified risks and may also be required by a mortgage lender.

Buyers should discuss coverage with a qualified insurance professional before closing, particularly when the property has unusual characteristics or is located in an area exposed to particular risks.

Potential considerations include:

- Property coverage
- Liability coverage
- Flood insurance
- Earthquake coverage
- Replacement-cost provisions
- Deductibles
- Business interruption coverage for commercial property
- Special endorsements

The CFPB identifies homeowners insurance as one of the services buyers generally need to arrange during the closing process.

## Commercial Property Specialists

Commercial transactions frequently require a broader professional team.

Depending on the property, buyers may need:

- Commercial real estate counsel
- Property-condition specialists
- Environmental consultants
- Land-use attorneys
- Zoning consultants
- Civil engineers
- Structural engineers
- Tax advisers
- Commercial insurance brokers
- Property managers
- Lease specialists

The need for these specialists can become particularly significant when the transaction depends on redevelopment, tenant income, environmental compliance, zoning approvals, or substantial capital improvements.

## Evaluating Credentials and Independence

Professional credentials should be verified rather than assumed.

HUD's homebuying training materials recommend checking licenses for real estate agents, brokers, and lenders, reviewing references and reviews, and consulting applicable state licensing authorities for complaints or disciplinary information.

Buyers should also ask whether a professional has an existing relationship with another party to the transaction.

Independence can be particularly important for inspections, appraisals, surveys, and legal advice. The CFPB specifically recommends an independent home inspector who is accountable to the buyer and provides a complete assessment of the property.

## Comparing Fees and Services

Price is one consideration, but the cheapest provider is not necessarily the appropriate provider for a complicated transaction.

When comparing professionals, buyers can request:

1. A description of services.
2. A written fee estimate.
3. Expected completion time.
4. Relevant credentials or licenses.
5. References.
6. Information about professional insurance where applicable.
7. Disclosure of potential conflicts.
8. A description of what is excluded from the quoted service.

For closing services, the CFPB specifically recommends requesting price quotes and references from potential providers and contacting references to ask about responsiveness and how problems were handled.

## Coordinating Multiple Professionals

Hiring qualified specialists is only part of the process. Their work may need to be coordinated so that one professional's findings can be reviewed by another.

For example, a survey could identify an encroachment that requires legal review. An inspection could identify a structural concern that requires an engineer. A title examination could uncover an easement that affects a planned development.

Maintaining a central transaction file can help organize:

- Reports
- Contracts
- Title documents
- Surveys
- Inspection findings
- Appraisals
- Insurance quotes
- Permits
- Correspondence
- Professional invoices
- Closing documents

The buyer should also maintain a calendar of contractual and financing deadlines.

## Protecting Against Transaction Fraud

Professional selection should include attention to transaction security.

Real estate transactions can involve large transfers of money and sensitive ownership information. ALTA reported in September 2026 that its latest survey of title professionals found seller-impersonation fraud attempts had become more widespread and sophisticated, with 59% of surveyed firms reporting at least one attempt during 2025.

Buyers should independently verify changes to wiring instructions and be cautious when receiving unexpected requests involving funds, identity documents, or changes to established communication channels.

Transaction participants should follow established verification procedures rather than relying solely on an email or message that appears to come from a known professional.

## Creating a Professional Selection Checklist

Before hiring a specialist, a buyer can use a basic checklist:

- Is the professional properly licensed or credentialed where required?
- Do they have experience with this property type?
- Can they provide relevant references?
- Are their fees clearly explained?
- What services are included?
- What services are excluded?
- Do they have professional liability or other relevant insurance?
- Are there potential conflicts of interest?
- Who will actually perform the work?
- What is the expected turnaround time?
- How will significant problems be communicated?
- Can the professional coordinate with the other members of the transaction team?

For complicated commercial transactions, these questions can be applied separately to each member of the professional team.

## Conclusion

Selecting specialists for a real estate transaction should be based on the specific risks and requirements of the property rather than simply assembling the largest possible team. A residential buyer may primarily need an experienced agent, inspector, lender, title professional, insurance provider, and—where appropriate—real estate attorney or other specialist. A commercial acquisition may require additional expertise in zoning, environmental conditions, engineering, leases, taxation, and property operations.

Buyers can improve the process by verifying credentials, understanding representation relationships, comparing fees, requesting references, checking licensing information, and confirming that important professionals are sufficiently independent. Early selection also gives specialists more time to identify problems before contractual deadlines or closing.

Because licensing, closing procedures, agency relationships, and real estate laws vary by jurisdiction, buyers should evaluate professional qualifications and transaction requirements under the law applicable to the particular property.
