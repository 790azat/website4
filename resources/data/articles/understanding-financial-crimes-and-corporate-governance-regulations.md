Financial crime and corporate governance are closely connected areas of modern business regulation. Financial-crime laws seek to prevent and punish activities such as fraud, money laundering, corruption, market manipulation, and the misuse of corporate structures. Corporate governance rules establish systems through which companies are directed, supervised, monitored, and held accountable.

Although the two areas serve different legal purposes, they frequently overlap. Weak governance can make it easier for misconduct to remain undetected, while effective oversight, transparent ownership, reliable financial reporting, and internal controls can help organizations identify and manage financial risks.

International frameworks such as the **Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations** and the **G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance** provide important reference points, although the specific legal obligations imposed on companies differ among jurisdictions.

## Understanding Financial Crime

Financial crime is a broad category covering unlawful conduct involving money, financial assets, markets, or economic transactions. Depending on the jurisdiction, examples can include fraud, money laundering, bribery, corruption, insider trading, market manipulation, sanctions violations, tax offenses, and certain forms of terrorist financing.

Money laundering is particularly important in international regulation because illicit proceeds can be transferred through multiple institutions and jurisdictions. A transaction may involve companies, financial institutions, intermediaries, trusts, or other legal arrangements located in different countries.

For this reason, financial-crime regulation increasingly emphasizes identifying the people who ultimately own or control companies and understanding the purpose and risks associated with financial relationships.

## The Role of Corporate Governance

Corporate governance concerns the systems through which companies are directed and controlled. It encompasses relationships between shareholders, boards, management, and other stakeholders.

The **G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance 2023** identify six major areas: an effective governance framework; shareholder rights and equitable treatment; institutional investors and markets; disclosure and transparency; board responsibilities; and sustainability and resilience.

The OECD Principles are not a universal corporate statute. They function as an international benchmark that countries can use when developing or evaluating their own legal, regulatory, and institutional frameworks.

Consequently, companies should distinguish between internationally recognized governance principles and the specific requirements imposed by the law of the jurisdiction in which they operate.

## Board Responsibilities and Oversight

Boards are an important component of corporate accountability. The OECD framework states that governance arrangements should support strategic guidance, effective monitoring of management, and board accountability to the company and shareholders.

Board responsibilities can include overseeing corporate strategy, financial operations, risk management, compliance systems, and internal controls. Boards may also be responsible for monitoring conflicts of interest, related-party transactions, financial reporting, and the use of corporate assets.

The precise legal duties of directors depend on national company law, securities regulation, listing requirements, and the organization's structure.

Some jurisdictions use a single-board model, while others separate management and supervisory functions into different boards. The OECD Principles recognize these structural differences while focusing on the underlying governance functions.

## Transparency and Financial Reporting

Transparency is one of the central connections between corporate governance and financial-crime prevention.

The OECD Principles state that corporate governance frameworks should promote timely and accurate disclosure of material information concerning matters such as financial condition, performance, ownership, and governance.

Financial statements and related disclosures allow shareholders, creditors, regulators, and other authorized users to evaluate the company's financial position and activities.

Depending on the jurisdiction, companies may also have obligations concerning:

- Annual and periodic financial reporting;
- Audited financial statements;
- Material corporate developments;
- Related-party transactions;
- Ownership information;
- Governance structures;
- Executive compensation; and
- Material financial and operational risks.

Accurate disclosure does not by itself prevent financial crime, but incomplete or misleading information can make misconduct more difficult to identify.

## Beneficial Ownership

Beneficial ownership has become an increasingly important part of international financial-crime regulation.

A company's registered shareholder is not necessarily the person who ultimately owns or controls it. FATF defines a beneficial owner as the natural person or persons who ultimately own or control a customer, including individuals exercising ultimate effective control over a legal person.

FATF's revised Recommendation 24 calls on countries to ensure that competent authorities have access to adequate, accurate, and up-to-date information about the true owners of companies.

These requirements are intended in part to prevent corporate vehicles from being used to conceal the identity of individuals involved in money laundering, corruption, sanctions evasion, and other illicit activity.

Corporate structures themselves are not inherently suspicious. Companies, trusts, and other legal arrangements have legitimate commercial and financial purposes. The regulatory concern arises when their structure is deliberately used to obscure ownership, control, or the source of assets.

## Customer Due Diligence and AML Controls

Anti-money-laundering regulation commonly requires regulated institutions to establish procedures for understanding customers and assessing financial-crime risks.

FATF standards include customer due diligence, recordkeeping, and suspicious-transaction reporting as important components of the international AML framework. Under the standards, financial institutions may be required to identify and verify customers and beneficial owners and understand the nature and ownership or control of legal-person customers.

Depending on the jurisdiction and institution, compliance procedures may include:

1. Identifying customers and beneficial owners;
2. Understanding the purpose and nature of a business relationship;
3. Assessing financial-crime risks;
4. Monitoring transactions;
5. Maintaining appropriate records;
6. Applying enhanced measures to higher-risk relationships; and
7. Reporting qualifying suspicious transactions to the appropriate financial-intelligence authority.

These requirements are generally risk-based rather than identical for every customer and transaction.

## Suspicious Transaction Reporting

Suspicious-transaction reporting creates a formal channel through which regulated institutions can alert financial-intelligence authorities to suspected illicit activity.

FATF Recommendation 20 provides that financial institutions should be required to report promptly to the relevant financial-intelligence unit when they suspect, or have reasonable grounds to suspect, that funds represent criminal proceeds or are related to terrorist financing.

The reporting obligation is distinct from determining whether a person has actually committed a crime. A suspicious-transaction report can trigger analysis or investigation, but the report itself does not establish criminal liability.

FATF standards also address confidentiality and prohibit institutions and their personnel from improperly disclosing that a suspicious-transaction report has been filed.

## Internal Controls and Risk Management

Internal controls are another major point of connection between governance and financial-crime prevention.

Organizations can use controls such as segregation of duties, approval procedures, transaction monitoring, financial reconciliations, access restrictions, audit procedures, compliance reviews, and independent oversight.

The OECD Principles state that boards should oversee risk-management systems and mechanisms designed to ensure compliance with applicable laws. They also identify oversight of internal controls, financial reporting, and corporate assets as important board functions.

The appropriate control environment depends on factors such as the company's size, industry, geographic exposure, ownership structure, and risk profile.

## Conflicts of Interest and Related-Party Transactions

Conflicts of interest can create governance risks when directors, executives, or major shareholders have personal or business interests that could affect corporate decisions.

Related-party transactions can require particular scrutiny because a company may transact with an entity connected to a director, executive, controlling shareholder, or another affiliated person.

The OECD Principles identify monitoring potential conflicts of interest, misuse of corporate assets, and abusive related-party transactions as important board responsibilities.

Legal requirements vary considerably. Some jurisdictions require disclosure or independent review, while others impose additional approval requirements for particular transactions.

## Auditing and Accounting Controls

Accounting systems provide another layer of corporate oversight.

Independent external audits can help provide assurance concerning financial statements, while internal audit and compliance functions may examine controls, processes, and organizational risks.

The OECD Principles state that boards should ensure the integrity of accounting and reporting systems and appropriate control systems, including independent external audit where applicable.

An audit does not guarantee that every instance of fraud or misconduct will be detected. Its purpose and scope depend on applicable auditing standards, legislation, and the terms of the engagement.

## Whistleblower and Reporting Mechanisms

Employees and other insiders can sometimes identify irregular conduct before regulators or external auditors do. For this reason, corporate governance frameworks may include confidential reporting channels for suspected unlawful or unethical behavior.

The OECD Principles specifically address whistleblowing policies and emphasize the importance of integrity, independence, confidentiality, and protection against retaliation.

The legal protections available to whistleblowers vary by jurisdiction. Some countries have comprehensive whistleblower statutes, while others provide protections through employment, securities, corporate, or sector-specific laws.

## Financial Crime and Corporate Structures

Corporate structures can serve legitimate purposes such as separating liability, organizing investments, facilitating international commerce, or managing ownership.

At the same time, FATF recognizes that corporate vehicles can be misused to disguise the identity of individuals involved in illicit activities or obscure the source of funds and assets.

This is why financial-crime regulation increasingly connects corporate transparency with beneficial-ownership information.

The objective is not to eliminate legitimate corporate structures but to make it more difficult for unlawful actors to exploit them anonymously.

## International Cooperation

Financial crime often extends beyond national borders. Funds can move through multiple jurisdictions, while companies and financial intermediaries may be located in different countries from the individuals who ultimately control them.

International standards therefore emphasize cooperation between financial-intelligence units, regulators, law-enforcement authorities, and other competent institutions.

FATF standards cover areas including international cooperation, beneficial ownership, customer due diligence, suspicious-transaction reporting, regulation and supervision, and transparency of legal persons and arrangements.

Corporate governance also has an international dimension. The OECD Principles are designed as a common benchmark while recognizing differences among national legal systems.

## Emerging Financial Risks

Technological change has introduced additional financial-crime challenges. Digital financial services, virtual assets, increasingly complex corporate structures, and cross-border electronic transactions can create new opportunities for legitimate commerce as well as new risks.

FATF standards apply AML/CFT preventive measures to virtual asset service providers, including customer due diligence, recordkeeping, suspicious-transaction reporting, and other relevant controls.

Regulatory frameworks therefore continue to evolve as financial products, payment systems, ownership structures, and methods of transferring assets change.

## Enforcement and Corporate Responsibility

Financial-crime enforcement can involve individuals, companies, financial institutions, directors, executives, and other participants, depending on the applicable law.

Possible consequences vary by jurisdiction and may include criminal penalties, civil enforcement, administrative sanctions, financial penalties, confiscation, regulatory restrictions, licensing consequences, or director-related restrictions.

Corporate governance failures may also create separate legal consequences where directors or officers breach statutory duties, reporting obligations, fiduciary duties, or other regulatory requirements.

Importantly, the existence of a governance failure does not automatically establish that a financial crime occurred. The applicable legal elements must still be established under the relevant law.

## Building a Compliance-Oriented Governance Framework

Organizations seeking to reduce financial and governance risks commonly consider several interconnected areas:

- Clear allocation of board and management responsibilities;
- Accurate accounting and financial reporting;
- Identification of beneficial owners;
- Risk-based customer and counterparty due diligence where required;
- Internal financial and compliance controls;
- Conflict-of-interest procedures;
- Independent audit and oversight functions;
- Confidential reporting mechanisms;
- Employee compliance training;
- Appropriate recordkeeping; and
- Periodic review of regulatory and financial-crime risks.

The precise obligations depend on the company's industry, jurisdiction, ownership structure, and regulatory status.

## Conclusion

Financial-crime regulation and corporate governance are separate but increasingly interconnected areas of law. Financial-crime rules focus on preventing and responding to unlawful financial activity, while corporate governance establishes structures for accountability, oversight, transparency, and responsible decision-making.

International standards emphasize several common themes: identifying beneficial owners, maintaining reliable information, conducting appropriate due diligence, reporting suspicious activity, protecting the integrity of financial reporting, managing conflicts of interest, and ensuring effective board oversight.

The G20/OECD Principles provide a broad international benchmark for corporate governance, while FATF standards provide an international framework for combating money laundering and related financial threats. Neither framework creates a single worldwide corporate law or AML statute. National legislation remains responsible for determining the precise obligations, enforcement mechanisms, and penalties applicable to businesses.

Understanding these overlapping systems is therefore important for companies, directors, investors, compliance professionals, and other participants in modern commercial activity. Effective governance does not eliminate financial-crime risk, but transparent ownership, reliable reporting, independent oversight, and appropriately designed controls can form important components of a broader regulatory framework.
