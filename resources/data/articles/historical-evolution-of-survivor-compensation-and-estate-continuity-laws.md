The law governing compensation after a person's death has developed significantly over the last several centuries. Modern wrongful-death claims, survival actions, probate procedures, and estate administration reflect a gradual shift away from older common-law rules that often provided limited remedies when a person's death resulted from another party's wrongful conduct.

Today, U.S. law generally recognizes two important but distinct concepts. A **wrongful-death action** allows certain surviving family members or beneficiaries to seek compensation for losses resulting from a person's death. A **survival action**, by contrast, allows certain claims belonging to the deceased person to continue through the person's estate after death. The precise rules, beneficiaries, damages, and procedures vary by state.

Understanding how these concepts developed helps explain why modern death-related claims can involve both the survivors and the estate of the deceased.

## The Common-Law Background

At early common law, personal injury claims were generally treated as personal rights belonging to the injured individual. The traditional doctrine was often expressed by the Latin maxim *actio personalis moritur cum persona*—roughly, a personal action dies with the person.

This created a significant legal problem when a person died because of another person's wrongful conduct.

If the deceased had not already brought a lawsuit, surviving family members could face substantial limitations in seeking compensation for the economic and personal consequences of the death.

Historical legal materials from the United States describe this distinction between injuries suffered by a person during life and losses caused by the person's death.

Over time, legislatures began creating statutory remedies to address these gaps.

## The Development of Wrongful-Death Laws

One of the most important developments was the passage of England's **Fatal Accidents Act of 1846**, commonly known as **Lord Campbell's Act**.

The statute responded to the limitations of the common law by creating a statutory cause of action for certain relatives of a person whose death resulted from another person's wrongful act, neglect, or default.

Historical U.S. legislative records identify the 1846 English statute as the model for much of the subsequent wrongful-death legislation adopted in the United States.

The basic concept was significant: instead of treating the death as eliminating the possibility of compensation, the law could recognize losses suffered by surviving family members as a result of the death.

## Adoption of Wrongful-Death Statutes in the United States

American jurisdictions gradually adopted their own wrongful-death statutes.

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, wrongful-death legislation had become widespread throughout the United States. Historical congressional records from 1910 noted that statutes modeled on Lord Campbell's Act had been adopted across the states, although important differences existed concerning who could bring an action, who could receive compensation, and how damages were calculated.

These differences remain important today.

Wrongful-death law is primarily governed by state statutes. States can differ concerning:

- Who may file a claim
- Which relatives or beneficiaries may recover
- What damages are available
- How damages are distributed
- Filing deadlines
- The role of a personal representative
- Whether certain damages are permitted
- How settlement proceeds are allocated

Consequently, there is no single nationwide wrongful-death formula.

## Wrongful Death and Survival Actions Are Different

A major development in estate and survivor law was the recognition that two different categories of legal interests can arise after a death.

### Wrongful-Death Claims

A wrongful-death action generally concerns losses suffered by surviving family members or other statutory beneficiaries because of the death.

Depending on state law, damages may include categories such as:

- Lost financial support
- Loss of services
- Funeral expenses
- Loss of companionship
- Certain emotional or relational losses
- Other legally recognized damages

The people entitled to recover and the damages available depend on the applicable state statute.

### Survival Actions

A survival action is different.

Under a survival statute, a legal claim belonging to the deceased may continue after death through the person's estate.

For example, suppose a person is seriously injured in an accident and files a personal-injury lawsuit but later dies from unrelated causes. A survival statute may allow the estate to continue pursuing the claim.

Depending on the jurisdiction, damages may include losses the deceased experienced before death, such as medical expenses, lost wages, property damage, and potentially pain and suffering.

The distinction can therefore be summarized as follows:

| Type of Claim | Primary Interest | Typical Claimant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wrongful death | Losses resulting from the death | Statutory beneficiaries or surviving family members |
| Survival action | Claims belonging to the deceased | Estate or personal representative |

The exact rules differ by state.

## The Role of the Personal Representative

As estate law developed, personal representatives became important in maintaining and administering legal claims belonging to a deceased person.

An executor or administrator may be responsible for:

- Identifying estate assets
- Collecting money owed to the estate
- Paying valid debts
- Managing pending litigation
- Preserving legal claims
- Distributing assets according to applicable law

A survival claim can therefore become an estate asset.

Cornell's Legal Information Institute explains that survival statutes commonly allow a personal representative, such as an executor or administrator, to pursue a claim on behalf of the deceased person's estate.

This creates an important connection between tort law and probate law.

## Estate Continuity and Pending Litigation

Modern survival statutes help prevent a person's death from automatically ending certain legal claims.

Without a survival mechanism, a person who had already suffered a legally recognized injury could potentially lose the ability to pursue that claim simply because death occurred before litigation was completed.

Survival statutes address this problem by allowing qualifying causes of action to continue.

However, the scope of a survival statute is not unlimited. State laws can differ substantially concerning which claims survive, what damages remain available, and what procedural steps the estate must take.

For example, Cornell's Wex materials note that states impose different requirements and limitation periods for survival actions.

## Changes in Compensation Principles

Early wrongful-death statutes frequently placed limitations on damages.

Historical legislation sometimes imposed fixed monetary ceilings or restricted the categories of losses that could be recovered. Over time, many jurisdictions changed these approaches.

A 1948 Congressional Record discussion concerning the District of Columbia, for example, described legislation removing an existing statutory limit on wrongful-death recovery.

The broader evolution has involved increasing attention to the economic and personal consequences that death can impose on surviving family members.

Modern statutes can nevertheless retain specific restrictions, including damage caps, beneficiary limitations, or special rules applicable to particular defendants.

## Who Can Receive Compensation?

The development of survivor-compensation laws also involved determining which people have a legally recognized interest in a deceased person's death.

Early statutory models commonly identified close family members such as spouses, parents, and children.

Modern statutes vary considerably.

Depending on the jurisdiction, eligible beneficiaries may include:

- A surviving spouse
- Children
- Parents
- Certain dependent relatives
- Other statutory beneficiaries

Some statutes prioritize beneficiaries in a particular order, while others use different methods of determining eligibility.

The existence of a biological or familial relationship does not necessarily mean that a person automatically has an independent right to bring a wrongful-death action. The governing statute controls.

## The Relationship Between Probate and Wrongful Death

Wrongful-death and survival claims can intersect with probate proceedings, but they do not necessarily follow identical procedures.

A survival claim generally represents an interest that belonged to the deceased and therefore can become part of the estate.

A wrongful-death claim, by contrast, is generally created by statute for specified beneficiaries and is based on losses associated with the death.

This distinction can affect:

- Who files the lawsuit
- Who receives the recovery
- Whether proceeds become estate property
- How creditors may be treated
- How settlements are approved
- How distributions are calculated

State law determines the precise relationship between these claims.

## The Expansion of Survivor Protections

Over time, survivor-compensation laws have increasingly recognized that death can produce losses extending beyond the immediate physical injury suffered by the deceased.

Families can lose financial support, household services, companionship, and other contributions.

Modern wrongful-death statutes therefore frequently permit recovery for categories of loss that earlier legal systems did not recognize in the same way.

Cornell's current overview of wrongful-death law explains that damages may be based on factors including the deceased person's income, expected future earnings, dependency relationships, and other legally recognized losses.

The precise categories remain jurisdiction-specific.

## Modern Estate Administration

Contemporary estate administration provides mechanisms for identifying and managing a deceased person's assets and legal interests.

When litigation is pending, an estate representative may need to determine:

- Whether the claim survives
- Which court has jurisdiction
- Whether a personal representative must be appointed
- Whether substitution of parties is required
- Which limitation period applies
- What damages remain available
- How any recovery should be distributed

These issues can become particularly important when a person dies while a personal-injury lawsuit is already underway.

The death of a party does not necessarily terminate the litigation, but procedural steps may be required to substitute the appropriate representative.

## Statutes of Limitations

Time limits have become an important component of modern survivor and estate claims.

Wrongful-death claims and survival actions may have different limitation periods.

The applicable deadline can depend on:

- The date of death
- The date of the underlying injury
- When the claim accrued
- Whether litigation was already pending
- The identity of the defendant
- Whether the defendant is a government entity
- State-specific tolling rules

For example, Cornell's Wex materials identify different limitation rules for survival actions in California and New York, illustrating why state-specific research is essential.

A deadline can also be affected by special statutory procedures, so relying on a general national rule can be misleading.

## Government Defendants and Special Compensation Systems

Not every survivor claim arises from an ordinary private accident.

Some deaths involve government employees, military personnel, federal programs, transportation systems, or specialized compensation statutes.

Federal law contains numerous specialized compensation schemes addressing particular categories of injury and death. These programs can establish their own eligibility requirements, administrative procedures, and limits.

For example, federal statutes governing certain employment or government-related injuries may provide compensation mechanisms that interact with or replace ordinary civil remedies in specified circumstances.

The existence of a specialized federal program therefore requires separate analysis rather than automatically applying ordinary state wrongful-death principles.

## How Modern Claims Are Evaluated

Today, a survivor or estate claim may require examination of several separate questions:

1. What caused the death?
2. What legal claim existed before or because of the death?
3. Was the claim a wrongful-death claim, a survival claim, or potentially both?
4. Who is legally entitled to bring the claim?
5. What damages are authorized?
6. What evidence establishes those damages?
7. What filing deadline applies?
8. Are special governmental or statutory rules involved?
9. How must any recovery be distributed?

These questions demonstrate why the historical distinction between wrongful death and survival actions remains important.

## Evidence in Survivor and Estate Claims

Evidence may include:

- Medical records
- Accident reports
- Employment records
- Income documentation
- Tax records
- Insurance information
- Estate documents
- Witness testimony
- Financial records
- Dependency information
- Funeral expenses
- Evidence concerning the deceased person's services and contributions

Economic-loss evidence can be particularly important when damages depend partly on lost income or financial support.

The admissibility and relevance of particular evidence depend on the applicable law and court procedures.

## The Modern Legal Landscape

Modern U.S. survivor-compensation law is therefore the product of several overlapping legal developments.

The first major shift was the creation of statutory wrongful-death remedies to address the shortcomings of common law.

The second involved survival statutes that allowed qualifying claims belonging to injured people to continue after their deaths.

The third involved the development of modern probate and estate-administration procedures capable of managing those claims and distributing estate assets.

These developments created a legal framework in which death does not necessarily terminate every legal interest associated with an injury.

At the same time, the rules remain highly dependent on state law.

## Final Takeaway

The historical development of survivor compensation reflects a fundamental change from older common-law principles that often restricted recovery after a person's death toward statutory systems recognizing both losses suffered by survivors and certain legal claims belonging to the deceased.

**Wrongful-death actions** generally provide a remedy for qualifying beneficiaries based on losses resulting from the death, while **survival actions** allow qualifying claims belonging to the deceased to continue through the estate.

The development of Lord Campbell's Act in England in 1846 strongly influenced American wrongful-death legislation, and U.S. jurisdictions subsequently developed their own statutes with substantial differences in beneficiaries, damages, procedures, and deadlines.

Today, determining whether a survivor or estate can recover requires careful attention to the applicable state statute, the nature of the claim, the role of the personal representative, available damages, and procedural deadlines.

Because wrongful-death, survival, and probate rules vary significantly by jurisdiction, families dealing with a death connected to an accident or alleged wrongful conduct should consider obtaining advice from an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction.
