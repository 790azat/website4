Tax audits and regulatory reviews can become significantly more complicated when a business operates across multiple jurisdictions, maintains complex financial records, employs a large workforce, or engages in transactions involving related entities. What begins as a request for supporting documents can develop into a broader examination of income, deductions, payroll taxes, reporting practices, or other compliance issues.

For U.S. businesses, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) describes an audit as an examination of books, accounts, and financial records to determine whether information reported on a tax return is accurate and whether the reported tax is correct. An audit does not necessarily mean that the taxpayer has done something wrong; returns can be selected through several methods, including statistical screening and examinations related to other taxpayers or transactions.

Understanding the areas that make an audit more complex can help businesses organize records, identify potential compliance gaps, and determine when professional assistance may be appropriate.

## Recognizing When an Audit Is More Complex

Not all tax examinations involve the same level of review. The IRS conducts audits by mail, at an IRS office, or through a field examination at a business or representative's office. The scope and duration depend on the issues involved, the availability of information, and whether the parties agree with the proposed findings.

Complexity can increase when an examination involves:

- Multiple tax years
- Several related businesses or entities
- Large or unusual transactions
- Significant deductions or credits
- Employment-tax questions
- International transactions
- Related-party transactions
- Incomplete accounting records
- Disagreements concerning the characterization of income or expenses
- Substantial amounts of electronically stored information

A business should distinguish between the specific issues identified in an audit notice and broader compliance questions that may emerge as the examination develops.

## Reviewing the Audit Notice Carefully

The audit notice is an important starting point. IRS correspondence identifies the examination and provides instructions concerning the information requested and the next steps.

The IRS states that taxpayers are initially contacted about an audit by mail rather than by telephone. The notice explains what is being examined and what records or information may be required.

Businesses should review the notice for:

- The tax period involved
- The tax return or returns being examined
- Specific items under review
- Requested documents
- Response deadlines
- Examiner contact information
- Instructions for submitting records

A company should also retain a complete copy of the notice and everything subsequently submitted to the agency.

## Maintaining Adequate Financial Records

Recordkeeping is one of the foundations of tax compliance.

The IRS explains that business records help taxpayers prepare financial statements and tax returns, identify income and deductible expenses, establish property basis, and substantiate items reported on tax returns. Businesses generally must retain records for as long as necessary to establish the income or deductions reported on a return.

Useful records can include:

- General ledgers
- Bank statements
- Invoices
- Receipts
- Contracts
- Payroll records
- Expense documentation
- Asset records
- Loan documents
- Tax returns
- Accounting-system reports
- Electronic correspondence

A complex audit may require information from several accounting systems or business units. Inconsistent records can make it harder to explain transactions and may require additional reconstruction.

## Employment Tax Compliance

Payroll and employment taxes can create particularly significant compliance exposure.

Employment-tax records must generally be retained for at least four years after the fourth quarter for the year. The IRS identifies records such as wage payments, employee information, withholding certificates, tax deposits, and related payroll documentation among the records employers should maintain.

Problems may arise from incorrect withholding, late deposits, inaccurate payroll reporting, or disagreements concerning worker classification.

Certain unpaid employment taxes can also create personal liability for individuals who qualify as responsible persons under the federal Trust Fund Recovery Penalty rules. The IRS explains that the penalty can apply to unpaid withheld income taxes and the employee portion of Social Security and Medicare taxes when statutory requirements are met.

Because employment-tax errors can extend beyond the company itself, businesses should treat payroll compliance as a separate area of risk rather than simply another bookkeeping function.

## Related-Party and Intercompany Transactions

Businesses with multiple subsidiaries, owners, partnerships, or affiliated companies can face additional questions about transactions between related parties.

Examples include:

- Intercompany loans
- Management fees
- Shared expenses
- Asset transfers
- Licensing arrangements
- Related-party sales
- Cost-sharing arrangements

These transactions should be supported by appropriate documentation and accounting records. The company should be able to explain the commercial purpose of the transaction, the amounts recorded, and how the transaction was reflected on applicable tax returns.

Where multiple entities are involved, inconsistencies between their records and tax filings can make an examination more difficult.

## Regulatory Compliance Beyond Income Taxes

Tax compliance is only one component of a broader corporate compliance program.

Depending on the company's activities, regulatory obligations may involve employment laws, licensing, financial reporting, industry-specific requirements, state registrations, consumer-protection rules, or federal reporting regimes.

Requirements can also change over time. For example, FinCEN announced in August 2026 that a final rule had removed beneficial-ownership reporting requirements for U.S. companies and U.S. persons, while retaining requirements for certain foreign entities registered to conduct business in the United States.

This illustrates why companies should verify current requirements rather than relying on older compliance checklists or previously applicable reporting obligations.

## Responding to Requests for Information

When an audit begins, businesses should establish an organized process for responding to document requests.

A practical system may include:

1. Creating a central audit file.
2. Assigning responsibility for gathering records.
3. Separating requested documents from unrelated materials.
4. Reviewing records for completeness.
5. Keeping copies of everything submitted.
6. Tracking deadlines and communications.
7. Documenting questions raised by the examiner.
8. Coordinating responses among accounting, legal, and management personnel.

The objective is not simply to produce a large quantity of documents. Responses should be organized so that the examiner can understand which records address each question.

## Identifying Potential Compliance Gaps

An audit can reveal issues that extend beyond the particular adjustment initially identified.

For example, a review of one deduction might reveal inconsistent expense classifications, while an employment-tax examination could raise questions about payroll procedures or worker classification.

Businesses should therefore distinguish between:

- A disagreement about the interpretation of a particular tax rule
- A factual documentation problem
- An accounting error
- A recurring compliance weakness
- A broader regulatory issue

Different problems may require different solutions.

Correcting a bookkeeping error, for example, is not necessarily the same as responding to a legal disagreement with an IRS position.

## Professional Representation and Audit Strategy

Businesses can generally represent themselves during an IRS examination, but the IRS recognizes the right of taxpayers to representation. Depending on the circumstances, an attorney, certified public accountant, or enrolled agent may represent a taxpayer before the IRS.

Professional assistance can be particularly relevant when an examination involves:

- Significant potential tax liabilities
- Multiple entities
- Complex transactions
- Employment-tax exposure
- International activity
- Disputed legal interpretations
- Large volumes of records
- Potential penalties
- Possible litigation or administrative appeals

Professionals may also help distinguish factual questions from legal issues and coordinate communications between the company and the examining agency.

## Challenging an Audit Finding

An audit does not necessarily end with the taxpayer accepting the examiner's proposed adjustments.

The IRS identifies several possible outcomes, including no change, agreement with proposed changes, or disagreement with the findings. When taxpayers disagree, they may have opportunities to request a conference with an IRS manager, pursue alternative dispute-resolution procedures, or seek an appeal when the applicable requirements and deadlines are satisfied.

The IRS Independent Office of Appeals handles various examination disputes, including proposed adjustments, penalty appeals, and audit reconsiderations.

For certain correspondence examinations, the IRS also provides an audit-reconsideration process that can allow taxpayers to submit new information supporting their position.

Businesses should therefore pay close attention to deadlines contained in IRS correspondence rather than assuming that an initial examination finding is necessarily final.

## Building a Stronger Compliance Framework

Businesses can reduce administrative uncertainty by treating tax compliance as an ongoing process rather than something addressed only when an audit begins.

A compliance framework may include periodic reviews of:

- Tax filings
- Payroll procedures
- Accounting classifications
- Record retention
- Entity structures
- Intercompany transactions
- State registrations
- Licensing requirements
- Regulatory reporting
- Internal approval procedures

Large corporate taxpayers may also have access to specialized IRS compliance programs. The IRS's Compliance Assurance Process, for example, is designed to help eligible large corporate taxpayers identify and resolve federal tax issues before returns are filed.

## When Professional Guidance Becomes Particularly Important

The need for professional guidance generally increases when tax questions intersect with corporate transactions, regulatory obligations, significant financial exposure, or potential personal liability.

A company facing an audit should first determine what is actually being examined, preserve relevant records, understand the response deadlines, and identify the factual and legal questions involved. From there, management can determine whether accounting, tax, legal, or other specialized assistance is appropriate.

Complex tax audits are rarely resolved by producing documents alone. They require an organized understanding of the company's records, applicable rules, transaction history, and procedural rights. A structured approach can help businesses respond accurately while identifying compliance weaknesses that may need attention beyond the immediate examination.
