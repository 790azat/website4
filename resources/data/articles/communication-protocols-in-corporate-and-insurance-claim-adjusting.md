Communication is a central part of insurance claim administration. Adjusters, policyholders, corporate risk departments, brokers, attorneys, witnesses, experts, and service providers may exchange information throughout the life of a claim. The accuracy, timing, and documentation of those communications can affect how a claim is investigated, evaluated, and resolved.

In the United States, insurance regulation is primarily state-based. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) develops model laws and regulations that states may adopt, modify, or decline to adopt. NAIC model regulations therefore provide useful frameworks, but they are not automatically binding nationwide.

The NAIC's Unfair Property/Casualty Claims Settlement Practices Model Regulation addresses areas including claim-file documentation, acknowledgment of pertinent communications, investigation, and settlement practices. Individual state requirements can differ.

## What Is Insurance Claim Communication?

Insurance claim communication includes the exchange of information between the parties involved in investigating and resolving a claim.

Depending on the type of loss, communications may occur between:

- The policyholder and insurer;
- A claimant and an insurer;
- An insurance adjuster and a corporate risk manager;
- Brokers and insurance carriers;
- Attorneys and claims professionals;
- Independent adjusters and insurers;
- Experts and claims personnel;
- Repair contractors and adjusters;
- Medical providers and insurers; and
- Other parties with relevant information.

Communication can take many forms, including telephone calls, letters, emails, online claim portals, text messages, recorded statements, inspection reports, estimates, photographs, and other electronic records.

The objective is generally to collect relevant information, clarify the circumstances of the loss, evaluate coverage and damages, and communicate decisions or requests for additional information.

## The Role of the Claims Adjuster

An adjuster's responsibilities depend on the type of claim, the insurer, the applicable law, and the adjuster's role.

An adjuster may:

1. Receive or review notice of a loss;
2. Establish or update the claim file;
3. Contact relevant parties;
4. Gather documents and statements;
5. Inspect damaged property where appropriate;
6. Coordinate experts or other professionals;
7. Evaluate coverage under the policy;
8. Assess the nature and extent of damages;
9. Communicate requests for additional information;
10. Prepare an evaluation or recommendation; and
11. Communicate payment, settlement, or coverage decisions.

An adjuster's communication is therefore part of a broader investigative process rather than simply an exchange of messages.

## Initial Claim Acknowledgment

Early communication can establish the basic administrative framework for a claim.

A first communication may confirm:

- That the claim has been received;
- The claim number;
- The identity of the assigned adjuster;
- Available contact information;
- Documents or information currently needed;
- Inspection arrangements;
- Immediate steps that may be necessary to protect property; and
- Any applicable procedural instructions.

The exact acknowledgment requirements depend on state law and the type of insurance involved.

The NAIC property/casualty claims model regulation specifically includes a section addressing failure to acknowledge pertinent communications. It also contains standards concerning investigation and claim disposition.

## Documenting Communications in the Claim File

A well-maintained claim file can provide a chronological record of the investigation.

Relevant records may include:

- Dates and times of communications;
- Names and roles of participants;
- Telephone call notes;
- Emails;
- Letters;
- Recorded statements;
- Photographs;
- Inspection reports;
- Repair estimates;
- Medical documentation where relevant;
- Expert reports;
- Coverage correspondence;
- Settlement offers;
- Payment records; and
- Documents supplied by the claimant.

The NAIC model framework specifically contemplates claim files that may be electronic, paper-based, or a combination of both. It also includes requirements concerning file and record documentation.

Recordkeeping is important because a claim can remain open for an extended period and may be handled by more than one claims professional.

## Communicating Information Requests

An insurer may need additional information before evaluating a claim.

Requests could concern:

- Accident circumstances;
- Ownership information;
- Policy documentation;
- Repair invoices;
- Photographs;
- Receipts;
- Medical records;
- Employment information;
- Proof of loss;
- Witness information;
- Prior damage; or
- Other evidence relevant to the claim.

Effective communication should identify what information is being requested and, where applicable, why it is relevant.

Corporate claim departments may also use standardized request forms or checklists to reduce inconsistent handling across claims.

## Communicating Coverage Issues

Coverage and liability are separate questions.

An insurer may need to determine whether the policy potentially covers the reported event before evaluating the amount of a loss. A claim can therefore involve communications concerning:

- Policy provisions;
- Exclusions;
- Deductibles;
- Conditions;
- Endorsements;
- Limits;
- Additional insureds;
- Other insurance;
- Reservation-of-rights issues; and
- Coverage determinations.

Communication about coverage should accurately identify the relevant policy provisions and distinguish an investigative position from a final determination when the investigation remains incomplete.

The NAIC's claims-settlement framework includes provisions concerning misrepresentation of policy provisions, illustrating the importance of accurate communication about insurance contracts.

## Corporate Risk Departments and Internal Communication

Large organizations often have their own internal claims protocols.

A corporate risk-management department may receive an incident report and coordinate communications among:

- Employees;
- Management;
- Risk managers;
- Insurance brokers;
- Insurers;
- Third-party administrators;
- Outside counsel;
- Safety personnel; and
- Contractors.

Internal reporting procedures can establish who must be notified, what information must be collected, and when the insurer or broker should be contacted.

For example, a company may require employees to report certain incidents immediately to a supervisor or designated risk-management contact. A separate procedure may require the risk department to notify the insurer within a specified period.

These internal policies do not necessarily determine the legal standard applicable to a claim. They are organizational procedures that can help companies manage reporting and documentation.

## Communication With Corporate Insureds

Corporate claims can be more complicated than individual consumer claims because several representatives may communicate with the insurer.

The insurer may deal with:

- A risk manager;
- A claims administrator;
- A broker;
- An employee involved in the incident;
- Corporate counsel;
- A property manager; or
- Another authorized representative.

Clear identification of the authorized contact can reduce confusion about who may provide information, approve repairs, receive documents, or discuss settlement.

Organizations may also establish centralized communication channels so that relevant claim information is preserved rather than dispersed across individual employees' email accounts.

## Recorded Statements and Interviews

Adjusters may request statements from insureds, claimants, witnesses, or employees.

Depending on the circumstances, a statement may be:

- Written;
- Recorded by telephone;
- Taken during an interview;
- Provided through a formal examination process; or
- Collected through another documented method.

The purpose may be to establish facts concerning the accident, property damage, injuries, or other circumstances of the claim.

A statement should be distinguished from a final determination of liability. An early account may be incomplete, and additional evidence can change the understanding of an incident.

## Email and Electronic Communication

Electronic communication has become a routine component of claims administration.

Emails and claim-portal messages can contain:

- Evidence submissions;
- Coverage questions;
- Settlement discussions;
- Repair documentation;
- Inspection arrangements;
- Requests for additional information; and
- Communications concerning claim status.

Corporate claims departments may use document-management systems to preserve these communications in the claim file.

Important communications should be retained according to applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and organizational requirements. The NAIC maintains a model regulation addressing market-conduct record retention and production, while actual recordkeeping obligations depend on the applicable jurisdiction and circumstances.

## Communication During Settlement Discussions

Claims communication can become particularly important during settlement negotiations.

A settlement discussion may involve:

- The claimed amount;
- Available policy limits;
- Evidence concerning liability;
- Damage calculations;
- Medical or repair documentation;
- Comparative fault;
- Prior payments;
- Deductibles; and
- Proposed settlement terms.

An offer or negotiation position should not automatically be treated as an admission of liability.

The parties may disagree about both the value of the claim and the underlying responsibility for the loss.

Where a settlement is reached, the parties should document the agreed terms carefully, including the amount, payment arrangements, release provisions, and any other conditions required by the agreement.

## Handling Disputed or Delayed Communications

Communication problems can arise when a claimant believes that an insurer has not responded adequately or when the insurer requires information that has not been provided.

A practical approach is to maintain a written record showing:

- When a communication was sent;
- What information was requested;
- What information was provided;
- Who responded;
- What issues remain unresolved; and
- What the next procedural step is.

Some state insurance laws impose specific requirements concerning acknowledgment, investigation, claim decisions, or communications. The applicable deadlines should therefore be verified under the law governing the particular claim rather than assumed from a general industry practice.

## Communication and Regulatory Compliance

The NAIC's model framework provides an important reference point for claims-handling practices, but it is essential to distinguish a model regulation from enacted state law.

The NAIC explains that its model laws and regulations are proposed frameworks that states may adopt, modify, or approach differently. They are not federal law and do not automatically apply in every state.

For property and casualty claims, NAIC Model Regulation 902 addresses minimum standards concerning investigation and disposition of claims and identifies practices that may constitute unfair claims practices. Its provisions include communication acknowledgment, documentation, policy-provision representations, and settlement standards.

A state may enact substantially similar provisions, modify them, or establish different requirements.

## Privacy and Sensitive Information

Insurance claims frequently involve sensitive information.

Depending on the claim, communications may contain:

- Medical information;
- Financial records;
- Social Security numbers;
- Employment information;
- Banking information;
- Business records; or
- Personal identifying information.

Claims departments therefore need procedures governing who can access information and how documents are transmitted and stored.

The appropriate privacy requirements depend on the type of information, the parties involved, the insurance product, and applicable federal and state law.

## Best Practices for Corporate Claim Communication

A structured communication process can help organizations maintain accurate claim records.

Useful practices include:

### 1. Establish a primary contact

Identify the person responsible for coordinating communications on the corporate side.

### 2. Use consistent reporting procedures

Employees should know how and when incidents must be reported internally.

### 3. Preserve original documentation

Keep emails, photographs, reports, statements, invoices, and other relevant materials in an organized system.

### 4. Record important telephone communications

Call notes should identify the participants, date, subject, and material information discussed.

### 5. Separate facts from assumptions

Reports should distinguish observed facts from preliminary opinions or allegations.

### 6. Track outstanding requests

Maintain a record of documents or information that remain outstanding.

### 7. Confirm significant decisions in writing

Important coverage, settlement, repair, or claim-status communications should generally be documented.

### 8. Follow applicable retention policies

Records should be maintained according to applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and organizational requirements.

## Communication Does Not Determine Liability by Itself

Claim communications can provide important evidence, but a communication does not automatically establish legal liability.

For example, an employee's initial description of an accident may contain incomplete information. An adjuster's preliminary assessment may change after additional evidence is reviewed. Similarly, a claimant's estimate of damages may differ from the amount ultimately supported by documentation.

The legal significance of a communication depends on the circumstances, the governing law, the policy language, and the evidence as a whole.

## Final Takeaway

Communication protocols are an important component of corporate insurance claim administration. Effective processes can help establish a clear record of notice, investigation, information requests, coverage discussions, damage evaluation, and settlement negotiations.

The NAIC provides model frameworks addressing claims documentation, acknowledgment of communications, investigation, and settlement practices, but those models do not automatically constitute binding law in every state. Actual requirements depend on the applicable state statutes, regulations, insurance policy, and circumstances of the claim.

For companies managing insurance claims, consistent reporting procedures, accurate documentation, controlled access to sensitive information, and clear communication channels can help create a reliable administrative record throughout the life of a claim.
