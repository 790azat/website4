Civil disputes can be resolved in several ways. Some parties reach an agreement through direct negotiation or mediation without asking a court to decide the merits. Others proceed through formal litigation and ultimately have a judge or jury determine the dispute.

An **out-of-court settlement** and a **formal judicial trial** represent fundamentally different methods of resolving a legal dispute. A settlement is based on an agreement between the parties, while a trial results in a judicial determination after formal procedures and presentation of evidence.

Neither process is identical in every case. Settlement terms depend on the parties' agreement, while trial procedures depend on the applicable court rules, jurisdiction, type of claim, and procedural posture.

Federal court statistics illustrate how relatively few civil disputes ultimately reach trial compared with the much larger volume of civil litigation. In 2025, U.S. district courts reported 303,563 civil case filings, while 3,377 civil trials were completed by active and senior Article III judges. Federal courts also reported that 63 districts operated ADR programs affecting more than 40,600 civil cases.

## What Is an Out-of-Court Settlement?

An out-of-court settlement is an agreement in which the parties resolve some or all of their dispute without requiring a judge or jury to decide the contested issues.

Settlement can occur:

- Before a lawsuit is filed;
- During pretrial litigation;
- During discovery;
- After motions have been filed;
- During mediation;
- During a settlement conference; or
- Even shortly before or during trial.

A settlement may involve payment of money, but it can also include other terms.

Depending on the dispute, the agreement might address:

- Payment schedules;
- Contract modifications;
- Return of property;
- Business arrangements;
- Corrective actions;
- Confidentiality provisions where enforceable;
- Releases of claims; or
- Other negotiated obligations.

The parties determine the settlement terms rather than receiving a remedy selected entirely by a judge.

## What Is a Formal Judicial Trial?

A judicial trial is a formal proceeding in which disputed factual and legal issues are presented to a court.

A civil trial may be:

- A **jury trial**, where eligible factual questions are determined by a jury; or
- A **bench trial**, where the judge determines both the applicable law and the relevant factual issues.

Trial procedures generally involve pleadings, discovery, evidence, witnesses, motions, and pretrial preparation.

The U.S. Courts describe civil litigation as a process that can include pleadings, discovery, motions, settlement efforts, and trial.

At trial, the court applies the applicable procedural and evidentiary rules rather than relying solely on the parties' voluntary agreement.

## The Central Difference: Agreement vs. Adjudication

The fundamental distinction can be summarized simply:

**Settlement:** The parties agree to resolve the dispute.

**Trial:** A judge or jury determines the dispute according to applicable law and evidence.

This difference affects almost every aspect of the process.

In a settlement, the parties can decide that they are willing to compromise a disputed issue.

At trial, the parties generally must present evidence supporting their respective positions, and the court determines the issues assigned to it.

## Comparison of the Two Processes

| Issue | Out-of-Court Settlement | Formal Judicial Trial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision-maker | Parties | Judge or jury |
| Control over outcome | Negotiated by parties | Determined through adjudication |
| Evidence | Negotiated and exchanged as needed | Governed by applicable evidence rules |
| Procedure | Relatively flexible | Formal court procedures |
| Privacy | May offer greater confidentiality depending on circumstances | Court proceedings and filings may be public, subject to applicable rules |
| Timing | Can resolve a dispute relatively early | Usually requires substantial litigation preparation |
| Costs | Can reduce litigation expenses, but negotiation and mediation still have costs | Can involve extensive litigation expenses |
| Appeal | Generally limited to disputes about the agreement or enforcement | Judicial decisions may be appealable under applicable rules |
| Remedy | Whatever the parties agree to | Relief authorized by law and awarded by the court |
| Finality | Depends on settlement terms and enforcement | Judgment becomes binding subject to applicable post-trial and appellate procedures |

These are general distinctions. The actual process can vary substantially by jurisdiction and case.

## Control Over the Outcome

Settlement gives the parties significant control over the terms of resolution.

For example, two businesses disputing a contract might agree to:

- A partial payment;
- A revised delivery schedule;
- A future business arrangement; and
- Mutual releases.

A judge deciding a conventional contract claim may have considerably less flexibility to create a remedy outside the legal claims and available forms of relief.

Trial therefore transfers decision-making authority from the parties to the judicial process.

That transfer can be important when the parties cannot agree on an acceptable resolution.

## The Role of Evidence

Evidence plays a role in both settlement negotiations and trials, but the way evidence is used can differ.

During settlement negotiations, parties may exchange evidence to persuade the other side about the strengths or weaknesses of the claim.

A party might provide:

- Photographs;
- Medical records;
- Contracts;
- Expert reports;
- Financial records;
- Witness statements;
- Accident reconstruction;
- Video recordings; or
- Other supporting documents.

The parties can use that information to evaluate the potential value and risk of the dispute.

At trial, however, evidence must generally satisfy the applicable rules governing admissibility and presentation.

The Federal Rules of Evidence, for example, govern evidence in federal proceedings subject to applicable law and exceptions.

Thus, information that is useful during settlement discussions does not automatically become admissible trial evidence.

## Timing and Case Duration

Settlement can resolve a dispute before the parties complete the entire litigation process.

A settlement reached early may avoid substantial discovery, motion practice, expert preparation, and trial preparation.

Trial generally requires more procedural steps.

Federal court statistics demonstrate that cases that reach trial can remain pending through a significant litigation process. The federal judiciary publishes separate statistics concerning median time from filing to disposition and median time from filing to completed civil trials.

The exact duration depends on the court, case complexity, discovery requirements, motions, expert evidence, scheduling, and other factors.

A simple dispute and a complex commercial case can therefore have dramatically different timelines.

## Costs and Litigation Expenses

Settlement can sometimes reduce litigation expenses because it may eliminate the need for additional discovery and trial preparation.

However, settlement is not necessarily inexpensive.

Parties may incur costs for:

- Attorneys;
- Mediators;
- Experts;
- Investigators;
- Document review;
- Negotiation;
- Valuation; and
- Other professional services.

A trial can generate additional expenses associated with:

- Depositions;
- Expert witnesses;
- Court reporters;
- Exhibits;
- Motion practice;
- Trial preparation;
- Witness preparation; and
- Court appearances.

The cost difference therefore depends on when the settlement occurs and how complex the litigation has become.

## Privacy and Public Records

Privacy can also differ.

Settlement negotiations may occur privately, although confidentiality depends on the circumstances, agreements, applicable law, and court rules.

Judicial proceedings are generally subject to public-access principles, particularly in federal courts. Court filings and hearings can therefore create a public record, although courts may seal or restrict access to certain materials under applicable standards.

A settlement reached before litigation can avoid creating many court filings altogether.

Once a lawsuit has been filed, however, settling the case does not necessarily mean every existing court record disappears.

## Certainty and Risk

Settlement provides a different form of certainty from trial.

Once parties execute an enforceable settlement agreement, they know the obligations they have accepted, subject to the agreement's terms and enforceability.

A trial produces a different type of resolution: the court determines the disputed issues.

Before trial, however, the parties face uncertainty about:

- Which witnesses will be believed;
- Which evidence will be admitted;
- How factual disputes will be resolved;
- How legal questions will be decided; and
- What judgment will ultimately be entered.

Settlement can therefore replace some litigation uncertainty with an agreed outcome.

That does not mean the settlement necessarily produces the same result a party would have received at trial.

## Remedies Available Through Settlement

One major characteristic of settlement is flexibility.

A settlement can potentially include terms that go beyond the basic damages sought in a lawsuit, provided they are lawful and agreed upon by the parties.

For example, a commercial settlement might combine:

- A monetary payment;
- A modification to a contract;
- Transfer of certain assets;
- A future business obligation; and
- Mutual releases.

The parties can structure the agreement around their negotiated objectives.

A court judgment, by contrast, is constrained by the claims, defenses, applicable law, and relief available through the proceeding.

## The Role of Mediation

Mediation is often used to help parties reach an out-of-court settlement.

A mediator generally facilitates communication rather than deciding who wins the dispute.

Federal courts use mediation and judge-hosted settlement conferences as part of their ADR programs. In 2025, 63 federal districts operated ADR programs, and those programs affected more than 40,600 civil cases.

Mediation can occur before or after a lawsuit begins.

If mediation fails, the parties generally retain whatever litigation rights they had before mediation, subject to applicable rules and agreements.

## Settlement After Litigation Begins

An important misconception is that filing a lawsuit necessarily means the case will end in a trial.

Settlement negotiations can continue throughout litigation.

Parties may settle:

- Before discovery;
- After document exchanges;
- After depositions;
- After expert reports;
- After summary-judgment motions;
- During a settlement conference; or
- Shortly before trial.

The information obtained during litigation can sometimes make settlement easier because the parties have a clearer understanding of the evidence.

## The Role of the Judge

The judge's role differs significantly in settlement and trial.

During settlement proceedings, a judge may facilitate negotiations through a settlement conference, depending on the court's procedures.

During trial, the judge has formal judicial responsibilities, including ruling on legal issues, controlling courtroom procedure, and deciding evidentiary questions.

In a bench trial, the judge also determines the factual issues.

In a jury trial, the judge generally instructs the jury on the applicable law while the jury determines the factual questions assigned to it.

## The Role of the Jury

A jury can play a central role in a civil trial when the applicable law provides a right to jury determination and the parties properly invoke that right.

The jury may evaluate:

- Witness credibility;
- Documentary evidence;
- Expert testimony;
- Factual disputes; and
- Other evidence presented at trial.

The judge remains responsible for legal rulings and courtroom procedure.

There is no equivalent jury decision in a negotiated settlement.

The parties instead determine whether to accept the proposed terms.

## Appeals and Post-Trial Review

Settlement and trial also differ after resolution.

A settlement is primarily governed by the agreement itself and applicable contract and procedural law. Disputes can sometimes arise concerning whether a settlement was properly formed or whether a party complied with its terms.

A trial produces a judgment that can, under applicable rules, be challenged through post-trial motions or an appeal.

An appeal generally does not provide an entirely new trial. Instead, an appellate court reviews specified legal or procedural issues under the applicable standards of review.

The availability and scope of appellate review depend on the type of judgment and governing rules.

## Enforcing a Settlement

A settlement agreement is only useful if its terms can be enforced.

A well-documented settlement generally identifies:

- The parties;
- Payment obligations;
- Deadlines;
- Releases;
- Continuing obligations;
- Conditions;
- Procedures for resolving future disputes; and
- Other material terms.

Some settlements are incorporated into court orders or judgments, while others remain contractual agreements between the parties.

The legal effect depends on how the settlement is structured and the applicable jurisdiction.

## Enforcing a Court Judgment

A judicial judgment can also require additional procedures before the prevailing party receives payment.

Depending on the case, enforcement can involve:

- Payment arrangements;
- Judgment liens;
- Garnishment;
- Execution procedures;
- Asset discovery; or
- Other collection mechanisms.

Winning at trial therefore does not necessarily mean that payment occurs immediately.

The enforceability of a judgment can depend on the debtor's assets, applicable exemptions, procedural rules, and other circumstances.

## When a Trial May Still Be Necessary

Some disputes do not resolve through settlement.

A trial may become necessary when:

- The parties cannot agree on value;
- Liability is strongly disputed;
- A legal issue requires judicial resolution;
- A party seeks a remedy unavailable through negotiation;
- Settlement negotiations break down;
- Important factual disputes remain unresolved; or
- One or more parties prefer a judicial determination.

A trial can provide a formal adjudication when voluntary agreement is not possible.

## Settlement Does Not Mean the Claim Was Invalid

A settlement should not automatically be interpreted as an admission that one party was legally correct.

Parties may settle because of uncertainty, litigation costs, business considerations, time concerns, risk allocation, or other factors.

Similarly, refusing to settle does not automatically establish that a party has a stronger legal position.

Settlement is a method of dispute resolution, not necessarily a determination of the underlying merits.

## Trial Does Not Guarantee a Particular Outcome

The same principle applies to litigation.

Taking a case to trial creates a process for judicial determination, but the result depends on the evidence, applicable law, procedural rulings, and fact finder's conclusions.

The existence of strong evidence does not guarantee a particular verdict, just as the filing of a lawsuit does not guarantee that the plaintiff will prevail.

## A Practical Comparison

When evaluating the two processes, parties may examine several questions:

### Settlement

- Can the parties agree on acceptable terms?
- Is early resolution important?
- Are there business relationships worth preserving?
- Are there terms a court may not be able to provide?
- What are the costs of continuing litigation?
- What risks remain uncertain?

### Trial

- Is a formal judicial decision necessary?
- Are important facts genuinely disputed?
- Does the case involve a significant legal question?
- Is the available evidence sufficient for trial?
- What procedural steps remain?
- What are the potential costs of continued litigation?

These questions do not produce a universal answer. Their significance depends on the particular dispute.

## Final Takeaway

Out-of-court settlements and formal judicial trials provide fundamentally different ways to resolve civil disputes. A settlement allows the parties to negotiate the terms of resolution, while a trial places disputed legal and factual issues before a judge or jury.

Settlement can provide greater flexibility and may resolve a dispute before the parties incur the full costs and procedural demands of trial. Formal litigation, however, provides structured procedures for discovery, evidence, judicial rulings, and ultimately a binding judgment.

Federal judicial data illustrates the distinction in practice. During 2025, U.S. district courts reported 303,563 civil filings but 3,377 civil trials completed by active and senior Article III judges, while federal ADR programs affected more than 40,600 civil cases.

Neither settlement nor trial operates in isolation. A lawsuit can involve negotiations and mediation throughout the litigation process, and a case can settle even after substantial trial preparation has occurred.

The practical distinction is ultimately one of **who determines the resolution**: in a settlement, the parties agree to the terms; in a trial, the judicial process determines the outcome under the applicable law and evidentiary rules.
