Eyewitness testimony can provide important information about an accident, crime, or other event, but human memory is not a literal recording of everything a person observes. What a witness remembers can be affected by perception, attention, stress, the passage of time, subsequent information, and the way questions are asked.

Research reviewed by the National Academies has identified numerous factors that can influence the accuracy and reliability of eyewitness identification, including conditions surrounding the original observation and procedures used later to obtain an identification.

For courts and investigators, this does not mean that eyewitness testimony is inherently unreliable. Instead, it means that testimony should be evaluated in light of the circumstances under which the memory was formed, retained, retrieved, and communicated.

## How Eyewitness Memory Works

Memory is commonly described in terms of several interconnected processes:

1. **Encoding** — information is initially perceived and processed.
2. **Storage** — information is retained over time.
3. **Retrieval** — the person later attempts to recall the information.

Each stage can introduce limitations.

A witness who had only a few seconds to observe an event may encode less information than someone who observed the event for several minutes. Poor lighting, distance, obstructions, movement, or divided attention can further affect what is initially perceived.

The National Academies has emphasized that human vision does not create a perfect, error-free representation of a witnessed event and that scientific research on perception and memory helps explain limitations in eyewitness identification.

## Attention and the Quality of the Original Observation

A person's ability to remember an event depends partly on what they were paying attention to at the time.

During a stressful or rapidly developing event, attention may be directed toward only certain aspects of the situation. A witness may remember a person's general appearance while having difficulty recalling details such as clothing, facial characteristics, or the exact sequence of movements.

Environmental conditions can also matter.

Factors potentially affecting the initial observation include:

- Lighting;
- Distance;
- Duration of exposure;
- Obstructions;
- Viewing angle;
- Movement;
- Weather;
- Distractions;
- Familiarity with the environment; and
- The witness's ability to see the relevant person or event.

Consequently, two witnesses can observe the same event but retain different details.

## Stress and Emotional Events

Witnesses sometimes observe events involving fear, violence, injury, or other intense circumstances.

Stress can affect attention and memory, but its effects are not necessarily simple or uniform. The relationship between emotional arousal and memory depends on the circumstances and the particular information being remembered.

For this reason, it is generally inappropriate to assume that a witness who experienced a highly stressful event must either have exceptionally accurate or exceptionally poor memory.

Instead, investigators and courts may examine the specific circumstances surrounding the observation.

## The Passage of Time

Memory can change as time passes.

A witness may initially provide an account shortly after an event and later provide additional details or describe certain details differently. This does not automatically establish that the later account is false, but differences between accounts can be relevant when evaluating testimony.

The time between an event and a later identification or interview is therefore an important consideration.

The National Academies' review of eyewitness research specifically considers variables associated with the retention and retrieval of memories and recommends procedures designed to preserve information about a witness's initial identification and confidence.

## Post-Event Information and Memory

Information encountered after an event can sometimes influence later recollection.

A witness might discuss the incident with another person, see news coverage, review photographs, hear another person's account, or receive questions containing assumptions about what happened.

Subsequent information can become intertwined with a person's recollection of the original event.

Research sponsored by the National Institute of Justice has examined the effects of leading and misleading questions on eyewitness reports. Studies of cognitive interviewing have also investigated techniques intended to improve the completeness of witness accounts while addressing the effects of questioning.

This is one reason investigators generally have an interest in obtaining an account before unnecessary exposure to potentially contaminating information.

## Leading Questions

The wording of a question can influence how a witness describes an event.

Compare:

"What did you observe?"

with:

"How fast was the red car traveling when it hit the pedestrian?"

The second question assumes facts that the witness may not have independently recalled.

Open-ended questions can allow witnesses to provide information in their own words. More specific questions may then be used to clarify particular details.

Research into cognitive interviewing has explored methods for increasing the amount of information obtained from witnesses while limiting some effects associated with misleading questions.

## The Role of Interviews

An interview is not simply a method of recording an existing memory. The interaction itself can influence what information a witness subsequently reports.

Investigators may therefore use structured approaches intended to reduce unnecessary suggestion.

The National Academies recommended that law-enforcement personnel receive training concerning vision, memory, factors affecting memory, and practices designed to minimize contamination of eyewitness recollections.

Research has also examined rapport-building during interviews. One NIJ-sponsored study found that rapport-building before exposure to post-event misinformation was associated with greater accurate reporting in the study conditions.

These findings illustrate why interviewing practices can matter as much as the witness's original observation.

## Eyewitness Identification

An eyewitness identification occurs when a witness is asked to identify a person believed to be involved in an event.

Identification procedures can include:

- Photo arrays;
- Live lineups;
- Showups;
- Other identification procedures.

The National Academies has examined how lineup composition, instructions, administration, and other factors can influence identification accuracy.

The identification procedure is therefore an important part of evaluating the resulting evidence.

## The Importance of Neutral Instructions

A witness may reasonably assume that investigators already know who committed an offense.

Instructions can help address that possibility.

The National Academies recommended that witnesses be clearly informed that the person responsible may or may not be present in a photo array or lineup and that the investigation will continue regardless of whether an identification is made.

Such instructions are intended to reduce pressure on witnesses to select someone simply because an identification procedure has been presented to them.

## Blind Administration

Another recommended safeguard concerns who administers an identification procedure.

The National Academies recommended **double-blind procedures**, in which the administrator does not know which person in the lineup or photo array is the suspect. The objective is to reduce the possibility that an administrator could unintentionally influence the witness through words, gestures, facial expressions, or other cues.

The precise procedures used by law-enforcement agencies vary by jurisdiction and agency policy.

## Witness Confidence and Accuracy

Witness confidence can have a powerful effect on how people perceive testimony.

A witness who appears highly confident may be viewed as particularly persuasive. However, confidence expressed later in the legal process does not necessarily provide a complete measure of the accuracy of the original memory.

The National Academies recommended documenting a witness's confidence **at the time of the initial identification**, rather than relying exclusively on confidence expressed later. The committee noted that later courtroom expressions of confidence can differ from the witness's initial confidence assessment.

This distinction is important because confidence can be influenced by experiences occurring after the original identification.

## Familiarity and Unconscious Transference

A witness may sometimes recognize a person without accurately remembering why that person seems familiar.

For example, a witness may have previously encountered an innocent person and later encounter that person during an identification procedure. Familiarity can potentially be confused with recognition of the person responsible for the event.

NIJ-sponsored research has examined **unconscious transference**, including situations in which a witness may identify a familiar but innocent person. A large 2024 study explored several theories concerning how familiarity and memory for different people encountered during an event can affect identification.

This illustrates why recognition and identification are not necessarily identical psychological processes.

## Memory Does Not Become More Accurate Simply Because a Witness Repeats It

Repeatedly describing an event can make a witness increasingly familiar with their own account.

That does not necessarily mean every detail becomes independently verified.

A witness may discuss the event with other witnesses, investigators, attorneys, family members, or media sources. Details introduced during those interactions can potentially become incorporated into later recollections.

For that reason, the timing and circumstances of successive statements can be relevant when evaluating changes in testimony.

## Eyewitness Testimony in Court

Courts may consider eyewitness testimony alongside other evidence.

Depending on the case, supporting or contradictory evidence may include:

- Photographs;
- Video recordings;
- Physical evidence;
- Digital records;
- Forensic evidence;
- Telephone records;
- Location information;
- Medical records;
- Other witnesses; and
- Statements made close to the time of the event.

The presence of corroborating evidence does not automatically establish that a witness's memory is accurate. Likewise, an inconsistency does not necessarily mean the entire testimony is false.

The fact finder generally evaluates the evidence as a whole under the applicable legal standards.

## Expert Testimony About Memory

Psychological research can sometimes be presented through expert testimony to help explain factors affecting eyewitness memory.

The National Academies concluded that scientific-framework expert testimony can inform fact finders about variables affecting visual experience, encoding, storage, and retrieval of memories.

NIJ-sponsored research has likewise examined whether expert testimony can improve jurors' sensitivity to factors affecting eyewitness reliability. One study reported that mock jurors who heard expert testimony placed greater weight on witnessing and identification conditions than those who did not hear such testimony.

Whether expert testimony is permitted, and what subjects an expert may address, depends on the applicable rules of evidence and the particular court.

## Factors That Can Affect Eyewitness Memory

When evaluating eyewitness evidence, investigators, attorneys, and fact finders may consider several categories of information.

### Conditions at the Time of the Event

- Lighting;
- Distance;
- Duration;
- Visibility;
- Distractions;
- Stress;
- Movement; and
- Attention.

### Conditions After the Event

- Time before the first interview;
- Discussions with other people;
- Exposure to news reports;
- Repeated questioning;
- Leading questions;
- Exposure to photographs or suspects; and
- Additional information about the event.

### Identification Procedures

- Instructions provided to the witness;
- Composition of the lineup or photo array;
- Administrator procedures;
- Whether the administrator knew the suspect's position;
- The witness's initial confidence; and
- Whether the identification was documented.

These factors do not produce a simple mathematical reliability score. They provide context for evaluating the testimony.

## Preserving the Initial Account

Early documentation can be particularly useful because it creates a record of what the witness reported before substantial additional information has been introduced.

The National Academies recommended recording the witness's confidence verbatim at the time of the initial identification and videotaping identification procedures as standard practice.

Preserving the original wording, rather than relying solely on later summaries, can allow investigators, attorneys, and courts to evaluate how the account developed over time.

## Eyewitness Memory and Accident Cases

Although much eyewitness research concerns criminal investigations, the underlying memory principles can also matter in civil disputes.

For example, after a traffic collision, witnesses may be asked to recall:

- Which vehicle entered an intersection first;
- Whether a traffic signal was red or green;
- Approximate vehicle speed;
- Lane positions;
- Whether a driver was using a phone;
- The sequence of impacts; or
- What occurred immediately before a collision.

These recollections may later be compared with photographs, surveillance footage, vehicle data, physical evidence, and accident-reconstruction analysis.

A witness's account is therefore one potential source of evidence rather than necessarily the only source.

## Practical Considerations for Investigators and Legal Professionals

A careful approach to eyewitness evidence can include:

1. **Obtain an account promptly when circumstances permit.**
2. **Use open-ended questions before introducing specific assumptions.**
3. **Document the witness's original account.**
4. **Record identification procedures.**
5. **Document initial confidence contemporaneously.**
6. **Preserve photographs, recordings, and other supporting evidence.**
7. **Record exposure to potentially relevant post-event information.**
8. **Distinguish what the witness personally observed from information learned later.**
9. **Compare testimony with independent evidence.**
10. **Consider the conditions under which the original observation occurred.**

These practices can help preserve information about both the memory and the circumstances in which it was obtained.

## Final Takeaway

Eyewitness testimony can be valuable, but psychological research demonstrates that human perception and memory have limitations. Memories are formed under particular viewing conditions, retained over time, and later retrieved through processes that can be influenced by questioning, post-event information, and identification procedures.

The strongest evaluation of eyewitness evidence therefore considers the entire history of the memory: **what the witness could observe, how the event was initially encoded, what happened during the retention period, how the witness was interviewed, and how any identification was conducted.**

For that reason, eyewitness testimony is often most informative when evaluated alongside independent evidence and when investigators preserve the witness's early account and identification circumstances as accurately as possible.
