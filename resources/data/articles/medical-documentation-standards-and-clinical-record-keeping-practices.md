Medical records are an essential part of modern healthcare. They document a patient's history, examinations, diagnoses, treatment decisions, medications, test results, responses to treatment, and ongoing care. They also allow clinicians to communicate with one another and provide a record that can be reviewed for billing, quality assurance, regulatory compliance, research, insurance, or legal purposes.

In the United States, medical-record requirements come from multiple sources rather than one universal documentation rule. Federal requirements may apply through programs such as Medicare and through laws such as HIPAA, while state laws, professional standards, accreditation requirements, and organizational policies can impose additional obligations.

CMS guidance, for example, emphasizes that medical records should be complete, legible, accurate, promptly completed, accessible, and properly maintained.

## 1. Identify the Correct Patient

Accurate patient identification is one of the foundations of clinical documentation.

A record should clearly correspond to the correct patient using appropriate identifiers. Depending on the healthcare system, these may include:

- Name
- Date of birth
- Medical record number
- Other approved identifiers

CMS guidance for clinical records emphasizes the importance of ensuring that information belongs to the correct patient.

Patient-identification procedures are particularly important when healthcare organizations have patients with similar names or when information is transferred between departments, facilities, or electronic systems.

## 2. Document the Reason for the Encounter

A clinical record should explain why the patient is receiving care.

Depending on the encounter, documentation may include:

- Chief complaint
- Symptoms
- Relevant history
- Reason for referral
- Follow-up purpose
- Relevant medical history
- Relevant medications
- Patient concerns

CMS guidance for evaluation and management services identifies the reason for the encounter and relevant history as components of appropriate medical-record documentation.

The level of detail should correspond to the circumstances and applicable documentation requirements rather than relying on unnecessary information.

## 3. Record Relevant Examination Findings

The medical record should document examination findings that are relevant to the patient's condition and the services provided.

Depending on the encounter, this may include:

- Vital signs
- Physical examination findings
- Neurological findings
- Cardiovascular findings
- Respiratory findings
- Musculoskeletal findings
- Skin findings
- Mental-status observations
- Other clinically relevant observations

Documentation should distinguish between what was observed, what the patient reported, and what the clinician concluded.

Clear distinctions can make the record easier for another healthcare professional to interpret.

## 4. Document Diagnostic Testing

When diagnostic tests are ordered or reviewed, the record should contain information necessary to explain their clinical relevance.

Depending on the service, documentation may include:

- Laboratory results
- Imaging findings
- Diagnostic reports
- Relevant previous test results
- The reason for ordering additional testing
- Interpretation of clinically significant findings

CMS guidance identifies documentation supporting the rationale for diagnostic and ancillary services as part of appropriate medical-record documentation.

The actual documentation requirements can vary depending on the service, payer, setting, and applicable regulations.

## 5. Record the Assessment and Clinical Impression

A clinical record should communicate the healthcare professional's assessment of the patient's condition.

This can include:

- Diagnosis
- Differential diagnosis
- Clinical impression
- Relevant risk factors
- Changes from previous visits
- Assessment of treatment response

CMS states that evaluation-and-management documentation should include an assessment, clinical impression, or diagnosis as appropriate.

A diagnosis should not be documented simply because it appears in an older record if the current encounter does not support its continued relevance.

## 6. Document the Treatment Plan

The record should explain what happens next.

Depending on the situation, the plan may include:

- Medication
- Procedure
- Referral
- Diagnostic testing
- Follow-up appointment
- Monitoring
- Patient education
- Lifestyle recommendations
- Additional evaluation
- Emergency precautions

When treatment changes, the record should provide enough information to explain the change.

CMS guidance specifically identifies a medical plan of care and documentation of patient progress and response to treatment as important components of medical records.

## 7. Include Medication Information

Medication documentation should be sufficiently clear to support safe treatment.

Relevant information can include:

- Medication name
- Dose
- Route
- Frequency
- Indication when relevant
- Start or stop information
- Allergies
- Medication changes
- Patient response or adverse effects when clinically relevant

Medication reconciliation can be particularly important when patients transition between healthcare settings or see multiple providers.

## 8. Date, Time, and Authenticate Entries

Clinical records need a reliable way to identify when information was recorded and who recorded it.

CMS materials emphasize the importance of author identification and record-maintenance systems that preserve the integrity and security of entries.

For organizations subject to specific federal requirements, entries may need appropriate authentication under applicable policies and regulations.

Authentication can involve:

- Electronic signatures
- Secure login credentials
- Approved digital-signature systems
- Handwritten signatures where permitted
- Other authorized authentication methods

Healthcare organizations should establish procedures that identify authorized users and maintain the integrity of their entries.

## 9. Complete Records Promptly

Timeliness is important because medical records are used by subsequent caregivers.

A delayed or incomplete entry can make it more difficult for another healthcare professional to understand what happened during an earlier encounter.

CMS guidance states that clinical records should be completed promptly so that information is available to subsequent caregivers.

Organizations may establish specific completion deadlines based on applicable regulations, payer requirements, accreditation standards, and internal policies.

## 10. Correct Errors Through Appropriate Procedures

Mistakes in medical records should be corrected through an approved process rather than simply deleting or concealing the original information.

HIPAA gives individuals a right to request an amendment to protected health information that is inaccurate or incomplete in a designated record set. If an amendment is accepted, the covered entity must identify the affected information and appropriately link or append the amendment.

Electronic health-record systems should therefore preserve an appropriate audit trail and prevent unauthorized alteration or destruction of protected health information.

The exact procedure for correcting a clinical entry can depend on the organization's policies and applicable law.

## 11. Preserve the Integrity of Electronic Records

Electronic health records create opportunities for efficient documentation but also require controls that protect the integrity of information.

The HIPAA Security Rule includes requirements involving access controls, audit controls, authentication, and mechanisms designed to protect electronic protected health information from unauthorized alteration or destruction.

Healthcare organizations should therefore have appropriate safeguards addressing:

- User authentication
- Access permissions
- Audit logs
- System security
- Data integrity
- Backup procedures
- Secure transmission
- Authorized amendments

The objective is to ensure that clinical information remains available and trustworthy while limiting unauthorized access.

## 12. Protect Patient Confidentiality

Medical records contain sensitive information and must be handled accordingly.

The HIPAA Privacy Rule establishes national standards governing the privacy of protected health information for covered entities and gives individuals rights concerning access to and correction of their health information.

Covered entities must also generally take reasonable steps to limit uses and disclosures of protected health information to the minimum necessary for the intended purpose, subject to exceptions and specific regulatory requirements.

Confidentiality procedures can include:

- Role-based access
- Secure messaging
- Password controls
- Physical safeguards
- Confidential disposal
- Privacy policies
- Employee training

## 13. Maintain Records for the Applicable Retention Period

There is no single federal HIPAA rule requiring healthcare providers to retain every medical record for a universal number of years.

HHS specifically states that the HIPAA Privacy Rule does not establish medical-record retention periods; state law generally determines how long medical records must be retained, subject to other applicable requirements.

This distinction is important because HIPAA's documentation-retention requirements are not the same thing as a universal medical-record retention period.

For example, HIPAA requires certain compliance documentation to be retained for six years, but that requirement does not mean every patient medical record must automatically be retained for exactly six years.

Healthcare organizations should therefore determine applicable federal, state, payer, accreditation, and organizational retention requirements.

## 14. Make Records Accessible to Authorized Users

Medical records should be retrievable when needed for patient care, authorized review, or other legitimate purposes.

CMS guidance states that medical records should be maintained so they can be accessed and retrieved promptly.

Poor record organization can interfere with continuity of care even when the underlying information exists.

Electronic systems should therefore have procedures for:

- Searching records
- Retrieving historical information
- Maintaining backups
- Handling system outages
- Transferring records appropriately
- Controlling authorized access

## 15. Understand Patients' Access Rights

HIPAA generally provides individuals with rights to access their protected health information held by covered entities, subject to specified exceptions.

HHS explains that patients generally have rights to examine and obtain copies of their medical records. They may also request amendments when they believe information is inaccurate or incomplete.

There are exceptions. For example, HIPAA treats separately maintained psychotherapy notes differently from ordinary medical and billing records.

Organizations should follow the applicable procedures and deadlines when responding to access or amendment requests.

## 16. Document Clinical Communication and Follow-Up

Important communication can be part of the clinical record.

Depending on the circumstances, documentation may include:

- Telephone encounters
- Patient messages
- Referral communications
- Consultation reports
- Follow-up instructions
- Test-result notifications
- Treatment discussions
- Communication with authorized caregivers

The purpose is to maintain continuity and provide a coherent record of clinically relevant decisions and communications.

## 17. Document Referrals and Transitions of Care

When a patient is referred to another clinician or transferred between facilities, relevant information should be communicated appropriately.

A useful transition record can include:

- Reason for referral
- Relevant diagnoses
- Current medications
- Important test results
- Treatment already provided
- Outstanding concerns
- Follow-up requirements

Accurate information transfer can reduce the risk that a subsequent clinician will have to reconstruct the patient's history from incomplete information.

## 18. Distinguish Clinical Documentation From Legal Conclusions

Medical records can become relevant in insurance disputes, disability claims, personal injury litigation, malpractice cases, workers' compensation proceedings, and other legal matters.

However, clinical documentation and legal conclusions serve different purposes.

A medical professional generally documents:

- Symptoms
- Findings
- Diagnoses
- Treatment
- Clinical reasoning
- Patient response

Whether those facts establish legal causation, negligence, disability, or damages is a separate question governed by applicable law and the evidence in the particular matter.

A medical record should therefore accurately reflect clinical observations rather than being written primarily to support a legal position.

## 19. Avoid Unsupported or Speculative Statements

Documentation should distinguish facts from assumptions.

For example, a clinician can document that a patient reports pain beginning after a particular event. That is different from conclusively stating that the event caused the condition when the clinical evidence does not establish causation.

Clear documentation can distinguish:

- Patient-reported information
- Objective findings
- Diagnostic results
- Clinical assessment
- Treatment decisions
- Areas of uncertainty

This approach can make the record more useful for both patient care and later review.

## 20. Prepare for Audits and Record Review

Medical records can be reviewed by healthcare organizations, payers, government programs, accreditation bodies, or authorized investigators.

CMS publishes guidance emphasizing accurate and supportive documentation for services billed to Medicare.

A well-maintained record should allow an authorized reviewer to understand:

1. Why the patient was seen.
2. What relevant information was obtained.
3. What examination or evaluation occurred.
4. What was determined.
5. Why tests or treatments were ordered.
6. What treatment was provided.
7. How the patient responded.
8. What follow-up was planned.

The exact documentation standard depends on the service and regulatory context.

## 21. Understand New Standards for Electronic Clinical Documentation

Healthcare documentation standards continue to evolve as electronic data exchange becomes more common.

In March 2026, CMS announced a final rule establishing HIPAA-adopted standards for certain health-care claims attachments and electronic signatures. The rule addresses secure electronic exchange of supporting clinical documentation such as medical records, clinical notes, imaging, telemedicine documentation, and laboratory results. The final rule became effective May 26, 2026, with compliance deadlines generally 24 months after the effective date.

This development illustrates the broader movement toward standardized electronic exchange of clinical information while maintaining authentication and security requirements.

## Final Takeaway

Effective medical documentation should be **accurate, complete, timely, identifiable, authenticated, secure, and accessible to authorized users**.

A useful clinical record should explain the patient's reason for care, relevant history and findings, diagnostic evaluation, assessment, treatment plan, progress, and follow-up. CMS guidance emphasizes the importance of complete and accurate records, while HIPAA establishes important privacy, access, amendment, security, and documentation requirements for covered entities.

Medical-record retention is more nuanced than a single nationwide rule. HIPAA does not establish a universal retention period for patient medical records; applicable state laws and other requirements may determine how long records must be maintained.

For healthcare organizations, maintaining clear documentation policies, reliable authentication, appropriate access controls, secure electronic systems, and procedures for correcting and preserving records can support both continuity of care and regulatory compliance.
