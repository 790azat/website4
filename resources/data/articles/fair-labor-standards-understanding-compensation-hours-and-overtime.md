Fair labor standards establish basic rules governing wages, working time, overtime compensation, recordkeeping, and other conditions of employment. In the United States, many of these requirements are established by the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), a federal law administered by the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division.

The FLSA creates minimum federal standards, but employees may also be protected by state or local wage-and-hour laws that provide greater protections. Understanding the distinction between federal requirements and additional state requirements is important when evaluating compensation, working hours, and overtime.

## Federal Minimum Wage Requirements

The FLSA establishes a federal minimum wage of **$7.25 per hour** for covered, nonexempt employees. This federal rate has remained in effect since July 24, 2009. Many states and local governments establish higher minimum wages, and employees covered by both federal and state requirements generally must receive the higher applicable rate.

Minimum-wage requirements generally apply to covered, nonexempt employees. Whether a particular worker is covered can depend on the employer, the employee's work, the organization's activities, and other provisions of the statute.

The FLSA also contains special rules concerning tipped employees, minors, certain occupations, and particular industries. Consequently, the general federal minimum wage should not be treated as the only wage rule potentially applicable to a particular employment relationship.

## Understanding Hours Worked

The FLSA generally requires covered employers to pay employees for time that qualifies as compensable work. The Department of Labor explains that hours worked ordinarily include periods when an employee is required to be on duty, on the employer's premises, or at another prescribed workplace. Time that an employer permits an employee to work can also be compensable.

This distinction can become important when employees perform work outside their scheduled shifts. For example, checking work-related communications, completing assignments after leaving the workplace, or performing other required activities may raise questions about whether the time must be counted.

Employers are generally responsible for maintaining accurate records of hours worked and wages paid for employees covered by the FLSA's recordkeeping provisions.

## The 40-Hour Overtime Standard

For covered, nonexempt employees, the federal overtime rule generally requires payment of at least **one and one-half times the employee's regular rate** for hours worked over 40 in a workweek.

The FLSA defines a workweek as a fixed and regularly recurring period of seven consecutive 24-hour periods, or 168 hours. It does not necessarily have to correspond to the calendar week. Hours generally cannot be averaged across two or more workweeks to avoid overtime obligations.

For example, an employee who works 45 hours during one workweek generally has five hours subject to the federal overtime requirement if the employee is covered and nonexempt.

The FLSA does not generally require overtime merely because work occurs on a Saturday, Sunday, holiday, or normal day of rest. The determining factor under the federal rule is generally the number of compensable hours worked during the applicable workweek.

## Calculating the Regular Rate

Overtime is not always calculated simply by multiplying an employee's ordinary hourly wage by 1.5. The FLSA's regular-rate rules can require employers to consider different forms of compensation when calculating overtime.

The Department of Labor explains that the regular rate generally includes remuneration for employment, subject to statutory exclusions. For employees receiving different forms of compensation, the regular rate can be calculated by dividing applicable compensation for the workweek by the total number of hours worked.

This can become particularly relevant for employees receiving commissions, bonuses, piece-rate compensation, or other forms of pay.

Certain payments are excluded from the regular-rate calculation under the FLSA. Because the treatment of compensation can depend on the nature and purpose of the payment, employers should review the applicable regulations rather than assuming that every payment is treated identically.

## Exempt and Nonexempt Employees

Not every employee is entitled to federal overtime under the same rules. The FLSA contains exemptions for particular categories of employees and industries.

The so-called "white-collar" exemptions can apply to qualifying executive, administrative, professional, outside-sales, and certain computer employees. Eligibility generally depends on both compensation and job duties. An employee's job title by itself does not determine whether an exemption applies.

For example, the executive exemption generally involves management responsibilities, supervision of other employees, and authority or meaningful influence over personnel decisions. Administrative and professional exemptions have their own requirements concerning the nature of the employee's work.

The Department of Labor emphasizes that exemptions are narrowly defined and that employers should examine the actual duties and compensation arrangements rather than relying solely on a position's title.

## Salaried Employees and Overtime

Receiving a salary does not automatically make an employee exempt from overtime. Certain exemptions require employees to satisfy specific salary-basis and duties requirements.

Under the Department of Labor's published guidance concerning the Part 541 exemptions, qualifying executive, administrative, and professional employees generally must satisfy applicable salary and duties tests. The standard salary level stated in that guidance is **$684 per week**, although employers should verify the current legal requirements and applicable regulations when making classification decisions.

An employee who receives a salary but does not satisfy an applicable exemption can remain entitled to overtime protection.

Misclassification can create significant wage-and-hour disputes, particularly when an employee regularly works substantially more than 40 hours per week.

## Recordkeeping and Time Tracking

Accurate timekeeping is an important component of wage compliance. Employers covered by the FLSA's recordkeeping requirements must maintain records concerning employees' working time and compensation.

Modern workplaces can make time tracking more complicated. Employees may work remotely, use mobile devices, travel between locations, or perform work outside conventional office hours.

Organizations can reduce uncertainty by establishing clear procedures for recording work time and communicating expectations about unauthorized or unscheduled work. However, simply instructing employees not to work overtime does not necessarily eliminate the obligation to compensate qualifying work that the employer knows about or permits.

## State and Local Wage Laws

Federal law establishes a baseline, but state and local laws can provide additional protections. Minimum wages vary substantially across the United States, and some jurisdictions impose overtime requirements that differ from the federal framework.

The Department of Labor's current state minimum-wage table shows numerous jurisdictions with minimum wages above the federal $7.25 rate as of July 1, 2026.

Some state laws also contain different rules concerning daily overtime, meal periods, rest breaks, reporting time, final pay, or other employment matters. Employers therefore generally need to evaluate both federal and applicable state or local requirements.

Where an employee is covered by multiple applicable wage laws, the rule providing the greater protection may determine the employee's entitlement.

## Employer Policies and Overtime

Employers can establish policies requiring employees to obtain approval before working overtime. Such policies can help organizations manage staffing and labor costs.

However, workplace policies should be distinguished from the legal obligation to pay qualifying overtime. The FLSA generally requires overtime compensation when a covered, nonexempt employee works more than 40 hours in a workweek, even where the employer has established internal rules concerning authorization.

Employers may address violations of legitimate workplace policies separately, but disciplinary procedures should not be used as a substitute for paying wages legally owed.

## Common Wage-and-Hour Issues

Disputes concerning fair labor standards can arise from several circumstances, including:

- Misclassification of employees as exempt
- Failure to count all compensable working time
- Incorrect overtime calculations
- Unpaid work performed before or after scheduled shifts
- Improper treatment of commissions or bonuses
- Inaccurate time records
- Unlawful deductions
- Failure to apply a higher state or local minimum wage
- Incorrect treatment of tipped employees

The Department of Labor notes that deductions may not reduce an employee's wages below the applicable minimum wage or reduce required overtime compensation.

Resolving these issues often requires examining payroll records, schedules, timekeeping data, job duties, employment policies, and the law applicable to the particular workplace.

## Conclusion

Fair labor standards establish important protections concerning compensation, working hours, overtime, and recordkeeping. Under the federal FLSA, covered nonexempt employees generally must receive at least the federal minimum wage and overtime compensation at a rate of at least one and one-half times the regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek.

At the same time, exemptions, state and local wage laws, compensation structures, and industry-specific rules can substantially affect the requirements applicable to a particular employee.

Accurate classification, reliable time records, proper overtime calculations, and regular review of applicable wage laws can help employers maintain compliant compensation systems. Employees who believe they have not received required wages may need to review both federal and state protections and consult an appropriate labor or employment professional regarding their circumstances.
