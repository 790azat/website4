Modern corporations operate within increasingly complex legal, regulatory, financial, technological, and operational environments. Effective governance and compliance systems help organizations establish clear responsibilities, monitor risk, maintain reliable reporting, and provide mechanisms for accountability.

Corporate governance concerns the structures and relationships through which a company is directed and supervised. Compliance focuses more specifically on identifying applicable legal and regulatory obligations and developing systems to meet them. Although these functions are distinct, they are closely connected. The 2023 G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance emphasize board oversight, transparency, accountability, internal controls, ethics, compliance, and risk management as important components of an effective governance framework.

## Establishing Clear Governance Responsibilities

A modern governance framework begins with clearly defined responsibilities among shareholders, directors, executives, committees, and other relevant stakeholders.

Boards generally provide strategic oversight and monitor management, while executives are responsible for implementing business strategy and managing day-to-day operations. The exact division of responsibilities varies according to jurisdiction, corporate structure, listing requirements, and applicable law.

The OECD Principles state that governance frameworks should support the board's strategic guidance of the company, effective monitoring of management, and accountability to the company and shareholders.

Written governance documents can reinforce these divisions. Articles of incorporation, bylaws, committee charters, delegation policies, codes of conduct, and internal procedures can establish who has authority to make particular decisions and how those decisions should be documented.

## Conducting Regular Risk Assessments

Compliance programs should be based on the company's actual risk profile rather than consisting only of generic policies.

Risk assessments can examine factors such as:

- The jurisdictions in which the company operates
- Industry-specific regulations
- Products and services
- Customers and suppliers
- Government relationships
- Third-party intermediaries
- Financial transactions
- Data and technology systems
- Employment practices
- Environmental obligations
- Anti-corruption and fraud risks

The OECD's 2025 work on corporate anti-corruption compliance emphasizes risk-based compliance programs that account for a company's size, structure, geographic and industry exposure, business partners, transactions, and regulatory environment. It also recommends regularly monitoring and reassessing risks so compliance resources can be adjusted when circumstances change.

## Developing Effective Internal Controls

Internal controls provide mechanisms for preventing, detecting, and correcting problems.

Financial controls may include authorization requirements, segregation of duties, reconciliation procedures, audit trails, approval thresholds, and periodic reviews. Operational controls can address purchasing, inventory, information security, contracting, customer interactions, and other business processes.

The OECD governance framework identifies internal controls, risk management, financial and operational controls, and oversight of compliance programs as important board responsibilities.

Controls should also be proportionate to the organization. A multinational corporation may require complex compliance infrastructure, while a smaller enterprise may use simpler procedures that nevertheless establish clear accountability and documentation.

## Strengthening Board and Committee Oversight

Board oversight becomes more effective when directors receive relevant information in a timely and understandable form.

Boards and committees may review:

- Major legal and regulatory risks
- Internal audit findings
- Compliance incidents
- Material litigation
- Financial reporting
- Cybersecurity and technology risks
- Conflicts of interest
- Third-party risks
- Regulatory investigations
- Remediation efforts

Committee structures vary between corporations and jurisdictions. Audit, risk, compensation, nomination, and compliance-related committees may have different responsibilities depending on the organization's structure.

The objective is not simply to create committees but to ensure that significant risks reach the appropriate decision-makers and that management remains accountable for addressing identified problems.

## Creating a Strong Compliance Culture

A compliance program can be weakened if employees perceive policies as formal documents with little connection to actual management decisions.

Leadership therefore plays an important role in establishing expectations. The OECD's anti-corruption guidance identifies visible commitment from boards and senior management as a component of effective internal controls, ethics, and compliance programs.

A compliance culture can be supported through:

- Clear codes of conduct
- Regular employee training
- Accessible reporting channels
- Consistent disciplinary procedures
- Conflict-of-interest disclosures
- Management accountability
- Periodic compliance assessments
- Recognition of appropriate ethical behavior

Policies should also be communicated in a way employees can understand and apply to their actual responsibilities.

## Managing Third-Party and Supply-Chain Risk

Corporate compliance responsibilities can extend beyond direct employees. Vendors, distributors, consultants, agents, contractors, joint-venture partners, and other intermediaries may create legal and reputational risks.

The OECD Principles specifically identify the importance of extending compliance programs, where appropriate, to subsidiaries and third parties such as agents, consultants, distributors, contractors, suppliers, and joint-venture partners.

A third-party compliance strategy may therefore include due diligence before engagement, contractual compliance provisions, risk-based monitoring, documentation requirements, and procedures for responding to identified violations.

The level of diligence should reflect the nature and risk of the relationship. Excessive procedures can consume resources without materially improving risk management, while insufficient diligence may leave significant exposure unidentified.

## Implementing Reporting and Whistleblower Channels

Employees and other stakeholders may identify misconduct before management or auditors do. Effective reporting systems can provide structured mechanisms for raising concerns.

Depending on the organization and applicable law, reporting channels may include:

- Direct management reporting
- Human resources channels
- Compliance departments
- Internal audit
- Independent hotlines
- Online reporting systems
- Board or audit committee escalation

A reporting mechanism should be accompanied by procedures for receiving, assessing, investigating, documenting, and resolving allegations.

Confidentiality and anti-retaliation protections may also be important. Their precise requirements depend on applicable law and the nature of the report.

## Integrating Internal Audit and Compliance

Internal audit and compliance perform related but different functions.

Compliance generally focuses on meeting applicable laws, regulations, policies, and standards. Internal audit can independently evaluate governance, risk management, and internal control processes.

The OECD's 2025 methodology notes that internal audit can support audit committees by evaluating governance, risk management, and internal controls. It also identifies reporting relationships that can help preserve appropriate independence for internal audit functions.

Clear separation of responsibilities can reduce duplication while ensuring that significant findings are communicated to appropriate oversight bodies.

## Maintaining Accurate Records and Transparency

Documentation is a central component of corporate compliance. Companies should be able to demonstrate how important decisions were authorized, how risks were evaluated, and how identified problems were addressed.

Depending on the organization, relevant records may include:

- Board minutes
- Committee materials
- Compliance assessments
- Training records
- Internal audit reports
- Investigation files
- Third-party due-diligence records
- Financial documentation
- Regulatory filings
- Remediation plans

For public companies and other regulated organizations, disclosure and transparency requirements can impose additional obligations. The OECD Principles identify disclosure and transparency as fundamental components of corporate governance frameworks.

Recordkeeping policies should also account for privacy, confidentiality, document-retention requirements, litigation holds, and applicable data-protection laws.

## Monitoring, Testing, and Remediation

A compliance program should not remain static after policies are adopted.

Periodic testing can determine whether controls are actually functioning. Monitoring can identify recurring problems, unusual transactions, policy violations, training deficiencies, or changes in regulatory exposure.

When a problem is identified, remediation should address both the immediate issue and its underlying cause. Depending on the circumstances, remediation may involve additional training, revised controls, disciplinary action, contractual changes, technology improvements, or modifications to reporting procedures.

The OECD framework emphasizes that compliance systems should be monitored and adapted as corporate circumstances and risks change.

## Adapting Governance to Corporate Growth

Governance structures that work for a small company may become inadequate as the organization expands across jurisdictions, adds subsidiaries, raises external capital, or becomes subject to additional regulation.

Growth can require changes to:

- Board composition
- Delegation of authority
- Internal audit capacity
- Compliance staffing
- Risk reporting
- Third-party oversight
- Information systems
- Financial controls
- Regulatory reporting

The 2023 G20/OECD Principles are designed as a flexible international reference rather than a substitute for domestic legislation. Their implementation depends on each jurisdiction's legal and regulatory framework.

Corporations should therefore adapt governance frameworks to the laws and regulatory requirements applicable to their specific operations rather than treating international principles as universal legal rules.

## Conclusion

Effective corporate governance and compliance require more than written policies. Modern organizations need clearly defined responsibilities, risk-based compliance programs, effective internal controls, reliable reporting systems, appropriate board oversight, third-party safeguards, and mechanisms for investigation and remediation.

International governance standards such as the 2023 G20/OECD Principles provide a framework for considering these elements, while domestic legislation, regulatory requirements, industry standards, and listing rules determine the obligations applicable to particular corporations.

A sustainable governance strategy should therefore evolve alongside the company's operations and risk profile. Regular assessment, transparent documentation, independent oversight, and consistent enforcement of internal standards can help corporations maintain accountable structures while responding to changing legal and commercial conditions.
