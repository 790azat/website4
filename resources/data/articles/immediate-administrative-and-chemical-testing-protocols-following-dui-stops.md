A DUI or impaired-driving stop can initiate a sequence of roadside observations, administrative procedures, sobriety testing, arrest decisions, and chemical testing. The precise rules differ substantially among jurisdictions, but the process generally involves gathering evidence about a driver's condition and determining whether statutory requirements for further enforcement have been met.

In the United States, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) describes impaired-driving detection as a process involving observations of driving behavior, contact with the driver, standardized field sobriety testing, and, depending on state law and agency procedures, preliminary breath testing before an arrest decision.

## Initial Traffic Stop and Administrative Procedures

A DUI investigation generally begins with a lawful traffic stop or another circumstance that brings an officer into contact with a driver. The officer may document driving behavior, physical observations, statements, and other circumstances that contribute to the investigation.

Administrative procedures can include recording identifying information, vehicle and licensing information, the reason for the stop, and observations made during the encounter.

The precise legal standard for initiating a stop varies by jurisdiction. Likewise, the authority to continue an investigation after the initial stop depends on the facts observed and applicable law.

## Roadside Observations

Officers may look for indications that a driver could be impaired. NHTSA's standardized detection framework includes observations made during the initial phases of an impaired-driving investigation before formal field sobriety testing.

Potential observations can include driving behavior, appearance, speech, coordination, responses to questions, and other circumstances. Officers are generally expected to document observations rather than simply state a conclusion that a driver appeared impaired.

The distinction matters because later proceedings may examine whether the officer had an adequate factual basis for continuing the investigation or making an arrest.

## Standardized Field Sobriety Testing

NHTSA's Standardized Field Sobriety Testing program uses a three-test battery consisting of the horizontal gaze nystagmus test, the walk-and-turn test, and the one-leg-stand test.

These tests are intended to assist trained officers in evaluating possible impairment. They are not themselves equivalent to a laboratory measurement of blood alcohol concentration.

NHTSA's current training materials describe the field sobriety testing stage as part of the pre-arrest screening process. Officers consider the test results together with observations from earlier stages before deciding whether probable cause exists for an arrest.

Physical conditions, environmental circumstances, footwear, injuries, medical conditions, and other factors can potentially affect performance. Consequently, the circumstances surrounding a test may become relevant when its reliability is later evaluated.

## Preliminary Breath Testing

A preliminary breath test, commonly called a PBT, is a roadside screening tool that estimates breath alcohol concentration. NHTSA distinguishes PBTs from evidential breath-testing instruments used to obtain measurements that may be introduced as evidence in court.

Depending on state law and agency policy, an officer may use a PBT during the investigation to help determine whether additional enforcement action is appropriate.

A roadside screening result should therefore not automatically be treated as identical to an evidential chemical test. The legal significance of a particular device and result depends on the jurisdiction, instrument, testing procedures, and applicable evidentiary rules.

## The Arrest Decision

The arrest decision represents an important transition in many DUI investigations. NHTSA training materials explain that officers use the available observations and field sobriety evidence to determine whether there is sufficient probable cause for an arrest.

The existence and timing of probable cause requirements are matters of constitutional and state law. NHTSA's materials also explain that, in the standard implied-consent framework, the chemical-test request generally follows an arrest for impaired driving.

This distinction is important because roadside screening and post-arrest evidential testing can serve different purposes.

## Chemical Testing After Arrest

Chemical testing can involve breath, blood, or urine, depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances. The purpose is generally to determine the presence or concentration of alcohol and, where authorized, other drugs.

NHTSA identifies preliminary breath tests, evidential breath tests, and screening or confirmatory testing for alcohol and other impairing drugs as different tools that may be incorporated into impaired-driving enforcement systems.

The precise test required or permitted depends on local law. Some jurisdictions establish specific procedures concerning the selection of the test, timing, warnings provided to the driver, medical considerations, and documentation.

## Implied Consent Laws

Many U.S. jurisdictions have implied-consent laws governing chemical testing of drivers suspected of impaired driving. Under this framework, operating a motor vehicle can carry statutory consequences concerning chemical testing after specified legal conditions are satisfied.

NHTSA explains that implied-consent laws generally provide that drivers implicitly consent to chemical testing when the applicable conditions are met and that refusal can result in administrative license sanctions.

Importantly, the consequences of refusal differ by jurisdiction. Administrative license suspension or revocation can be separate from the criminal prosecution arising from the underlying DUI allegation.

## Chemical-Test Refusal

A driver who refuses a requested chemical test may face consequences independent of the ultimate outcome of a DUI prosecution.

NHTSA reports that all states have implied-consent laws and that, with the exception of Wyoming under the framework described by NHTSA, states generally impose administrative sanctions for BAC-test refusal. The exact duration and nature of sanctions vary significantly among jurisdictions.

Some jurisdictions may also permit evidence of refusal to be considered in a criminal proceeding, while others establish additional statutory procedures. A driver therefore should not assume that refusing a chemical test has no legal consequences.

## Blood Testing and Medical Circumstances

Blood testing can involve additional procedural and medical considerations compared with breath testing. Depending on jurisdictional law, blood may be obtained with consent, pursuant to a warrant, under a statutory exception, or through another legally authorized procedure.

Certain medical conditions can also affect the procedure. For example, federal regulations governing impaired-driving testing on military installations contain specific rules for individuals with hemophilia, blood-clotting disorders, or certain anticoagulant treatment.

Those rules illustrate why chemical-testing procedures should not be generalized across jurisdictions. The applicable state or federal framework determines what authority exists and what procedures must be followed.

## Drug-Impaired Driving

Alcohol testing is only one component of impaired-driving enforcement. When impairment is suspected to involve drugs, the investigation can require different techniques and testing procedures.

NHTSA notes that drug-impaired-driving investigations can be particularly complex because observable impairment may not correspond to a particular BAC result. The agency describes Advanced Roadside Impaired Driving Enforcement and Drug Evaluation and Classification programs as specialized training frameworks for identifying and documenting possible drug impairment.

Drug testing may involve biological samples and laboratory analysis. A positive test for a substance does not necessarily answer every legal question about impairment, because the significance of a detected substance depends on the substance, concentration, timing, jurisdictional law, and other evidence.

## Documentation and Evidence Preservation

Documentation is a central part of DUI enforcement. NHTSA training materials identify post-arrest observations, statements, the chemical-test request, warnings or admonitions, testing procedures, and test results as matters that may be documented by investigators.

Records can include:

- Officer observations
- Dash-camera or body-camera recordings
- Field sobriety test documentation
- Preliminary breath-test information
- Arrest records
- Chemical-test warnings
- Breath-test instrument records
- Blood or urine collection records
- Laboratory reports
- Chain-of-custody documentation
- Statements made during the investigation

The availability and legal significance of these records depend on applicable law and agency procedures.

## Administrative and Criminal Proceedings

A DUI incident can produce more than one legal process. A driver's license may be subject to an administrative suspension or revocation while the criminal case proceeds separately.

These proceedings can involve different standards, deadlines, and procedures. For example, an administrative license proceeding may focus on whether statutory conditions for suspension were satisfied, while the criminal prosecution addresses whether the elements of the offense have been established.

The relationship between the two proceedings varies by jurisdiction.

## Reviewing the Testing Process

When a DUI case is later reviewed, the testing process may be examined from several perspectives:

1. **Legality of the stop:** Was the initial encounter legally authorized?
2. **Basis for further investigation:** Were sufficient facts present to continue the DUI investigation?
3. **Field sobriety procedures:** Were standardized procedures followed where applicable?
4. **Arrest:** Was there a legally sufficient basis for arrest?
5. **Chemical-test request:** Were required warnings and procedures followed?
6. **Testing method:** Was the appropriate test administered?
7. **Instrument or laboratory procedures:** Were applicable requirements satisfied?
8. **Evidence handling:** Was the sample properly documented and preserved?
9. **Administrative consequences:** Were statutory license procedures followed?
10. **Jurisdiction:** Which laws and procedural requirements govern the particular case?

These questions are highly jurisdiction-specific. A procedural issue that matters in one state may have a different legal effect in another.

## Conclusion

The period immediately following a DUI stop can involve several distinct stages: initial observations, field sobriety testing, preliminary screening, arrest, chemical testing, administrative licensing procedures, and evidence documentation.

Chemical testing is an important part of many impaired-driving investigations, but roadside screening, evidential testing, and drug testing serve different functions. Similarly, refusal of a chemical test can trigger administrative consequences that are separate from the criminal case.

Because DUI procedures are heavily dependent on jurisdiction, the applicable statutes, regulations, constitutional rules, and agency procedures should be examined for the specific location involved. NHTSA's national materials provide a useful framework for understanding the general investigative process, but they do not replace the law governing an individual case.
