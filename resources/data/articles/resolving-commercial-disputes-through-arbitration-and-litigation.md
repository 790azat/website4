Commercial disputes can arise from almost any business relationship. Contractual disagreements, payment disputes, partnership conflicts, intellectual property claims, construction disagreements, and allegations of breach can all require formal dispute-resolution procedures. When negotiations do not resolve the problem, businesses may turn to arbitration or litigation.

Arbitration and litigation share the objective of resolving a legal dispute, but they operate through different procedural frameworks. Litigation takes place within the court system, while arbitration generally involves a private proceeding before one or more arbitrators selected according to an agreement or applicable rules. The appropriate process depends on the contract, applicable law, nature of the dispute, desired remedies, and procedural circumstances.

## Understanding Commercial Litigation

Commercial litigation is the traditional court-based method of resolving business disputes. A case generally begins when one party files a complaint or other initiating pleading against another party with a court that has jurisdiction.

The litigation process can involve:

- Pleadings and preliminary motions
- Discovery and document production
- Depositions
- Expert testimony
- Evidentiary disputes
- Settlement negotiations
- Trial
- Judgment
- Appeals where permitted

Court procedures are governed by applicable procedural and evidentiary rules. Federal cases, for example, may be subject to the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and Federal Rules of Evidence, while state courts operate under their respective state rules.

## Understanding Commercial Arbitration

Arbitration is a form of alternative dispute resolution in which the parties submit a dispute to an arbitrator or panel rather than having the matter determined through a conventional court trial.

Commercial arbitration usually depends on an arbitration agreement. That agreement may be included in the original commercial contract or entered into later after a dispute develops.

Organizations such as the American Arbitration Association provide procedural frameworks for commercial arbitration. AAA's current Commercial Arbitration Rules address matters including filing, arbitrator selection, information exchange, hearings, and awards.

## The Importance of the Arbitration Clause

An arbitration clause can significantly affect how a future dispute is handled. Commercial contracts may specify that disputes arising from or relating to the agreement must be submitted to arbitration.

The clause may also identify:

- The arbitration administrator
- Applicable procedural rules
- The number of arbitrators
- The location of proceedings
- Governing law
- Procedures for selecting arbitrators
- Whether mediation must occur first
- Requirements concerning confidentiality
- The ability to seek judicial relief

The American Arbitration Association publishes model commercial arbitration clauses and alternative provisions combining mediation with arbitration.

Because arbitration provisions can affect the parties' future procedural rights, they should be reviewed carefully when the underlying contract is negotiated.

## Initiating an Arbitration

When a dispute falls within an enforceable arbitration agreement, the claimant generally initiates proceedings according to the applicable arbitration rules.

For an AAA-administered commercial matter, the process can involve submitting a demand for arbitration and the applicable filing materials. The respondent may then submit a response and any counterclaims permitted under the applicable rules.

The arbitration administrator may assist with procedural administration and arbitrator appointment. The precise process depends on the parties' agreement and the rules governing the proceeding.

## Selecting an Arbitrator

The selection of the decision-maker is an important distinction between arbitration and litigation.

In litigation, the dispute is assigned to a judge under the court system's procedures, with juries potentially deciding questions of fact in appropriate cases. In arbitration, the parties generally participate in selecting the arbitrator or arbitrators according to their agreement or applicable institutional rules.

Commercial arbitration may allow the parties to consider professional experience relevant to the dispute. For example, a technically complex business disagreement might involve an arbitrator with substantial experience in the relevant commercial field.

The selection process must still comply with applicable rules concerning neutrality and disclosure.

## Information Exchange and Evidence

Both arbitration and litigation require parties to present evidence supporting their positions, but the procedures can differ.

Court litigation may involve extensive formal discovery, including interrogatories, requests for production, requests for admission, depositions, and subpoenas. Arbitration procedures can provide information-exchange mechanisms while allowing the parties and arbitrator greater flexibility in determining the appropriate scope.

The applicable arbitration rules are therefore important. The AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules, for example, contain procedures governing information exchange and hearings.

Businesses should preserve relevant contracts, correspondence, invoices, financial records, meeting notes, electronic communications, and other potentially important evidence as soon as a significant dispute becomes reasonably foreseeable.

## Court Litigation and Arbitration Compared

The differences between the two processes can be summarized broadly:

| Issue | Litigation | Arbitration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision-maker | Judge and, where applicable, jury | Arbitrator or arbitration panel |
| Forum | Public court system | Private dispute-resolution proceeding |
| Procedure | Governed by court rules | Governed by agreement and arbitration rules |
| Discovery | Often governed by formal procedural rules | Often more flexible, depending on rules |
| Trial/hearing | Court trial | Arbitration hearing |
| Decision | Judgment | Arbitration award |
| Appeal/review | Generally broader appellate framework | Judicial review is generally more limited |
| Privacy | Court proceedings may be publicly accessible | Proceedings are generally private, subject to applicable rules and law |

These are general distinctions rather than universal rules. Specific contracts, statutes, arbitration rules, and jurisdictions can substantially change the procedure.

## Costs and Time Considerations

Cost and duration can be important considerations in commercial dispute resolution.

Litigation may involve filing fees, attorneys' fees, discovery expenses, expert witnesses, court reporting, and trial-related costs. Complex litigation can continue for substantial periods, particularly when discovery and appeals are extensive.

Arbitration also has costs. In addition to legal and expert expenses, parties may incur administrative fees and arbitrator compensation. The AAA publishes fee schedules for commercial arbitration and provides an administrative fee calculator for certain proceedings.

It is therefore inaccurate to assume that arbitration will always be less expensive than litigation. The financial impact depends on the complexity, value, duration, procedural requirements, and number of parties involved.

## Privacy and Confidentiality

Businesses sometimes choose arbitration because the proceeding is conducted outside the traditional public courtroom environment. However, privacy and confidentiality are not necessarily identical concepts.

Whether information must remain confidential depends on the parties' agreement, applicable arbitration rules, protective orders, statutes, and other legal requirements.

Commercial contracts should therefore address confidentiality separately when the parties have legitimate reasons to protect trade secrets, proprietary information, customer data, or other sensitive business information.

## Remedies and Enforcement

Both arbitration and litigation can result in legally significant decisions, but the mechanisms differ.

A court may issue a judgment that can generally be enforced through the applicable judicial enforcement procedures. An arbitrator issues an award, which may subsequently be recognized or enforced through a court where required.

Commercial arbitration clauses commonly contemplate that a judgment on an arbitration award may be entered in a court having jurisdiction. The AAA's standard commercial arbitration clause reflects this structure.

International disputes can involve additional enforcement considerations. The applicable arbitration agreement, national law, and international conventions may affect recognition and enforcement in another country.

## Settlement During Arbitration or Litigation

Formal proceedings do not necessarily prevent the parties from reaching a settlement.

Businesses may negotiate directly, participate in mediation, or settle during arbitration or litigation. The American Arbitration Association also provides commercial mediation procedures, including models that place mediation before arbitration or allow mediation to proceed concurrently with arbitration.

A settlement can resolve the dispute without requiring the decision-maker to issue a final determination on every contested issue.

## Choosing a Dispute-Resolution Framework

Businesses should consider several factors when drafting contracts or evaluating an existing dispute-resolution clause.

Relevant questions include:

1. Does the contract contain a valid arbitration agreement?
2. Which jurisdiction's law governs the agreement?
3. Where can litigation or arbitration take place?
4. What types of disputes are covered?
5. Which procedural rules apply?
6. How will the decision-maker be selected?
7. What remedies are available?
8. What are the likely discovery requirements?
9. What confidentiality protections exist?
10. How can a judgment or award be enforced?

These questions can help businesses understand the procedural consequences before a dispute becomes more complicated.

## International Commercial Disputes

Cross-border commercial disputes may require additional planning because multiple legal systems can be involved.

International arbitration can provide a contractual mechanism for resolving disputes between businesses located in different countries. The AAA's International Centre for Dispute Resolution maintains separate international arbitration procedures and rules for cross-border cases.

International agreements should carefully address governing law, arbitration seat, language, applicable institutional rules, service procedures, and enforcement considerations.

## Preparing for a Commercial Dispute

Whether a business expects arbitration or litigation, early preparation can improve the organization's ability to understand and present its position.

Businesses should preserve relevant documents, identify important contractual provisions, establish a timeline of events, document financial consequences, and maintain communications concerning the dispute.

It is also important to review the original agreement before taking procedural action. A dispute-resolution clause may impose specific notice requirements, negotiation periods, mediation obligations, arbitration procedures, or jurisdictional restrictions.

## Conclusion

Arbitration and litigation provide different frameworks for resolving commercial disputes. Litigation operates through the court system and its formal procedural rules, while arbitration relies on an agreement that transfers decision-making to an arbitrator or panel under specified procedures.

Neither mechanism operates identically in every case. Contract language, governing law, procedural rules, the value and complexity of the dispute, available remedies, and enforcement considerations can all influence how a commercial disagreement proceeds.

For businesses, careful drafting at the beginning of a commercial relationship can be just as important as dispute strategy after a conflict develops. A clearly written dispute-resolution clause can establish expectations regarding the forum, procedures, decision-maker, and enforcement process before disagreements arise.
