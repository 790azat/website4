A criminal jury trial is a structured judicial proceeding in which the prosecution presents evidence intended to establish the defendant's guilt, the defense has an opportunity to challenge that evidence and present its own case when appropriate, and a jury ultimately determines whether the prosecution has met its burden of proof. Although procedures vary among jurisdictions, federal criminal trials generally follow a recognizable sequence from jury selection and opening statements through presentation of evidence, closing arguments, jury instructions, deliberations, and the verdict.

Understanding these stages helps explain what happens inside the courtroom and why each phase has a particular function.

## Jury Selection and Preliminary Instructions

Before opening statements begin, prospective jurors are questioned and selected for service. The process is intended to identify jurors who can decide the case impartially and follow the court's instructions.

Once the jury is selected and sworn, the judge may provide preliminary instructions concerning the jurors' responsibilities, courtroom procedures, and basic legal principles. Federal court materials explain that jurors are instructed about their duties and the manner in which the trial will proceed.

The judge may also address matters concerning courtroom conduct, communication, and the treatment of evidence. These preliminary instructions establish the framework within which the jury will hear the case.

## Opening Statements

Opening statements are generally the first substantive presentations by the attorneys.

The prosecution normally presents its opening statement first because it carries the burden of proving the charged offenses. Defense counsel may then make an opening statement, waive it, or, under certain federal procedures, reserve it until later in the prosecution's presentation.

An opening statement is not evidence. Instead, it provides the jury with an overview of what the attorney expects the evidence to establish. The federal courts describe opening statements as a roadmap of the expected evidence rather than an opportunity to argue the ultimate merits of the case.

This distinction is important. Jurors are expected to decide the case based on evidence admitted during the trial, not on statements made by lawyers.

## The Prosecution's Case

After opening statements, the prosecution presents its evidence.

The government's evidence may include testimony from witnesses, physical evidence, documents, photographs, recordings, forensic evidence, or other materials admitted under the applicable rules of evidence.

Witnesses generally testify under questioning by the attorney who called them. The opposing side then has an opportunity to cross-examine them. Cross-examination can address the witness's observations, memory, credibility, inconsistencies, or other matters relevant to the testimony.

Attorneys may also raise objections when they believe a question or proposed evidence violates an applicable rule. The judge decides whether the objection should be sustained or overruled.

The jury is expected to distinguish between evidence and attorney statements. Model federal jury instructions specifically explain that lawyers' questions, objections, opening statements, and closing arguments are not themselves evidence.

## Motions for Judgment of Acquittal

After the prosecution has presented its evidence, the defense may make a motion for judgment of acquittal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29.

The motion argues that the prosecution's evidence is legally insufficient to sustain a conviction. Federal court guidance explains that such a motion may also be made after the close of all evidence.

If the court grants the motion, the relevant charge may be resolved without requiring the jury to reach a verdict on that count. If the motion is denied, the trial generally continues.

This stage illustrates the distinction between the judge's role and the jury's role. The judge determines whether the evidence is legally sufficient to permit a conviction, while the jury ultimately determines factual questions under the court's instructions.

## The Defense Case

If the case continues, the defense may present evidence.

The defendant, however, does not carry the burden of proving innocence. The U.S. Courts explain that the burden of proof remains on the government, which must establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

Defense evidence may include witness testimony, documents, expert evidence, physical evidence, or other admissible material supporting the defense's position.

The defendant also has the option, within applicable constitutional and procedural rules, not to testify. A defendant's decision not to present evidence does not transfer the prosecution's burden of proof to the defense.

When defense witnesses testify, the prosecution generally has an opportunity to cross-examine them.

## Rebuttal Evidence

After the defense has presented its case, the prosecution may sometimes offer rebuttal evidence.

Rebuttal is intended to respond to matters raised by the defense rather than simply repeat the prosecution's original presentation. Federal court materials describe rebuttal as evidence presented to contradict or counter evidence introduced by the opposing party.

The precise scope and availability of rebuttal can depend on the evidence presented and the court's rulings.

## Closing Arguments

Once the evidentiary phase is complete, the attorneys present closing arguments.

Unlike opening statements, closing arguments are intended to analyze the evidence and persuade the jury about what conclusions should be drawn from it. Attorneys may discuss witness credibility, inconsistencies, physical evidence, and how the evidence relates to the legal elements of the charged offenses.

In federal criminal trials, the prosecution generally argues first, followed by the defense, with the prosecution receiving an opportunity for rebuttal. This sequence reflects the prosecution's burden of proof.

Closing arguments still do not become evidence. Jurors must base their decision on evidence admitted during the trial and the law provided by the judge.

## Jury Instructions

After the evidence and arguments, the judge instructs the jury on the law that applies to the case.

These instructions explain the legal elements the prosecution must establish and the standards the jury must apply when evaluating the evidence. They can also address concepts such as the presumption of innocence and proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 30 allows parties to request jury instructions and establishes procedures for objections to the court's proposed instructions.

The instructions are particularly important because jurors do not decide the case according to their own preferred definition of the law. Their role is to determine the facts and apply the law as explained by the court.

## Jury Deliberations

After receiving the court's instructions, the jury retires to deliberate.

Deliberations take place privately. Jurors review the evidence admitted during trial, discuss the factual issues, and apply the legal instructions provided by the judge. Federal court guidance explains that jurors may submit questions concerning the evidence or instructions through the procedure established by the court.

The length of deliberations can vary significantly. Some cases may be resolved relatively quickly, while complex cases can require substantially more discussion.

The jury's task is not to determine whether the defendant appears generally likeable, whether the allegations are disturbing, or whether a particular attorney presented a compelling speech. Its decision must be based on the evidence and applicable law.

## The Verdict

At the conclusion of deliberations, the jury returns its verdict in court.

In federal criminal cases, the jury must generally reach a unanimous verdict. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 31 provides for the return of the verdict in open court and addresses circumstances involving multiple defendants or multiple counts.

A verdict may find a defendant guilty or not guilty on particular charges. Different counts can therefore produce different results.

If the jury cannot reach agreement on one or more counts, the court may declare a mistrial as to those counts. Federal Rule 31 specifically addresses situations involving jury disagreement and possible retrial.

A not-guilty verdict is not the same as a judicial finding that every factual allegation was disproved. Rather, it means the prosecution did not obtain the required verdict of guilt under the applicable legal standard. In federal criminal cases, the government bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

## What Happens After the Verdict?

The trial itself ends with the verdict, but the criminal case may continue.

If the defendant is found guilty, sentencing generally occurs separately. The federal courts explain that the judge determines the sentence after a guilty verdict, with sentencing potentially involving imprisonment, fines, restitution, or other legally authorized consequences.

If the defendant is found not guilty in federal court, the government generally cannot appeal the acquittal or retry the defendant for the same offense because of constitutional protections against double jeopardy.

The rules governing appeals, sentencing, and post-trial motions are separate from the jury's determination of guilt.

## Understanding the Trial as a Structured Process

A criminal jury trial is not a single event but a sequence of procedural stages. Opening statements introduce the anticipated evidence; witnesses and exhibits provide the evidentiary record; cross-examination tests testimony; closing arguments interpret the evidence; jury instructions establish the applicable law; and deliberations allow jurors to reach a verdict.

Each stage serves a different purpose. The prosecution carries the burden of proving the charged offenses, while the defense has the opportunity to challenge the government's evidence and present a defense consistent with the applicable rules.

Although the general structure is recognizable, criminal procedure varies among federal districts and state jurisdictions. Local rules, statutes, constitutional requirements, and the particular circumstances of a case can alter the precise order or details of proceedings. Anyone involved in an actual criminal prosecution should therefore consult the rules applicable to the relevant court and obtain qualified legal advice concerning the specific case.
