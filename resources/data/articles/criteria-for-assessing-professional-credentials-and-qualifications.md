Professional credentials can provide useful information when selecting a lawyer, consultant, contractor, accountant, healthcare provider, or other specialist. However, credentials are not all equivalent. A government-issued license, an academic degree, a professional certification, an industry membership, and a marketing award each communicate something different about a person's qualifications.

The most useful assessment therefore begins by asking a simple question: **What does the credential actually establish?**

For regulated professions, licensing is often the first issue to verify. For example, lawyers in the United States are licensed by state authorities rather than through a single national licensing system. The American Bar Association maintains state-by-state information for checking whether an attorney is licensed to practice.

Beyond licensing, a meaningful evaluation can consider education, specialized training, professional experience, disciplinary history, current standing, certifications, and the relevance of the professional's background to the particular service required.

## What Are Professional Credentials?

Professional credentials are documented qualifications or affiliations that indicate a person's education, training, authorization, or professional standing.

They can include:

- Government-issued licenses;
- Academic degrees;
- Professional certifications;
- Board certifications;
- Specialized credentials;
- Continuing education;
- Professional memberships;
- Industry registrations;
- Fellowships; and
- Professional awards.

These categories should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example, a license may establish that a person is legally authorized to perform a regulated profession. A certification may demonstrate that the person has satisfied requirements established by a professional organization. An academic degree establishes educational achievement but may not authorize someone to practice a regulated profession.

## Licensing Is Often the First Criterion

For regulated occupations, licensing should generally be verified before other credentials are considered.

Licensing requirements are established by the relevant jurisdiction and can include education, examinations, background reviews, supervised experience, continuing education, or other requirements.

The FTC advises consumers considering licensed professions to check with the appropriate state licensing organization to determine what training and credentials are required.

A license can answer an important question:

**Is this person legally authorized to provide the regulated service?**

It does not necessarily answer another question:

**Is this person particularly experienced or well suited to my specific needs?**

Those are separate assessments.

## Verify the Current Status of the License

A professional may have obtained a license in the past but no longer hold an active license.

Possible statuses can include:

- Active;
- Inactive;
- Suspended;
- Expired;
- Restricted;
- Probationary; or
- Revoked.

The terminology varies by profession and jurisdiction.

For attorneys, the ABA recommends checking with the relevant state licensing authority to determine whether a lawyer is admitted to practice and in good standing and whether disciplinary action has occurred.

The verification should ideally come from the regulator or licensing authority rather than solely from the professional's website.

## Education and Academic Qualifications

Educational background can provide useful information about a professional's training.

Depending on the profession, relevant qualifications might include:

- Bachelor's degree;
- Master's degree;
- Doctoral degree;
- Professional degree;
- Accredited technical training;
- Apprenticeship;
- Specialized coursework; or
- Profession-specific education.

However, the importance of a particular degree depends on the service.

For example, a legal matter may require a licensed attorney, while an engineering project may require a professional engineer with the appropriate state authorization.

The question is not simply whether someone has an impressive educational background. It is whether the education is **relevant to the service being provided**.

## Accreditation and Recognition

Accreditation can provide additional information about an educational institution or professional program.

The FTC advises consumers evaluating vocational or certificate programs to verify whether the school is accredited and whether the relevant state licensing agency recognizes the program when licensing is required.

The same principle applies when evaluating professional credentials.

Ask:

- Who issued the credential?
- What organization oversees it?
- What standards must applicants satisfy?
- Is the organization independent?
- Is the credential recognized by the relevant regulator or industry?
- Must the credential be renewed?
- Are continuing-education requirements involved?

A credential from an organization with transparent standards generally provides more useful information than a title with unclear requirements.

## Certifications and Specializations

Certification can demonstrate additional training or experience beyond basic professional licensing.

For lawyers, some states have formal specialization programs. The ABA reports that 18 states currently have specialization programs that certify lawyers in specified areas of law.

A specialization credential can therefore be relevant when a matter requires knowledge of a particular field.

However, not every professional who describes themselves as a "specialist" necessarily holds a formal specialization credential.

When evaluating a claimed specialization, determine:

- Whether the specialization is formally recognized;
- Who administers the program;
- What eligibility requirements apply;
- Whether an examination is required;
- Whether experience requirements exist; and
- Whether the credential remains current.

## Relevant Professional Experience

Experience should be evaluated in relation to the actual service needed.

A professional may have decades of experience but little experience with a particular type of matter.

The ABA advises people choosing lawyers to consider the lawyer's area of expertise and prior experience and to ask what types of cases the lawyer generally handles.

Useful questions include:

- How long have you provided this type of service?
- How frequently do you handle similar matters?
- What types of clients do you normally serve?
- Do you work in the relevant jurisdiction?
- Have you handled matters involving similar technical or legal issues?

The objective is to measure **relevance**, not simply years of experience.

## Professional Standing and Disciplinary History

For regulated professions, professional standing can be an important part of credential assessment.

A licensing authority may maintain information concerning:

- Current license status;
- Disciplinary actions;
- Suspensions;
- Public reprimands;
- Restrictions; and
- Other regulatory matters.

The ABA explains that state agencies, rather than the ABA itself, are responsible for lawyer discipline and maintain relevant records.

A disciplinary record should also be interpreted carefully. The existence, age, nature, and resolution of a disciplinary matter can differ significantly.

The appropriate question is not simply whether a record exists, but **what the record actually says and whether it is relevant to the service being considered**.

## Professional Memberships

Membership in a professional organization can provide useful background information, but membership alone does not necessarily demonstrate expertise.

Professional organizations may provide:

- Continuing education;
- Networking;
- Industry standards;
- Publications;
- Conferences;
- Ethical codes; or
- Certification programs.

However, some memberships may be open to a broad range of professionals.

When evaluating a membership, determine whether it is:

- Selective or open;
- Credential-based;
- Professionally regulated;
- Educational;
- Merely commercial; or
- Primarily a networking organization.

The name of an organization alone does not establish the significance of membership.

## Continuing Education

Many professions change over time.

New laws, technologies, standards, regulations, and professional practices can make continuing education relevant even for highly experienced professionals.

Depending on the profession, continuing education may be:

- Legally required;
- Required to maintain a certification;
- Required for professional membership; or
- Voluntary.

For regulated professions, checking whether the professional has satisfied applicable continuing-education requirements can help establish whether a license or credential is current.

## Professional Awards and Rankings

Awards can be difficult to evaluate because their selection criteria vary considerably.

Some awards are based on:

- Peer review;
- Independent judging;
- Documented professional accomplishments;
- Objective eligibility standards; or
- Professional association requirements.

Others may primarily function as marketing products.

The FTC specifically warns consumers to look beyond seals and badges when choosing lawyers because some awards can be "vanity" or marketing awards that lawyers purchase. It recommends examining how long the award has existed, what requirements apply, how many recipients are selected, and who provides the award.

An award can therefore be one piece of information, but it should not substitute for licensing and experience verification.

## Published Work and Professional Contributions

For certain specialties, published work can provide insight into a professional's knowledge.

Relevant contributions may include:

- Peer-reviewed research;
- Professional articles;
- Technical publications;
- Books;
- Legal scholarship;
- Industry presentations;
- Standards-development work; or
- Educational materials.

Publication does not automatically establish practical competence, but it can demonstrate engagement with a particular subject.

The relevance of the publication matters. A professional with extensive publications in one field may have limited experience in another.

## Publicly Verifiable Work History

A professional's work history can sometimes be independently verified.

Potential sources include:

- Licensing databases;
- Professional association records;
- Court records;
- Government registries;
- Company records;
- Published professional work;
- Regulatory filings; and
- Official certification databases.

The FTC recommends looking for actual accomplishments and past work experience when evaluating lawyers and notes that some court documents are publicly available.

Public records should be interpreted carefully because the absence of a particular record does not necessarily mean that a professional lacks relevant experience.

## Insurance and Financial Protections

For some professional services, insurance coverage may be relevant.

Depending on the profession, a provider may carry:

- Professional liability insurance;
- Errors and omissions insurance;
- Malpractice insurance;
- General liability insurance; or
- Other specialized coverage.

Whether insurance is required varies by profession and jurisdiction.

The ABA notes that malpractice insurance is not mandatory for lawyers in most states, although consumers may ask whether a particular attorney carries coverage.

For contractors and other service providers, licensing authorities may impose different insurance or bonding requirements.

## Verify Claims Independently

A professional's website is useful for learning what the professional claims to offer, but important credentials should be independently verified where possible.

For example:

**Professional's website:** "This attorney is licensed in three states."

**Independent verification:** Check each relevant state licensing authority.

Or:

**Professional's website:** "Certified specialist."

**Independent verification:** Check the organization responsible for the certification and its current credential database.

Independent verification reduces the risk of relying on outdated, incomplete, or promotional information.

## Compare Credentials With the Actual Need

A credential should be evaluated according to its relevance.

Consider a hypothetical business that needs assistance with an employment dispute.

A professional might have:

- 20 years of general legal experience;
- A business-law certification;
- Extensive contract experience; and
- Little experience with employment litigation.

Another professional might have:

- 10 years of practice;
- Employment-law specialization;
- Experience handling workplace disputes; and
- Relevant litigation experience.

The number of years alone does not determine which professional has the more relevant qualifications.

The assessment should focus on the relationship between **the credential, the experience, and the specific problem**.

## Distinguish Credentials From Outcomes

Professional credentials do not guarantee a particular result.

A license demonstrates authorization to practice. A certification may demonstrate that specified requirements have been met. Experience indicates prior exposure to particular types of work.

None of these necessarily guarantees:

- A successful lawsuit;
- A favorable settlement;
- A successful business transaction;
- A particular investment result;
- A specific medical outcome; or
- Completion of a project without complications.

This distinction is particularly important when professionals use credentials as part of promotional materials.

## Red Flags in Credential Claims

Additional verification may be appropriate when a professional:

- Cannot identify the licensing authority;
- Uses unexplained certifications;
- Claims credentials that cannot be independently verified;
- Lists awards without identifying selection criteria;
- Uses unusually broad claims of expertise;
- Provides inconsistent educational information;
- Claims to be licensed in a jurisdiction where no record can be found;
- Pressures a prospective client to pay before basic information is confirmed; or
- Guarantees a particular professional outcome.

These signs do not automatically establish that a professional is unqualified. They indicate that further verification may be appropriate.

## A Practical Credential-Assessment Checklist

Before engaging a professional, consider documenting the following:

| Criterion | What to Verify |
| --- | --- |
| License | Is the license required and currently active? |
| Jurisdiction | Is the professional authorized where the service is needed? |
| Education | Is the relevant educational background verifiable? |
| Accreditation | Is the institution or program appropriately accredited? |
| Certification | Who issued the credential and what requirements apply? |
| Specialization | Is the claimed specialty formally recognized? |
| Experience | Does the professional handle similar matters regularly? |
| Standing | Is relevant disciplinary information available? |
| Continuing education | Are required requirements current? |
| Insurance | Is professional liability coverage relevant and available? |
| Professional membership | What does membership actually signify? |
| Awards | Are the selection criteria transparent? |
| Work history | Can significant claims be independently verified? |

The importance of each category will depend on the profession.

## Questions to Ask During an Initial Consultation

A prospective client can also ask direct questions.

### Credentials

- What licenses do you currently hold?
- Where are you authorized to practice?
- Are any certifications or specializations relevant to this matter?

### Experience

- How often do you handle matters like this?
- What types of clients do you normally work with?
- What training is most relevant to this service?

### Professional standing

- Is your license currently active?
- Are there any restrictions on your ability to provide the service?

### Engagement

- Who will actually perform the work?
- What services are included?
- What will the total fee structure look like?
- Will the agreement be provided in writing?

These questions help move the assessment beyond promotional claims and toward verifiable information.

## A Five-Step Verification Process

A practical process can be summarized in five stages.

### 1. Identify the required qualification

Determine whether the service requires a license, certification, registration, degree, or other credential.

### 2. Find the regulating authority

Identify the government agency or professional organization responsible for the credential.

### 3. Verify current status

Check whether the credential is active and whether relevant restrictions or disciplinary information are publicly available.

### 4. Evaluate relevance

Compare the professional's education, experience, specialization, and training with the actual service needed.

### 5. Confirm the engagement terms

Before hiring, document the scope of services, fees, responsibilities, and communication arrangements.

This process is generally more informative than relying on a single credential, ranking, or online profile.

## Final Takeaway

Professional credentials can provide valuable information, but different credentials establish different things. A license may demonstrate legal authorization to practice, an academic degree may demonstrate education, a certification may establish that specified professional requirements have been met, and a professional award may simply reflect a particular organization's selection process.

For lawyers, the ABA recommends checking whether an attorney is admitted to practice and in good standing with the relevant state licensing authority, while also considering experience and area of practice.

Consumers should also be cautious about treating awards, seals, badges, or online reviews as substitutes for credential verification. The FTC specifically recommends examining the basis for professional awards and independently checking licensing and experience information.

A thorough assessment therefore asks four fundamental questions:

**Is the professional authorized? Is the credential genuine? Is the qualification relevant? And does the professional have appropriate experience for the specific service required?**

Answering those questions provides a more meaningful basis for evaluating professional qualifications than relying on titles or promotional claims alone.
