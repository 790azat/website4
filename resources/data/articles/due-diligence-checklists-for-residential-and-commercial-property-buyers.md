Buying real estate involves more than agreeing on a purchase price and signing a deed. Before completing a transaction, buyers may need to investigate ownership, title, physical condition, boundaries, zoning, financing, taxes, leases, environmental conditions, and other factors that could affect the property's use or value.

The appropriate due diligence depends heavily on the type of property. A residential buyer may focus on the home's physical condition, title, insurance, financing, and closing documents. A commercial buyer may need a substantially broader investigation involving leases, operating income, zoning, environmental conditions, building systems, tenant rights, and development restrictions.

The following checklists provide a general framework for U.S. property transactions. State and local requirements can differ considerably.

## Establishing the Due Diligence Period

Buyers should begin by reviewing the purchase agreement and identifying all deadlines that control the investigation.

Important dates may include:

- Inspection deadlines
- Financing and appraisal deadlines
- Title-review periods
- Survey deadlines
- Environmental-review periods
- Lease-review deadlines
- Permit and zoning-review deadlines
- Contingency expiration dates
- Closing date

A buyer should understand what happens if a problem is discovered before a contingency expires. Depending on the contract, the buyer may have rights to request repairs, renegotiate terms, obtain additional information, or terminate the transaction.

## Residential Property Due Diligence Checklist

### 1. Verify Ownership and Title

The buyer should confirm that the seller has the legal authority to transfer the property.

The title review may identify:

- Existing mortgages
- Tax liens
- Judgment liens
- Easements
- Restrictions
- Covenants
- Rights of way
- Prior claims
- Other recorded interests

Title insurance can provide protection against certain pre-existing title claims. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that owner's title insurance can protect a homeowner when another party asserts a qualifying claim against the property arising from before the purchase.

### 2. Review the Property Survey

A survey can help establish the location of property boundaries and identify potential encroachments, easements, access issues, or discrepancies between physical conditions and recorded information.

For commercial transactions and other transactions requiring detailed survey information, the 2026 ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey Standards took effect on February 23, 2026. The standards address survey-related matters such as boundaries, easements, encroachments, access, and legal descriptions.

Whether a particular residential transaction requires a survey depends on the property, lender, jurisdiction, and transaction terms.

### 3. Conduct an Independent Inspection

A residential inspection should examine the property's observable physical condition.

Potential areas include:

- Roof
- Foundation
- Structural components
- Plumbing
- Electrical systems
- Heating and cooling
- Windows and doors
- Attic and basement areas
- Drainage
- Appliances
- Visible water damage
- Safety concerns

The CFPB recommends scheduling an independent inspection as soon as possible so buyers have time to investigate significant problems and determine how those issues affect the transaction.

Specialized inspections may be appropriate for conditions such as termites, radon, mold, septic systems, wells, or structural concerns.

### 4. Confirm Zoning and Permitted Uses

Buyers should determine whether the property's existing and intended uses comply with applicable zoning and local regulations.

This can be especially important when a buyer intends to:

- Add an accessory structure
- Convert a property
- Operate a business
- Build an addition
- Change parking arrangements
- Redevelop the property

A buyer should not assume that an existing use automatically means the same use will be permitted after purchase.

### 5. Review Taxes, Assessments, and Insurance

Buyers should examine property-tax information, special assessments, homeowner association obligations where applicable, and expected insurance costs.

The buyer should also determine whether taxes or assessments are prorated at closing and whether outstanding amounts must be resolved before title transfers.

### 6. Review Financing and Closing Documents

For financed residential purchases, buyers should compare their expected loan terms with the final closing documents.

The CFPB states that borrowers must receive a Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing for covered mortgage transactions.

Before signing, buyers should review the purchase price, loan amount, interest rate, closing costs, credits, taxes, insurance, and other material figures.

The CFPB also recommends a final walkthrough before closing to confirm that agreed repairs have been completed and that items included in the transaction remain at the property.

## Commercial Property Due Diligence Checklist

Commercial real estate requires many of the same basic investigations but usually involves a much broader financial and operational review.

### 1. Examine the Title and Survey

Commercial buyers should review the title commitment or equivalent title information and investigate recorded interests affecting the property.

The survey may need to address:

- Property boundaries
- Easements
- Rights of way
- Access
- Encroachments
- Improvements
- Parking
- Utility locations
- Building setbacks
- Legal descriptions

The 2026 ALTA/NSPS standards provide minimum requirements for qualifying ALTA/NSPS land title surveys and are specifically designed to coordinate the needs of clients, lenders, surveyors, and title insurers.

### 2. Investigate Zoning and Land Use

Commercial buyers should verify whether the property can legally be used for the buyer's intended purpose.

Review may include:

- Zoning classification
- Permitted uses
- Conditional-use requirements
- Building restrictions
- Parking requirements
- Sign regulations
- Density limits
- Development rights
- Setback requirements
- Certificates of occupancy
- Pending zoning changes

This review is particularly important when the transaction depends on redevelopment, expansion, conversion, or a change in use.

### 3. Conduct Environmental Due Diligence

Environmental investigation can be particularly important for commercial property because historical uses may create potential contamination or regulatory issues.

A buyer may need to investigate previous property uses, environmental databases, records, site conditions, and potential contamination.

The federal Environmental Protection Agency's **All Appropriate Inquiries** framework provides standards for conducting environmental inquiries into the ownership and history of certain properties before acquisition. The process can be relevant to parties seeking protections under federal environmental law.

The appropriate environmental investigation depends on the property and transaction. Industrial facilities, gas stations, dry cleaners, manufacturing sites, and properties with other potentially hazardous historical uses may warrant specialized investigation.

### 4. Review Building Systems and Physical Conditions

Commercial physical due diligence can extend well beyond a standard home inspection.

Depending on the property, professionals may examine:

- Structural systems
- Roof systems
- HVAC
- Electrical infrastructure
- Plumbing
- Fire protection
- Elevators
- Parking structures
- Building envelope
- Accessibility
- Deferred maintenance
- Capital expenditure requirements

A property-condition assessment can help buyers understand immediate repairs as well as longer-term capital needs.

### 5. Analyze Existing Leases

For income-producing property, leases can be among the most important documents in the transaction.

Buyers should review:

- Lease terms
- Rent schedules
- Renewal options
- Security deposits
- Tenant improvement obligations
- Operating-expense provisions
- Maintenance responsibilities
- Assignment rights
- Termination provisions
- Defaults
- Guarantees
- Pending tenant disputes

The buyer should compare lease documents with actual rent rolls and payment records to identify inconsistencies.

### 6. Review Financial Performance

Commercial property due diligence should normally include an examination of historical and projected financial information.

Documents may include:

- Rent rolls
- Operating statements
- Property-tax records
- Utility expenses
- Insurance costs
- Maintenance expenses
- Capital expenditure records
- Service contracts
- Vendor agreements
- Receivables and delinquencies

The objective is to determine whether the property's financial performance is consistent with the assumptions underlying the purchase price.

## Comparing Residential and Commercial Due Diligence

| Due Diligence Area | Residential Property | Commercial Property |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title review | Important | Important |
| Survey | May be required or advisable | Frequently significant |
| Physical inspection | Usually important | Often involves multiple specialists |
| Zoning | Important for intended uses | Often central to transaction |
| Environmental review | Property-dependent | Frequently more extensive |
| Lease analysis | Usually limited or irrelevant | Critical for income-producing property |
| Financial analysis | Primarily buyer's affordability | Often focused on property income and expenses |
| Building systems | Generally focused on residential systems | Can involve complex commercial infrastructure |
| Tax review | Property taxes and assessments | Property taxes, assessments, and transaction implications |
| Closing review | Loan and purchase documents | Purchase agreement, financing, title, leases, and transaction documents |

## Documenting Findings and Negotiating Issues

Due diligence is most useful when findings are documented systematically.

A buyer can maintain a checklist showing:

1. The issue identified.
2. The document or inspection supporting the finding.
3. The professional responsible for reviewing it.
4. The potential financial or legal consequence.
5. Any requested seller response.
6. The applicable contractual deadline.
7. Whether the issue has been resolved.

This approach can prevent important issues from being overlooked as the closing date approaches.

## When Professional Guidance May Be Appropriate

Professional assistance can be particularly useful when due diligence reveals title defects, boundary disputes, environmental concerns, zoning restrictions, significant structural problems, complicated leases, development restrictions, or unusual contractual obligations.

Depending on the transaction, the professional team may include:

- Real estate attorneys
- Title professionals
- Licensed surveyors
- Inspectors
- Engineers
- Environmental consultants
- Appraisers
- Accountants
- Insurance professionals
- Property managers

The professionals required will depend on the property's characteristics and the buyer's intended use.

## Completing the Final Review

Before closing, buyers should confirm that material due diligence issues have been resolved or appropriately addressed in the transaction documents.

A final review can include:

- Updated title information
- Survey
- Inspection reports
- Required repairs
- Financing documents
- Insurance
- Taxes and assessments
- Required permits
- Lease information
- Environmental reports
- Seller representations
- Closing statement or disclosure documents
- Final walkthrough

Residential buyers should also carefully review the final closing documents before signing. The CFPB advises buyers to take time to review the documents and raise questions when information differs from what they previously expected.

Commercial buyers may have substantially larger transaction files and should coordinate the final review among their legal, financial, title, and technical professionals.

## Conclusion

Real estate due diligence is designed to identify issues before a buyer becomes legally and financially committed to a property. Residential transactions commonly emphasize title, physical condition, financing, insurance, and closing documentation. Commercial transactions can require a much broader investigation of zoning, environmental conditions, building systems, leases, operating income, and development rights.

A checklist cannot replace property-specific professional review, but it can provide a structured framework for identifying documents, deadlines, risks, and unresolved questions before closing. Because real estate laws and requirements vary by state and locality, buyers should evaluate the applicable rules and transaction documents for the particular property involved.
