Homeowners associations (HOAs) manage shared residential communities through a combination of governing documents, board decisions, community rules, contracts, budgets, and applicable law. Because an HOA can exercise authority over assessments, common areas, architectural standards, maintenance, and enforcement, effective governance requires clearly defined responsibilities and reliable procedures.

HOAs are commonly organized around governing documents that establish how the association operates and how homeowners participate in community management. California's Department of Justice, for example, explains that HOAs generally operate through an elected board and that their governing documents can include declarations, bylaws, and rules and regulations. The precise structure and legal requirements vary substantially among jurisdictions.

## Understanding the HOA Governance Structure

An HOA's governance structure generally involves homeowners or members, a board of directors, officers, committees, and, in some communities, a professional management company.

The board typically acts on behalf of the association within the authority granted by applicable law and the association's governing documents. Depending on the jurisdiction, the board may oversee common-property maintenance, budgets, assessments, contracts, rule enforcement, insurance, and other community functions.

A management company may handle day-to-day administrative matters, but its authority ordinarily comes from a management agreement and the governing framework established by the association.

The division of responsibilities should be documented clearly so homeowners understand which decisions belong to the board, which require membership approval, and which can be delegated to officers, committees, or managers.

## The Role of Bylaws

Bylaws are generally the internal rules governing how an organization operates. The IRS describes bylaws as an organization's internal operating rules and notes that state law may impose requirements concerning bylaws for nonprofit corporations.

For an HOA, bylaws may address matters such as:

- Number and qualifications of directors
- Board elections
- Terms of office
- Officer positions
- Board and membership meetings
- Notice requirements
- Quorum
- Voting procedures
- Committee authority
- Member rights
- Amendment procedures
- Removal or replacement of directors
- Delegation of authority

Bylaws should be read together with the association's other governing documents rather than treated as the only source of authority.

## Understanding the Hierarchy of Governing Documents

An HOA may have several layers of governing documents.

These can include:

1. Articles of incorporation or other formation documents.
2. Declaration of covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs).
3. Bylaws.
4. Board-adopted rules and regulations.
5. Policies and procedures.
6. Individual contracts and resolutions.

The legal relationship between these documents depends on applicable law and the language of the documents themselves.

For example, California's Attorney General explains that CC&Rs establish rules governing the operation of an HOA and can establish homeowner obligations and restrictions, while bylaws generally establish how the association itself is run.

Because conflicts can arise between different documents, boards should review proposed rules and amendments against the complete governing framework before adoption.

## Board Elections and Director Responsibilities

Board elections are a central component of HOA governance. Governing documents and applicable statutes can determine who may serve, how candidates are nominated, how voting occurs, and how vacancies are filled.

Director responsibilities can include:

- Reviewing financial information
- Approving contracts
- Supervising management
- Maintaining common areas
- Establishing budgets
- Collecting assessments
- Addressing rule violations
- Overseeing insurance
- Responding to significant legal issues

The precise authority of a board depends on the association's governing documents and applicable law.

Directors should also understand the limits of their authority. A board cannot necessarily create a rule or take an action merely because it believes the decision would benefit the community. The decision must have an appropriate legal or governing-document basis.

## Conducting Effective Board Meetings

Meeting procedures can directly affect the transparency and legitimacy of HOA decisions.

A well-organized meeting process can include:

- Providing required notice
- Preparing an agenda
- Identifying matters requiring board action
- Allowing member participation where required
- Recording decisions accurately
- Documenting votes
- Maintaining approved minutes
- Preserving supporting materials

The importance of documentation is reflected in broader nonprofit governance guidance from the IRS. For organizations subject to its reporting framework, the IRS asks whether governing-body and authorized-committee meetings and written actions were contemporaneously documented in a manner permitted by state law.

HOA-specific meeting requirements, however, are determined by applicable state or local law and the association's governing documents.

## Managing HOA Finances

Financial governance is one of the board's most important responsibilities.

HOA financial management can involve:

- Annual budgets
- Regular assessments
- Reserve funds
- Maintenance expenses
- Insurance
- Vendor payments
- Special assessments
- Delinquent accounts
- Financial reporting
- Audits or reviews

Boards should establish procedures for approving expenditures and maintaining adequate documentation.

Financial controls can also separate responsibilities. For example, the person approving an expense may be different from the person processing payment or reconciling the association's accounts.

The exact accounting and disclosure requirements depend on jurisdiction, association structure, and governing documents.

## Managing Conflicts of Interest

HOA directors and officers can encounter situations in which personal or business interests overlap with association responsibilities.

Potential conflicts can arise when:

- A director owns a company seeking an HOA contract.
- A board member has a personal relationship with a vendor.
- A director has a financial interest in a proposed transaction.
- A board decision directly affects a director's property differently from other properties.

A conflict-of-interest policy can establish procedures for disclosure, evaluation, and appropriate abstention from decision-making.

The IRS identifies conflict-of-interest policies among governance practices that can contribute to sound organizational administration, although the specific federal tax rules applicable to a particular HOA depend on its organizational and tax status.

## Rulemaking and Enforcement

HOA boards may have authority to adopt rules addressing matters such as parking, architectural changes, use of common areas, pets, landscaping, noise, or other community concerns.

Rules should be consistent with the association's declaration, bylaws, applicable statutes, and other controlling requirements.

Enforcement procedures should also be consistent and documented. Depending on applicable law, homeowners may be entitled to notice of an alleged violation, an opportunity to respond, a hearing, or other procedural protections.

California's Attorney General notes that HOA governing documents can establish both homeowner requirements and remedies for violations, while state law may impose additional requirements governing enforcement.

Consistency is particularly important. Selective or irregular enforcement can create disputes concerning whether the board followed its governing authority and established procedures.

## Amending HOA Bylaws

Bylaws should be reviewed periodically because community structures, technology, state law, and operational requirements can change.

An amendment process may involve:

1. Identifying the provision requiring modification.
2. Reviewing the proposed language.
3. Checking applicable law.
4. Determining whether board or homeowner approval is required.
5. Providing required notice.
6. Conducting the required vote.
7. Recording or filing the amendment if required.
8. Updating the association's official records.
9. Distributing the revised governing documents.

The amendment procedure itself is usually established in the bylaws, declaration, or applicable statute.

Because changing quorum, voting rights, board authority, membership rights, or amendment procedures can have significant consequences, proposed amendments should be reviewed carefully before adoption.

## Keeping Governance Documents Current

An HOA should maintain a centralized and authoritative set of governing documents.

The records may include:

- Current bylaws
- Articles of incorporation
- Declaration and amendments
- Board resolutions
- Community rules
- Policies
- Meeting minutes
- Election records
- Contracts
- Financial records
- Insurance documents
- Notices and disclosures

The IRS recognizes that changes to an organization's governing documents can include changes to the composition, authority, or duties of the governing body, member voting rights, quorum, approval requirements, and amendment provisions.

Although those federal reporting rules do not govern every HOA, the principle illustrates why changes to fundamental governance provisions should be carefully documented.

## Working With Professional Management Companies

Larger HOAs may hire professional management companies to assist with accounting, maintenance coordination, communications, vendor management, collections, and administrative work.

The management agreement should define the manager's authority and responsibilities. Important provisions can address:

- Services included
- Fees
- Spending authority
- Emergency authority
- Record access
- Insurance
- Vendor relationships
- Reporting obligations
- Contract duration
- Termination rights
- Handling of association funds

Delegating administrative responsibilities does not necessarily transfer the board's ultimate governance responsibilities. Directors should maintain appropriate oversight of delegated functions.

## Documenting Decisions and Maintaining Records

Strong recordkeeping can protect both the association and homeowners by creating a reliable history of governance decisions.

Important records can show:

- What decision was made
- When it was made
- Who participated
- What authority supported the decision
- How directors voted
- What documents were reviewed
- What follow-up actions were assigned

Minutes should generally focus on accurately documenting official business rather than becoming a transcript of every conversation.

Electronic records can also require careful management. Associations should establish retention practices that address meeting records, financial information, contracts, correspondence, homeowner records, and other documents while considering applicable privacy and legal requirements.

## Handling Disputes Within the Community

HOA disputes can involve assessments, architectural decisions, rule enforcement, elections, maintenance responsibilities, records access, board conduct, or interpretation of governing documents.

A structured dispute-resolution process can help identify the issue before litigation becomes necessary.

Depending on applicable law, possible mechanisms include:

- Internal complaints
- Board review
- Hearings
- Mediation
- Alternative dispute resolution
- Arbitration
- Judicial proceedings

The available procedures vary by jurisdiction. California, for example, has statutory provisions concerning dispute resolution within common-interest developments, illustrating how state law can establish procedures beyond those contained in an association's bylaws.

## Reviewing Governance Practices Periodically

Governance should be treated as an ongoing process rather than a one-time drafting exercise.

An annual governance review can examine:

- Whether bylaws remain current
- Whether board elections followed required procedures
- Whether meeting procedures are compliant
- Whether financial controls are functioning
- Whether policies conflict with higher-level documents
- Whether contracts remain appropriate
- Whether records are complete
- Whether insurance coverage should be reviewed
- Whether recent legislation requires policy changes

Periodic review can help identify inconsistencies before they develop into disputes.

## Conclusion

Effective HOA governance depends on a clear relationship between the association's governing documents, board authority, homeowner rights, financial procedures, and applicable law.

Bylaws establish important internal operating rules, while declarations, statutes, board-adopted rules, resolutions, and contracts can address other aspects of community management.

Boards can strengthen governance by documenting decisions, maintaining accurate records, managing conflicts of interest, following election and meeting procedures, maintaining financial controls, and reviewing governing documents periodically. Because HOA law is highly jurisdiction-specific, associations should evaluate their bylaws and governance practices under the laws applicable to the particular community.
