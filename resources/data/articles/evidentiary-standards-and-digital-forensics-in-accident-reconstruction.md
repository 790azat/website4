Accident reconstruction increasingly combines traditional physical evidence with information generated by vehicles, mobile devices, cameras, navigation systems, and other digital technologies. These sources can help investigators determine what happened before, during, and after a collision, but their usefulness depends on how the information was collected, preserved, interpreted, and presented.

Accident reconstruction is therefore not simply a matter of finding a digital record and treating it as definitive. A reconstruction may involve measurements from the accident scene, vehicle damage, witness accounts, photographs, event data recorder information, video, medical records, and expert analysis. NHTSA's crash investigations, for example, combine scene inspections, vehicle inspections, interviews, photographs, EDR information when available, and official records.

When a reconstruction becomes part of litigation, additional questions can arise concerning admissibility, authenticity, reliability, relevance, expert methodology, and preservation.

## What Is Accident Reconstruction?

Accident reconstruction is the process of analyzing available evidence to determine how a collision occurred.

A reconstructionist may attempt to establish:

- Vehicle movements before impact;
- Approximate speeds;
- Directions of travel;
- Point or area of impact;
- Vehicle positions;
- Braking or steering activity;
- Collision sequence;
- Vehicle deformation;
- Occupant movement;
- Roadway conditions; and
- Factors that may have contributed to the crash.

NHTSA's Special Crash Investigations program illustrates the multidisciplinary nature of this work. Its investigators collect information about skid and yaw marks, vehicle positions, crash damage, occupant kinematics, safety systems, EDR information, and witness or victim interviews.

A reconstruction is therefore generally based on multiple sources rather than a single piece of evidence.

## What Counts as Evidence?

Evidence in an accident case can be broadly divided into physical, documentary, testimonial, and digital information.

### Physical Evidence

Physical evidence can include:

- Vehicle damage;
- Tire marks;
- Debris;
- Roadway measurements;
- Vehicle components;
- Road signs and signals;
- Skid or yaw marks; and
- Damage to roadside structures.

These observations can help establish the physical mechanics of a collision.

### Documentary Evidence

Documents can include:

- Police reports;
- Medical records;
- Repair estimates;
- Inspection records;
- Maintenance records;
- Traffic-camera records;
- Photographs; and
- Insurance documentation.

### Testimonial Evidence

Witnesses, drivers, passengers, first responders, investigators, and experts may provide testimony concerning what they observed or concluded.

### Digital Evidence

Digital evidence can include:

- Event Data Recorder information;
- Dashcam recordings;
- Security-camera footage;
- Smartphone data;
- GPS or navigation information;
- Vehicle telematics;
- Electronic braking or stability-system information;
- Fleet-management records; and
- Digital communications.

The reliability of each source must be considered separately.

## Evidentiary Standards in Civil Accident Cases

In a typical U.S. civil case, the evidentiary standard generally differs from the standard used in a criminal prosecution.

Civil cases ordinarily use the **preponderance of the evidence** standard, meaning the fact finder must determine that a proposition is more likely true than not true. Some particular issues or proceedings can involve different standards, however.

Evidence must also satisfy applicable rules governing relevance, authenticity, hearsay, expert testimony, and other evidentiary requirements.

The fact that information is technically available does not automatically mean it will be admissible or given a particular weight by a court.

For example, a video may be relevant but still raise questions about whether it has been authenticated, whether it has been altered, or whether its timestamp is reliable.

## Relevance and Reliability

Two fundamental questions in digital accident evidence are **what the evidence shows** and **how reliably it shows it**.

A GPS record may indicate a device's location at a particular time, but it may not establish who was operating the device.

Similarly, an EDR may record certain vehicle parameters, but the recorded information may cover only a short period surrounding a crash. NHTSA describes EDRs as devices that can record technical vehicle and occupant information for seconds before, during, and after a crash.

The meaning of the data therefore depends on the capabilities and limitations of the particular system.

## Event Data Recorders

Event Data Recorders are an important source of accident-reconstruction information.

Depending on the vehicle and system, EDR information can include data relating to:

- Pre-crash vehicle dynamics;
- System status;
- Driver inputs;
- Crash characteristics;
- Restraint-system use;
- Airbag deployment; and
- Certain post-crash information.

NHTSA explains that EDRs can be valuable investigative tools but also emphasizes that they have limitations and should be used with other sources of information.

An EDR therefore should not automatically be treated as a complete recording of the accident.

It may not contain every relevant event, and its available data elements can depend on the vehicle and circumstances of the crash.

## Mobile Phone and Smartphone Evidence

Smartphones can contain information relevant to accident reconstruction, including:

- Call records;
- Messages;
- Application activity;
- Photographs and videos;
- Location information;
- Navigation records;
- Device timestamps; and
- Other system-generated information.

NIST describes mobile-device forensics as a process involving preservation, acquisition, examination, analysis, and reporting of digital information.

The way smartphone evidence is acquired matters. Directly manipulating a device can potentially change data or affect volatile information. NIST's digital-evidence guidance emphasizes preserving evidence without altering its contents and documenting the handling process.

In litigation, parties may therefore need to address not only what a device contains but also how the relevant information was obtained.

## Video and Dashcam Evidence

Video can provide valuable visual information about a collision.

Potential sources include:

- Dashcams;
- Traffic cameras;
- Business surveillance systems;
- Residential security cameras;
- Vehicle cameras;
- Body-worn cameras; and
- Smartphone recordings.

Video may help establish vehicle positions, traffic signals, lane movements, timing, and driver behavior.

However, video also has limitations. Camera angle, frame rate, field of view, compression, lighting, obstructions, and missing portions of a recording can affect what can reliably be concluded.

A short video clip may therefore show an important portion of an accident without establishing everything that occurred beforehand.

## Preserving Digital Evidence

Digital evidence can be particularly vulnerable to alteration, deletion, overwriting, or loss.

NIST identifies preservation as a fundamental part of digital evidence handling and emphasizes maintaining evidence without changing the underlying data. Its guidance addresses the preservation, acquisition, examination, analysis, and reporting stages of digital forensics.

Preservation can involve:

1. Identifying potentially relevant devices and systems;
2. Documenting their condition;
3. Preventing unnecessary alteration;
4. Preserving original files where possible;
5. Maintaining appropriate custody records;
6. Creating forensic copies when appropriate; and
7. Recording the methods used to acquire the evidence.

These procedures help establish a defensible history of the evidence.

## Chain of Custody

Chain of custody is the documented history of who possessed evidence, when it was transferred, and how it was handled.

For digital evidence, documentation can include:

- Device identification;
- Date and time of collection;
- Person responsible for collection;
- Storage location;
- Transfer records;
- Acquisition methods;
- Software or hardware used;
- File hashes where appropriate; and
- Documentation of subsequent examinations.

A strong chain of custody can help address challenges concerning whether evidence presented in court is the same evidence originally collected.

NIST's preservation guidance specifically addresses the special challenges associated with maintaining digital evidence and the need for appropriate evidence-management practices.

## Forensic Imaging and Data Acquisition

Digital investigators may create forensic copies or images of relevant storage media rather than repeatedly examining the original source.

The precise technique depends on the device and evidence source.

For mobile devices, NIST's guidance distinguishes the preservation and acquisition stages and discusses the need for methods appropriate to the technology involved.

The objective is generally to obtain relevant information while minimizing changes to the original evidence and maintaining documentation that allows the process to be evaluated.

## Expert Testimony in Accident Reconstruction

Accident reconstruction frequently involves expert testimony.

An expert may analyze:

- Vehicle dynamics;
- Momentum and energy;
- Crash damage;
- Roadway measurements;
- EDR information;
- Video;
- Digital records;
- Visibility;
- Human factors; or
- Vehicle safety systems.

The expert's conclusion should be distinguished from the underlying evidence.

For example, an EDR may provide recorded vehicle information, while the reconstructionist may use that information together with physical measurements and engineering calculations to reach an opinion about the collision.

The expert's methodology and reasoning can therefore become relevant issues in litigation.

## Corroborating Digital Evidence

One of the most important principles in accident reconstruction is corroboration.

Suppose an EDR indicates a particular vehicle speed shortly before a collision. Investigators may compare that information with:

- Tire marks;
- Vehicle deformation;
- Roadway measurements;
- Video footage;
- Witness statements;
- Traffic signals;
- GPS information; and
- Other vehicle data.

If multiple independent sources are consistent, they may provide a stronger factual basis for reconstruction.

If they conflict, the discrepancy may require additional investigation.

NHTSA's own crash-investigation methodology illustrates this approach by combining physical scene information, vehicle inspections, EDR data, interviews, photographs, and official records.

## Limitations of Digital Evidence

Digital evidence is not necessarily complete or error-free.

Potential limitations include:

- Missing data;
- Short recording windows;
- Device malfunction;
- Incorrect timestamps;
- Clock synchronization problems;
- Incomplete video;
- Data overwriting;
- Proprietary formats;
- Software interpretation issues;
- Sensor limitations;
- GPS accuracy limitations; and
- Uncertainty about who controlled the device.

For these reasons, investigators should identify both what the evidence establishes and what it does not establish.

A technically precise data point can still be misleading if it is taken out of context.

## Authenticity and Alteration

Digital evidence can raise questions about whether a file or record is genuine and unchanged.

Potential authentication issues include:

- Who created the recording?
- Where did the file originate?
- When was it created?
- Has it been edited?
- Was the original file preserved?
- Can the metadata be verified?
- Was the recording transferred correctly?
- Can the system that generated it be identified?

Metadata can sometimes help establish these facts, but metadata itself should be evaluated rather than automatically assumed to be accurate.

## Accident Reconstruction and Causation

Reconstruction evidence can help answer factual questions about how a collision occurred, but determining legal causation is ultimately a matter governed by the applicable law.

For example, an expert may conclude that a vehicle was traveling at a particular speed before impact. That technical finding does not automatically establish negligence.

A court may separately need to determine whether the conduct violated an applicable legal duty and whether that conduct legally caused the claimed injury.

The same distinction applies to digital evidence concerning distracted driving, braking, lane position, or other conduct.

## Preserving Evidence After a Collision

Parties involved in serious collisions should take reasonable steps to preserve potentially relevant information.

Depending on the circumstances, that may include:

- Preserving damaged vehicles;
- Avoiding unnecessary deletion of photographs or videos;
- Retaining original recordings;
- Identifying potentially relevant cameras quickly;
- Preserving relevant smartphone data;
- Keeping repair and inspection records;
- Identifying vehicles equipped with EDR or telematics systems; and
- Maintaining copies of important documents.

Some surveillance systems automatically overwrite older footage, making prompt identification and preservation particularly important.

Evidence-preservation obligations can also arise once litigation is reasonably anticipated, although the precise requirements depend on the applicable law and circumstances.

## Digital Forensics Does Not Replace Physical Reconstruction

Modern digital evidence can be extremely useful, but accident reconstruction remains multidisciplinary.

Physical evidence can reveal information that digital systems do not record. Conversely, electronic records may provide information that cannot be established from physical evidence alone.

A comprehensive reconstruction can therefore combine:

**Scene evidence → Vehicle evidence → Digital evidence → Witness evidence → Expert analysis**

Each category can provide an independent perspective on the same event.

## Final Takeaway

Accident reconstruction increasingly depends on both physical evidence and digital information. EDRs, smartphones, dashcams, surveillance systems, GPS records, and vehicle telematics can provide valuable information about a collision, but their evidentiary value depends on authenticity, preservation, reliability, context, and appropriate interpretation. NHTSA specifically cautions that EDR information has limitations and should be considered alongside other evidence.

Digital forensics adds another layer of analysis. NIST guidance emphasizes preserving digital evidence, documenting its handling, acquiring information through appropriate methods, and analyzing and reporting the results in a manner that supports reliability and evaluation.

Ultimately, the strongest accident reconstructions generally do not depend on one data source. They compare digital records with physical evidence, witness accounts, vehicle inspections, and other available information to develop a supported explanation of what occurred.
