Consumer products are subject to a variety of federal safety requirements intended to reduce unreasonable risks of injury, illness, fire, poisoning, and other harm. Manufacturers, importers, distributors, and retailers can have different responsibilities depending on the product, the applicable safety rule, and the information available about a potential hazard.

In the United States, the **Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)** administers and enforces numerous federal consumer-product safety requirements. The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 (CPSIA) expanded the CPSC's regulatory and enforcement tools and established additional requirements involving children's products, testing, certification, tracking information, reporting, and recalls.

Product testing and recalls are related but separate parts of the safety system. Testing is intended to identify whether products comply with applicable requirements, while a recall is generally a corrective action taken when a product presents a safety hazard or violates an applicable requirement.

## The Role of the CPSC

The CPSC is a federal agency responsible for protecting the public from unreasonable risks associated with consumer products within its jurisdiction.

Its authority covers thousands of types of products, although certain categories fall primarily under other federal agencies. For example, motor vehicles, food, drugs, cosmetics, and certain other products may be regulated by different federal agencies under separate laws.

For products within CPSC jurisdiction, federal requirements can address:

- Product design
- Materials and chemical content
- Flammability
- Mechanical hazards
- Electrical hazards
- Choking risks
- Labeling
- Packaging
- Testing
- Certification
- Recordkeeping
- Product tracking
- Recalls

The applicable requirements depend on the particular product and the federal regulations governing it.

## Consumer Product Safety Laws

The **Consumer Product Safety Act (CPSA)** provides a central statutory framework for the CPSC.

The **Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008** significantly expanded that framework. Among other provisions, the CPSIA addressed lead and phthalates, toy safety, durable infant and toddler products, third-party testing, certificates of compliance, tracking labels, imports, enforcement penalties, and the SaferProducts.gov reporting system.

Not every consumer product is subject to the same testing requirements.

A manufacturer or importer must first determine which federal safety rules apply to the particular product.

## Product Safety Testing

Testing is one of the primary tools used to determine whether a product satisfies applicable safety requirements.

For many regulated products, manufacturers and importers must conduct testing and maintain evidence supporting compliance.

For **children's products**, the requirements are particularly specific. Federal law generally requires children's products subject to applicable federal safety requirements to be tested by a third-party laboratory accepted by the CPSC.

The precise testing requirements depend on factors such as:

- Product category
- Intended age group
- Materials
- Applicable safety rules
- Product design
- Components
- Manufacturing changes

A single children's product can be subject to multiple testing requirements and may require testing for more than one applicable safety rule.

## Third-Party Testing for Children's Products

The CPSIA established significant third-party testing requirements for children's products.

A CPSC-accepted laboratory must have the appropriate accreditation and scope to conduct the required testing. The CPSC explains that accreditation is tied to particular regulations, meaning that a laboratory qualified for one type of testing may not necessarily be qualified for every safety requirement applicable to a product.

After required testing, the responsible manufacturer or importer generally must issue a **Children's Product Certificate (CPC)** certifying compliance with applicable children's product safety requirements.

Testing records and certificates must also be retained for the periods required by federal regulations. CPSC guidance states that firms generally must maintain specified children's-product testing and certification records for at least five years.

## Testing Requirements for General-Use Products

Non-children's products can be subject to different requirements.

For general-use products covered by CPSC safety rules, manufacturers and importers may be required to issue a **General Certificate of Conformity (GCC)**.

The CPSC explains that a GCC must generally be based on testing of each product or a reasonable testing program. The responsible manufacturer or importer remains legally responsible for the certificate even if a laboratory or other third party assists with testing or preparation.

Unlike children's products, general-use products do not universally require testing by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory. The specific applicable regulation determines what testing and certification are required.

## Material Changes and Retesting

Product safety does not necessarily end with the first successful test.

Changes to a product can potentially affect compliance. These changes may involve:

- Materials
- Components
- Suppliers
- Manufacturing processes
- Product design
- Chemical formulations
- Dimensions
- Safety features

CPSC guidance states that general-use products should generally be retested when there is a material change, and the agency recommends periodic testing as a good practice even where it is not specifically required.

Children's products are subject to more specific periodic-testing requirements. CPSC guidance explains that ongoing children's-product production generally requires periodic retesting, with the precise schedule governed by the applicable regulations and circumstances.

## Certificates of Compliance

Certification provides a formal statement that a product complies with applicable safety requirements.

For general-use products covered by CPSC rules, the GCC identifies the applicable rules, standards, bans, or regulations and is based on appropriate testing or a reasonable testing program.

For children's products, the responsible firm generally issues a CPC based on the required third-party testing.

Certification does not mean that a product can never present a hazard. A product can pass testing and later reveal a problem because of:

- Manufacturing defects
- Changes in production
- Supplier problems
- Unexpected consumer use
- Newly discovered hazards
- Inadequate instructions
- Quality-control failures

For this reason, manufacturers and other businesses have continuing safety responsibilities.

## The Duty to Report Dangerous Products

One of the most important obligations under federal consumer-product law is the duty to report certain potentially dangerous products to the CPSC.

The CPSC states that manufacturers, importers, distributors, and retailers must immediately report certain information, including information indicating that a product could create a substantial risk of injury, presents an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death, or fails to comply with an applicable consumer-product safety requirement.

The CPSC states that companies generally must report within **24 hours** after obtaining reportable information.

The reporting obligation is important because companies should not necessarily wait until a formal investigation has conclusively established that a product is dangerous.

The CPSC specifically advises businesses to report when they have information that triggers the statutory reporting requirements.

## Retailer Reporting Responsibilities

Retailers can have reporting obligations of their own.

The CPSC explains that retailers may be required to report information indicating that a product presents a substantial risk of injury, an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death, or violates an applicable consumer-product safety requirement.

A retailer therefore cannot always assume that reporting is solely the manufacturer's responsibility.

The retailer's position in the supply chain, the information it possesses, and whether the CPSC has already received complete information can affect the reporting analysis.

## What Triggers a Product Recall?

A recall may be appropriate when a product presents a safety hazard or fails to comply with an applicable requirement.

A potential recall can result from information obtained through:

- Consumer complaints
- Injury reports
- Product testing
- Internal quality-control investigations
- Retailer reports
- Distributor information
- Government investigations
- Warranty claims
- Lawsuits
- Manufacturing audits
- Laboratory testing

A recall can address either a safety defect or a failure to comply with an applicable federal requirement.

## Steps in a Product Recall

A recall is generally more than simply publishing a notice.

The CPSC's recall checklist identifies several steps that recalling firms may need to consider, including reporting to the CPSC when required, stopping production, identifying affected model or date codes, isolating inventory, notifying distributors and retailers, determining an appropriate remedy, correcting the product, and improving quality-control measures.

A recall process can therefore involve:

1. Identifying the hazard.
2. Determining the affected products.
3. Reporting to the CPSC when legally required.
4. Stopping or controlling distribution.
5. Identifying products already sold.
6. Developing a corrective-action plan.
7. Notifying consumers.
8. Providing a remedy.
9. Monitoring the effectiveness of the recall.
10. Correcting the underlying safety problem.

The appropriate steps depend on the product and circumstances.

## Recall Remedies

A recall can use different forms of consumer relief.

Common remedies include:

- Refunds
- Repairs
- Replacements
- Replacement parts
- Product modifications
- Instructions to stop using the product

The appropriate remedy depends on the nature and severity of the hazard.

The CPSC's recall guidance specifically identifies refunds, repairs, and replacements as potential corrective actions.

Consumers should follow the specific instructions provided in the official recall notice rather than continuing to use a product that has been identified as hazardous.

## Fast Track Recalls

The CPSC operates a **Fast Track Product Recall Program** designed to facilitate corrective action in qualifying circumstances.

The program is intended for situations where a company reports a potentially hazardous product and agrees to an acceptable corrective-action plan.

Fast Track procedures do not apply automatically to every product problem. The CPSC evaluates whether the circumstances satisfy the program's requirements.

The agency provides businesses with recall guidance and a recall handbook explaining reporting obligations and corrective-action procedures.

## Tracking and Identifying Products

Product identification can be critical during a recall.

The CPSIA established tracking requirements for certain children's products. Generally, covered products and their packaging must contain permanent identifying information where practicable, allowing manufacturers and consumers to distinguish products and production periods.

Useful identifiers can include:

- Model numbers
- Serial numbers
- Lot numbers
- Date codes
- UPC information
- Manufacturing dates

These identifiers help determine which products are affected and can prevent unnecessarily broad recalls.

## Consumer Complaints and Safety Reports

Consumer reports can play an important role in identifying emerging hazards.

Consumers can report unsafe products and incidents through the CPSC's reporting systems. The CPSIA also created **SaferProducts.gov**, a publicly searchable database containing reports of harm involving consumer products within the CPSC's jurisdiction.

A complaint does not automatically establish that a product is defective. Reports can nevertheless provide regulators and companies with information that may warrant further investigation.

Patterns involving similar complaints can be particularly important when identifying potential hazards.

## Product Recalls and Injury Claims

A product recall and a product-liability lawsuit are separate processes.

A recall is primarily a regulatory and corrective-action mechanism designed to address a product hazard.

A personal-injury or wrongful-death claim, by contrast, can seek compensation for legally recognized losses.

Depending on the circumstances, a product-liability case may involve allegations of:

- Manufacturing defects
- Design defects
- Failure to warn
- Inadequate instructions
- Breach of warranty
- Other legally recognized theories

State law generally plays a major role in determining the elements of such claims.

## Does a Recall Prove Product Liability?

Not necessarily.

The existence of a recall can be important evidence, particularly when the recalled defect resembles the condition that allegedly caused an injury. However, a recall does not automatically establish that a particular person's injury was caused by the product or that a particular defendant is legally responsible.

A court may still need to consider:

- Whether the plaintiff used the product
- Whether the product was defective
- Whether the alleged defect existed when the product left the defendant's control
- Whether the defect caused the injury
- Whether warnings were adequate
- Whether the plaintiff's conduct contributed to the incident
- Whether applicable statutes of limitations have expired

The legal significance of a recall can therefore vary substantially from case to case.

## What Consumers Should Do After a Product Injury

If a consumer is injured by a potentially defective product, immediate medical needs should come first.

When reasonably possible, the consumer should:

1. Seek appropriate medical treatment.
2. Preserve the product.
3. Keep packaging, labels, receipts, and instructions.
4. Photograph the product and visible damage.
5. Record when and how the product was used.
6. Identify potential witnesses.
7. Keep medical records and related expenses.
8. Check whether the product has been recalled.
9. Report the incident to the appropriate company or regulator when appropriate.
10. Avoid modifying or discarding the product before its condition can be documented.

Preserving the product can be particularly important when the alleged defect cannot be reconstructed from photographs alone.

## What Businesses Should Do When a Hazard Is Discovered

A business that discovers a potential product hazard should establish a process for quickly evaluating the information.

The process can include:

- Identifying the affected product
- Preserving testing and complaint records
- Determining whether a reporting obligation exists
- Stopping distribution when appropriate
- Separating affected inventory
- Contacting relevant supply-chain partners
- Assessing potential injuries
- Developing corrective measures
- Coordinating with regulatory authorities
- Documenting the corrective action

The CPSC's reporting guidance emphasizes the legal importance of prompt reporting when the statutory requirements are triggered.

## New Electronic Certification Requirements

Consumer-product compliance procedures continue to evolve.

The CPSC states that, beginning **July 8, 2026**, importers of most regulated consumer products will be required to electronically file certificates of compliance with U.S. Customs and Border Protection through the Partner Government Agency Message Set.

This development illustrates why businesses importing regulated products need to monitor current federal requirements rather than relying indefinitely on older compliance procedures.

The precise products and certificates covered by the requirement should be verified against current CPSC and CBP guidance.

## Final Takeaway

Product safety regulation in the United States combines testing, certification, reporting, regulatory oversight, and recall procedures.

For children's products subject to federal safety requirements, third-party testing by a CPSC-accepted laboratory is generally required, while general-use products can be subject to different testing and certification rules.

Manufacturers, importers, distributors, and retailers may also have duties to promptly report information about dangerous or noncompliant products. The CPSC generally requires reportable information to be submitted within 24 hours.

When a recall is necessary, businesses may need to stop distribution, identify affected products, notify the supply chain and consumers, and provide an appropriate remedy such as a repair, replacement, or refund.

For consumers, a recall can be an important safety warning, but it does not automatically determine whether a manufacturer, retailer, or another party is legally responsible for an injury. Product-liability claims involve separate legal questions governed largely by applicable state law.

Consumers injured by an allegedly defective product should preserve the product and related documentation, obtain appropriate medical care, and consider advice from an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction.
