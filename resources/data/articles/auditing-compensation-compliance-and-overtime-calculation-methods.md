Compensation compliance requires employers to maintain accurate wage practices, properly classify employees, record working time, and calculate overtime according to applicable law. In the United States, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) establishes federal requirements concerning minimum wages, overtime compensation, and recordkeeping for covered employees. State and local laws may impose additional or more protective requirements.

A compensation audit can help identify calculation errors, inconsistent payroll practices, incomplete records, or potential violations before they develop into larger disputes. For employers, the process involves reviewing both the underlying payroll data and the methods used to calculate employee compensation.

## Establishing the Scope of a Compensation Audit

The first step is determining which employees, pay practices, and legal requirements are being reviewed.

Under the FLSA, covered nonexempt employees generally must receive overtime compensation at a rate of at least one and one-half times their regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. Certain employees and occupations may be exempt from federal overtime requirements, while special rules can apply to particular industries and public-sector positions.

An audit should therefore begin by identifying:

- Which employees are covered by the FLSA
- Which employees are classified as exempt or nonexempt
- The applicable workweek for each employee
- The pay structures used
- The methods used to record working time
- Additional compensation paid during the review period
- Applicable state and local wage requirements

Classification should not be based solely on whether an employee receives a salary. Whether an exemption applies depends on the requirements of the applicable exemption.

## Reviewing Employee Classification

Employee classification is a central component of overtime compliance.

A worker who is incorrectly treated as exempt may not receive overtime compensation even though the FLSA requires it. Conversely, an employer may incorrectly apply overtime rules to an employee who qualifies for an applicable exemption.

The Department of Labor provides separate guidance concerning executive, administrative, professional, computer, outside-sales, and other exemptions.

An audit should therefore compare the employer's classification decisions against the requirements applicable to each position. Job titles alone do not necessarily establish whether an exemption applies.

Where classifications are uncertain, employers may need a more detailed legal review rather than relying solely on payroll software or internal labels.

## Verifying the Workweek

The FLSA treats the workweek as a fixed and regularly recurring period of 168 hours, consisting of seven consecutive 24-hour periods.

The workweek does not have to correspond to the calendar week. It can begin on any day and at any hour established by the employer. Different workweeks may be established for different employees or groups of employees.

This matters because overtime generally must be calculated separately for each workweek.

Employers generally cannot average hours from two workweeks to avoid an overtime obligation. For example, an employee who works 46 hours in one week and 34 hours in the next cannot ordinarily have those hours averaged to produce an 80-hour two-week total for FLSA overtime purposes.

## Auditing Hours Worked

Accurate overtime calculations depend on accurate records of hours worked.

The Department of Labor states that covered employers must maintain records containing information such as hours worked each day, total hours worked each workweek, the employee's regular hourly rate when applicable, overtime earnings, additions and deductions, total wages, and payment dates.

An audit can compare timekeeping records with other information, including:

- Payroll records
- Work schedules
- Timecards
- Electronic timekeeping systems
- Shift records
- Attendance information
- Relevant work communications
- Production or assignment records

The objective is to determine whether recorded hours accurately reflect compensable working time.

## Identifying Unrecorded Working Time

One common audit issue is time that employees worked but that was not included in the payroll records.

The FLSA generally requires covered employees to be compensated for work they are required or permitted to perform, including certain work performed outside scheduled hours. The Department of Labor explains that hours worked generally include time when an employee is required to be on duty or at a prescribed workplace, as well as additional time the employer permits the employee to work.

An employer's policy prohibiting unauthorized overtime does not necessarily eliminate the obligation to pay for compensable overtime that was actually worked. The Department of Labor specifically states that an announcement that overtime will not be permitted or paid unless authorized in advance does not necessarily remove the obligation to compensate qualifying overtime hours.

Auditors should therefore examine whether employees regularly perform work before clocking in, after clocking out, during required activities, or outside scheduled shifts.

## Calculating the Regular Rate

The regular rate is one of the most important components of an overtime audit.

For many covered nonexempt employees, overtime is calculated using the employee's regular rate rather than simply multiplying the employee's basic hourly wage by 1.5.

The Department of Labor explains that the regular rate generally includes remuneration for employment, subject to statutory exclusions. A basic calculation is:

**Total includable compensation for the workweek ÷ Total hours actually worked = Regular rate**

Overtime is then generally calculated at not less than one and one-half times that regular rate for qualifying hours over 40.

This calculation becomes more important when an employee receives compensation in addition to a standard hourly wage.

## Reviewing Bonuses, Commissions, and Other Compensation

An audit should identify compensation beyond ordinary hourly wages or salary.

Depending on the nature of the payment, bonuses, commissions, piece-rate earnings, and other forms of compensation may affect the regular rate. The FLSA contains specific statutory exclusions, meaning that not every payment is treated identically for overtime purposes.

For example, employees paid on a piece-rate or commission basis may still be entitled to overtime. The Department of Labor explains that overtime calculations for such employees generally require determining an appropriate regular rate based on qualifying compensation and hours worked.

An audit should therefore examine the underlying payroll components rather than assuming that the employee's stated hourly rate represents the complete regular-rate calculation.

## Employees Working at Multiple Rates

Some employees perform different types of work during the same workweek at different straight-time rates.

In these circumstances, the Department of Labor explains that the regular rate may be calculated as a weighted average of the applicable rates. The earnings from the different rates are combined and divided by the total hours worked. Certain alternative calculation methods may apply when specific statutory requirements are satisfied.

Payroll systems should therefore be tested using employees who work multiple positions, shifts, locations, or assignments with different compensation rates.

## Testing Overtime Calculations

A practical audit should independently recalculate a sample of employee paychecks.

For each selected workweek, the reviewer can compare:

1. Total hours recorded.
2. Hours exceeding the applicable overtime threshold.
3. Straight-time earnings.
4. Compensation included or excluded from the regular rate.
5. Calculated regular rate.
6. Overtime premium.
7. Total wages actually paid.
8. Any adjustments or deductions affecting the calculation.

The Department of Labor provides an FLSA Overtime Calculator Advisor that can assist with understanding federal overtime calculations for covered, nonexempt employees.

Independent recalculation can reveal whether a payroll system is consistently applying the employer's intended rules.

## Reviewing Payroll Records

Recordkeeping is an essential part of compensation compliance.

The Department of Labor states that covered employers must maintain specified records for nonexempt employees. These include information concerning hours worked, wage rates, straight-time earnings, overtime earnings, additions and deductions, total wages, and payment periods.

Federal recordkeeping rules generally require payroll records to be preserved for at least three years, while records supporting wage computations generally must be retained for two years.

Employers should also consider additional retention requirements under other federal, state, local, or industry-specific laws.

## Comparing Payroll Practices Across Departments

A compensation audit can reveal inconsistencies between departments or locations.

For example, one department may automatically include certain bonuses in overtime calculations while another may exclude them. One location may accurately record pre-shift work while another may rely exclusively on scheduled hours.

Differences do not necessarily establish a legal violation, because compensation practices can vary for legitimate reasons. However, unexplained inconsistencies warrant further review.

A useful audit should therefore examine whether the same legal standards and payroll rules are being applied consistently to similarly situated employees.

## Correcting Identified Errors

Finding a payroll discrepancy is only one part of the compliance process.

When an audit identifies a possible underpayment, the employer should determine the affected employees, relevant workweeks, applicable legal requirements, and amount potentially owed. Corrections should be documented and handled in accordance with applicable wage laws.

Employers should also determine whether the error resulted from a broader payroll-system problem. Correcting one paycheck without examining similar records may leave the underlying issue unresolved.

Where potential violations are significant or legally complex, employers may need advice from qualified wage-and-hour counsel.

## Building an Ongoing Compliance Process

Compensation compliance is more effective when treated as an ongoing process rather than a one-time payroll review.

Employers can establish periodic checks covering employee classifications, timekeeping, overtime calculations, bonuses, commissions, payroll deductions, record retention, and changes in applicable wage laws.

Training supervisors is also important. Managers should understand that requiring employees to work beyond scheduled hours can create compensation issues even when the additional work was not formally authorized.

Payroll personnel should likewise understand which compensation components must be considered when calculating overtime.

## Evaluating Compensation Compliance

An effective compensation audit connects three elements: **classification, time, and pay**.

First, the employer must determine which legal requirements apply to each employee. Second, it must accurately capture compensable working time. Third, it must calculate wages and overtime using the appropriate regular-rate methodology.

The FLSA provides a federal baseline, but state and local wage laws may impose additional requirements. Employers operating in multiple jurisdictions should therefore avoid assuming that federal compliance alone resolves every compensation issue.

Accurate records, properly tested payroll calculations, consistent policies, and periodic review can help identify problems before they become larger disputes. Where the facts involve potentially significant underpayments, employee classification questions, or competing federal and state requirements, professional legal and payroll advice can help determine the appropriate response.
