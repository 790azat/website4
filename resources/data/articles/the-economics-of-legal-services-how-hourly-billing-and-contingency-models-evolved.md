The way lawyers charge for legal services has changed substantially over time. Traditional arrangements included retainers, fixed fees, and negotiated payments, while modern law firms commonly use hourly billing, contingency fees, flat fees, blended rates, and other arrangements.

These fee structures do more than determine how much a client pays. They also allocate financial risk between the lawyer and client.

An hourly arrangement generally links the lawyer's compensation to the time spent working on a matter. A contingency arrangement instead links compensation to the outcome of a case, with the lawyer typically receiving an agreed percentage of a monetary recovery.

The development of these models reflects changes in the legal profession, accounting practices, litigation costs, professional ethics, and access to legal services. In the United States, modern fee arrangements are also governed by state-specific professional-conduct rules, many of which are based on the American Bar Association's Model Rules of Professional Conduct.

## Early Approaches to Legal Fees

Legal fees were not always calculated using the detailed timekeeping systems familiar today.

Historically, lawyers could use arrangements such as retainers, fixed payments, fees for particular services, or negotiated amounts based on the nature of the representation.

The Supreme Court's later discussion of legal billing practices noted that hourly records originally served primarily as an internal accounting mechanism rather than necessarily determining what a client would ultimately pay. Other approaches included annual retainers, fee-for-service arrangements, estimated fees, and contingency arrangements.

This distinction is important because **timekeeping and hourly billing are not necessarily the same thing**.

A lawyer could record time for administrative or accounting purposes while still charging a client through another fee structure.

## The Development of Hourly Billing

Modern hourly billing developed gradually.

The Supreme Court noted in *Gisbrecht v. Barnhart* that the American Bar Association's 1958 report on the economics of law practice encouraged attorneys to record the hours spent on matters as a way of improving the relationship between professional effort and compensation. The Court also observed that hourly billing became widespread by the early 1970s.

Several economic factors contributed to this development.

Hourly billing gave law firms a relatively straightforward method for:

- Tracking lawyer time
- Allocating work among attorneys
- Measuring costs
- Billing clients periodically
- Managing large matters
- Comparing workloads
- Calculating revenue

As firms grew larger and legal matters became more complex, detailed timekeeping became increasingly useful for managing professional services.

## How Hourly Billing Works

Under a traditional hourly arrangement, a lawyer charges an agreed rate for time spent performing legal work.

A bill might include time spent on:

- Research
- Drafting
- Client communications
- Negotiations
- Court appearances
- Document review
- Discovery
- Depositions
- Strategy
- Administrative work related to the representation

Different lawyers at the same firm may have different hourly rates based on experience, role, or other factors.

For example, a matter could involve a partner, associate, and paralegal, each billed at different rates.

The total legal fee is then generally calculated by multiplying the applicable rates by the time recorded, subject to the terms of the engagement agreement and applicable ethical rules.

## The Economics of Hourly Billing

Hourly billing places much of the financial risk of litigation or legal work on the client.

If a matter takes longer than expected, the client's legal bill can increase unless the agreement establishes a cap, estimate, alternative fee arrangement, or other limitation.

For the lawyer, the model provides a closer connection between professional time and revenue.

However, hourly billing can also create economic concerns.

The ABA's Model Rule 1.5 commentary specifically cautions that lawyers should not exploit an arrangement based primarily on hourly charges by using wasteful procedures.

This reflects a broader ethical principle: the billing structure should not encourage conduct inconsistent with the client's interests.

## Retainers and Advance Payments

A retainer can have different meanings depending on the agreement and jurisdiction.

Some retainers function as advance payments for legal services. Others are designed to secure a lawyer's availability.

The precise treatment of a payment depends on the agreement and applicable professional-conduct rules.

The ABA's Model Rule 1.5 commentary states that a lawyer may require advance payment but must return any unearned portion under the applicable rules.

Clients should therefore understand whether money paid at the beginning of a representation is an earned fee, an advance against future services, or another type of arrangement.

## The Development of Contingency Fees

Contingency fees developed as another way of allocating financial risk.

Under a contingency arrangement, the lawyer's compensation generally depends on obtaining a monetary recovery for the client.

The Supreme Court has noted that contingency fees were recognized in American law well before hourly billing became widespread. It also observed that the American Bar Association formally approved contingency fees in 1908.

The model became particularly significant in litigation where clients might otherwise have difficulty paying substantial legal fees before a recovery.

## How a Contingency Fee Works

A contingency arrangement typically establishes a percentage of the recovery that will constitute the lawyer's fee.

For example, an agreement might specify one percentage if the case settles before litigation and a different percentage if the matter proceeds further.

The exact structure varies by lawyer, jurisdiction, and type of case.

Cornell's Legal Information Institute explains that contingency fees are particularly common in personal-injury matters and generally compensate the lawyer through a percentage of the client's monetary recovery.

The agreement should also address litigation expenses and whether those expenses are deducted before or after calculating the attorney's percentage.

## Why Contingency Fees Changed Access to Legal Services

One important economic feature of contingency arrangements is that they can reduce the need for a client to pay substantial attorney fees at the beginning of a case.

This can make litigation more accessible to people who have a potentially valuable claim but limited ability to finance an extended lawsuit.

The arrangement also transfers some risk to the lawyer.

If there is no recovery, the lawyer may receive no contingency fee, although the client can still potentially be responsible for certain litigation expenses depending on the agreement and applicable law.

This risk affects how lawyers evaluate potential cases.

A firm considering a contingency matter may assess:

- Strength of the evidence
- Potential damages
- Liability issues
- Litigation costs
- Likely duration
- Defendant's ability to pay
- Insurance coverage
- Probability of recovery

## Contingency Fees as Risk Allocation

The economic difference between hourly and contingency billing can be summarized as a question of risk.

With hourly billing, the client generally pays for legal work as it is performed.

With contingency billing, the lawyer accepts greater uncertainty about whether and when compensation will be received.

That difference helps explain why contingency percentages can appear substantial even when a case ultimately resolves without a trial.

The lawyer may spend months or years working on a case without receiving the contingency fee until a recovery occurs.

At the same time, the client is not simply purchasing hours of legal work. The client and lawyer are entering an arrangement in which compensation is linked to the outcome and the financial risk of pursuing the claim.

## Ethical Regulation of Legal Fees

Modern attorney-fee arrangements are subject to professional-conduct rules.

ABA Model Rule 1.5 states that a lawyer must not charge an unreasonable fee or unreasonable expenses. The rule identifies factors relevant to reasonableness, including the time and labor required, difficulty of the matter, customary fees in the locality, the amount involved, time limitations, the lawyer's experience, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent.

Individual states generally establish their own professional-conduct rules, and those rules may differ from the ABA Model Rules.

As a result, the Model Rules provide an important reference point but should not be treated as identical to the law in every jurisdiction.

## Written Fee Agreements

Modern professional rules emphasize transparency concerning legal fees.

ABA Model Rule 1.5 provides that the basis or rate of the fee and expenses for which the client will be responsible should be communicated to the client, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after representation begins.

Contingency agreements receive additional requirements.

Under the Model Rule, a contingency agreement must be in writing and signed by the client. It must explain how the fee will be calculated, including the percentage or percentages applicable to different stages of the case and how litigation expenses will be treated.

State rules may impose additional requirements.

## Limits on Contingency Fees

Contingency arrangements are not permitted in every type of legal matter.

The ABA Model Rules prohibit contingency fees for representing a defendant in a criminal case and prohibit certain contingency arrangements in domestic-relations matters, such as fees contingent on obtaining a divorce or the amount of alimony or property settlement.

Other legal restrictions can apply depending on the jurisdiction and type of case.

Some states also impose statutory or regulatory limits on particular contingency fees.

The applicable state rules therefore need to be reviewed before assuming that a particular arrangement is permissible.

## Alternative Fee Arrangements

Hourly and contingency billing are not the only options.

Law firms and clients can sometimes use:

- Flat fees
- Fixed-fee stages
- Blended hourly rates
- Capped fees
- Retainers
- Subscription arrangements
- Success fees
- Hybrid hourly-contingency arrangements

The appropriate structure depends on the type of legal service and the agreement between the lawyer and client.

A transactional matter with a predictable scope may be suitable for a fixed fee, while complex litigation may involve hourly or contingency compensation.

## Fixed Fees

A fixed or flat fee establishes a predetermined amount for defined legal services.

This can provide the client with greater predictability concerning cost.

Examples can include:

- Preparation of a standard legal document
- Certain immigration services
- Estate-planning packages
- Routine business formations
- Defined contract reviews

The scope of work should be clearly defined.

If a matter becomes substantially more complicated than anticipated, the lawyer and client may need to negotiate additional services or a modified arrangement, subject to applicable professional rules.

## Hybrid Fee Models

Some matters combine different compensation methods.

For example, a client might pay an initial hourly amount while agreeing that additional compensation will depend on a successful outcome.

Another arrangement might use a reduced hourly rate combined with a success-based component.

These structures can distribute risk differently from either a pure hourly or pure contingency agreement.

The precise legality and ethical requirements depend on the jurisdiction and circumstances.

## Expenses Are Different From Attorney Fees

A common source of confusion is the difference between attorney fees and litigation expenses.

A case can generate costs for:

- Court filing fees
- Expert witnesses
- Medical records
- Depositions
- Transcripts
- Investigators
- Service of process
- Travel
- Document reproduction
- Specialized testing

A contingency arrangement does not necessarily mean the client pays none of these expenses.

The ABA Model Rule requires contingency agreements to explain litigation and other expenses and whether they are deducted before or after the attorney's fee is calculated.

Clients should therefore examine both the attorney-fee percentage and the treatment of case expenses.

## The Economics of Law-Firm Time

Hourly billing also reflects the economics of professional services.

Law firms generally have substantial operating costs, including:

- Lawyer compensation
- Staff salaries
- Office expenses
- Technology
- Professional insurance
- Research systems
- Licensing and bar expenses
- Administrative support

Billable time can therefore be one component of a firm's revenue model.

But not every hour a lawyer works can necessarily be billed to a client.

Lawyers may also spend time on business development, professional education, administration, recruiting, management, and other activities.

This helps explain why firms closely track lawyer time even when some work is not directly billed.

## Technology and Changes in Legal Billing

Technology has changed how legal services are delivered and measured.

Electronic document systems, legal research databases, case-management software, artificial intelligence tools, and automated document review can reduce the amount of time required for certain tasks.

This creates an economic question for hourly billing: if a technology allows a lawyer to complete a task more quickly, should the client pay less because fewer hours were recorded?

There is no universal answer.

The ABA Model Rule focuses on the reasonableness of the fee rather than requiring every fee to correspond mechanically to the number of hours worked.

Technology therefore continues to encourage firms and clients to consider alternative approaches to pricing legal services.

## Value, Risk, and Results

Modern legal fees are increasingly discussed in terms of both **time and value**.

The ABA Model Rule's factors for evaluating fee reasonableness include not only time and labor but also the amount involved, results obtained, experience, customary local fees, and other circumstances.

This does not mean that lawyers can simply charge any amount based on the perceived value of a case.

Instead, it reflects the broader principle that reasonable legal fees cannot necessarily be measured by hours alone.

## Fee Transparency

Clear communication about fees can reduce disputes between lawyers and clients.

A well-defined agreement can explain:

- What services are included
- What services are excluded
- The hourly rates, if applicable
- The contingency percentage, if applicable
- How expenses are handled
- When invoices are issued
- Whether an advance payment is required
- What happens if representation ends
- Whether rates can change
- Who is responsible for third-party costs

The ABA's Model Rule commentary emphasizes that written fee information can reduce misunderstandings about the financial terms of representation.

## Comparing Hourly and Contingency Models

The two models allocate economic risk differently.

| Feature | Hourly Billing | Contingency Fee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main calculation | Time × agreed rate | Agreed percentage of recovery |
| Payment timing | Usually during representation | Usually after recovery |
| Client's financial risk | Generally pays for time worked | Lawyer assumes more risk of no fee |
| Common applications | Business, transactional, regulatory, litigation | Personal injury and other recovery-based cases |
| Total cost predictability | Can vary with hours worked | Depends on recovery and agreement |
| Expenses | Usually addressed separately | Usually addressed separately |
| Written agreement | Strongly encouraged and often required by rules | Specific written requirements generally apply |

Neither model is universally appropriate. The right structure depends on the nature of the legal work, the client's circumstances, applicable law, and the agreement reached with counsel.

## How Fee Models Affect Client Decisions

Before entering a legal representation agreement, a client should understand the economic structure of the relationship.

Important questions can include:

- How is the lawyer paid?
- Is the rate hourly or fixed?
- If the fee is contingent, what percentage applies?
- Does the percentage change if the case goes to trial or appeal?
- Who pays litigation expenses?
- Are expenses deducted before calculating the contingency fee?
- What happens if there is no recovery?
- Can the lawyer's rates change?
- What happens if the client ends the representation?
- What work falls outside the agreed scope?

The answers should be documented in the engagement agreement and evaluated under the applicable professional rules.

## Final Takeaway

The economics of legal services have evolved from relatively flexible historical arrangements toward increasingly formalized billing and accounting systems.

Hourly billing became widespread in the United States during the twentieth century, particularly as law firms adopted systematic timekeeping and developed larger, more complex professional practices. The Supreme Court has noted that hourly billing became widespread by the early 1970s.

Contingency fees developed along a different path. They allowed lawyers to link compensation to the outcome of a case and shifted more financial risk toward the lawyer. They became particularly important in personal-injury litigation and other matters where clients might otherwise face substantial upfront legal costs.

Modern professional-conduct rules seek to balance these economic arrangements with transparency and fairness. ABA Model Rule 1.5 requires reasonable fees and establishes detailed requirements for communicating fee arrangements and documenting contingency agreements.

Today, clients may encounter hourly, flat, contingency, hybrid, and other fee structures. Because fee rules vary by state and by the type of legal matter, a client should review the engagement agreement carefully and consider advice from an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction if the financial terms are unclear.
