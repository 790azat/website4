Workplace incidents can range from minor injuries and near misses to serious accidents involving hospitalization, permanent disability, or death. Employers use safety programs, training, protective equipment, emergency procedures, inspections, and incident investigations to reduce these risks.

In the United States, workplace safety is governed in large part by the **Occupational Safety and Health Act** and regulations administered by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA). Additional requirements can apply depending on the industry, state, workplace, and type of hazard.

When a worker is injured, several different issues may arise. The employer may have OSHA reporting or recordkeeping obligations, the worker may need medical treatment and workers' compensation benefits, and regulators may investigate whether safety requirements were followed.

These processes are related but are not identical. An OSHA violation does not automatically establish civil liability for an injury, and an OSHA record does not by itself determine the amount of compensation a worker may receive.

## What OSHA Regulates

OSHA establishes and enforces workplace safety and health requirements for covered employers and employees.

The regulations address a wide range of hazards, including:

- Falls
- Hazardous machinery
- Electrical hazards
- Hazardous chemicals
- Respiratory hazards
- Noise exposure
- Fire and emergency hazards
- Workplace violence
- Personal protective equipment
- Material handling
- Walking-working surfaces
- Construction hazards

OSHA's requirements differ depending on the industry and workplace. General-industry standards are primarily found in 29 CFR Part 1910, while construction requirements are generally found in Part 1926.

Employers may also be subject to state-plan requirements where an OSHA-approved state occupational-safety program applies.

## Employer Safety Programs

OSHA recommends a proactive approach to workplace safety rather than waiting for an employee to be injured before identifying hazards.

Its recommended safety and health practices include management leadership, worker participation, hazard identification and assessment, hazard prevention and control, education and training, program evaluation, and communication.

A workplace safety program may include:

- Routine hazard inspections
- Employee safety training
- Equipment inspections
- Personal protective equipment
- Written operating procedures
- Emergency plans
- Safety meetings
- Hazard reporting
- Maintenance programs
- Incident investigations

The specific requirements depend on the workplace and the OSHA standards that apply.

## Emergency Action Plans

Emergency procedures are an important part of workplace safety.

OSHA's general-industry emergency-action-plan standard, 29 CFR § 1910.38, requires an emergency action plan when an OSHA standard requires one. Among other things, the plan must address emergency reporting, evacuation procedures, accounting for employees after evacuation, and designated employees responsible for rescue or medical duties.

Construction employers can be subject to separate requirements under 29 CFR § 1926.35.

Depending on the workplace, emergency planning may address:

- Fires
- Chemical releases
- Severe weather
- Workplace violence
- Explosions
- Natural disasters
- Medical emergencies
- Evacuations
- Shelter-in-place procedures

OSHA emphasizes that emergency plans should be site-specific and that workers should receive appropriate training concerning their responsibilities.

## Common Workplace Incidents

Workplace incidents can involve many different hazards.

### Falls

Falls are a major concern in construction, warehouses, manufacturing facilities, and other workplaces.

Potential hazards include:

- Unprotected edges
- Improper ladders
- Scaffolding problems
- Wet surfaces
- Openings
- Inadequate guardrails
- Poor housekeeping

The applicable OSHA requirements depend on the work environment and the specific activity involved.

### Machinery Injuries

Industrial machinery can create crushing, cutting, amputation, and entanglement hazards.

Employers may need to use appropriate machine guarding, lockout/tagout procedures, employee training, and maintenance practices.

An investigation may examine whether safety guards were installed, whether workers were trained, and whether established procedures were followed.

### Electrical Incidents

Electrical hazards can cause shocks, burns, fires, and fatal injuries.

Investigators may examine equipment condition, electrical installations, training, protective equipment, lockout procedures, and whether applicable electrical-safety standards were followed.

### Chemical Exposure

Chemical incidents can result from spills, leaks, improper storage, inadequate ventilation, or insufficient protective equipment.

Depending on the substance, OSHA standards may impose requirements concerning labeling, safety data sheets, exposure limits, personal protective equipment, training, and emergency procedures.

## What Happens Immediately After an Injury?

A workplace injury should first be treated as a health and safety issue.

Depending on the circumstances, an appropriate response may include:

1. Obtain emergency medical treatment when necessary.
2. Make the area safe to prevent additional injuries.
3. Notify the appropriate supervisor or workplace contact.
4. Follow the employer's incident-reporting procedure.
5. Preserve relevant evidence.
6. Identify witnesses.
7. Document the location and conditions.
8. Obtain appropriate medical documentation.
9. Determine whether the incident must be reported to OSHA.
10. Begin an appropriate incident investigation.

Emergency treatment should not be delayed simply because an employer's internal reporting process has not yet been completed.

## OSHA Reporting Requirements

OSHA distinguishes between **recording** workplace injuries and **reporting** certain serious incidents directly to OSHA.

OSHA states that all employers must report a work-related fatality within **8 hours**. Employers must generally report a work-related inpatient hospitalization, amputation, or loss of an eye within **24 hours**.

These reporting requirements are separate from ordinary OSHA recordkeeping requirements.

Whether a particular incident must be reported can depend on the circumstances and definitions contained in OSHA regulations.

## OSHA Recordkeeping

Many employers with more than 10 employees must maintain records of certain work-related injuries and illnesses, although there are exceptions for some lower-risk industries.

OSHA's recordkeeping system generally uses:

- **OSHA Form 300** — Log of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses
- **OSHA Form 300A** — Summary of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses
- **OSHA Form 301** — Injury and Illness Incident Report

OSHA regulations establish criteria for determining which work-related injuries and illnesses must be recorded. These include cases involving death, days away from work, restricted work or job transfer, medical treatment beyond first aid, loss of consciousness, and certain significant diagnoses.

An injury being OSHA-recordable does not necessarily mean that an employer was legally negligent.

## Workplace Incident Investigations

OSHA strongly encourages employers to investigate workplace injuries, illnesses, and near misses.

The purpose of a proper investigation should generally be to identify hazards and **root causes**, rather than simply assigning blame to an individual worker. OSHA recommends developing procedures that allow investigations to begin promptly and using trained investigators who approach the process objectively.

An investigation may examine:

- What happened?
- Where did it happen?
- What equipment was involved?
- What task was being performed?
- What conditions existed?
- What safety procedures applied?
- Was the worker trained?
- Was protective equipment available?
- Were previous warnings or complaints made?
- Was equipment properly maintained?
- Were there similar previous incidents?

The goal is generally to identify corrective measures that can prevent another incident.

## Evidence After a Workplace Incident

Evidence can be important both for safety investigations and for determining whether an injured worker may have a legal claim.

Potential evidence includes:

- Photographs
- Video surveillance
- Equipment condition
- Safety manuals
- Training records
- Inspection records
- Maintenance logs
- Work schedules
- Incident reports
- OSHA records
- Witness statements
- Medical records
- Safety meeting records
- Emails or other workplace communications

Physical evidence should be preserved when reasonably possible, particularly when machinery, defective equipment, or a hazardous condition may have contributed to the incident.

## Workers' Compensation

For many employees, an important source of benefits after a workplace injury is **workers' compensation**.

Workers' compensation systems generally provide benefits for qualifying work-related injuries without requiring the employee to prove ordinary negligence by the employer. Benefits can include medical treatment and, depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, partial wage replacement or other compensation.

The rules governing workers' compensation are primarily established by state law for most private-sector employees.

Federal workers' compensation systems apply to certain categories of workers, including some federal employees and workers covered by specialized federal programs.

Because eligibility requirements and procedures vary, an injured worker should determine which workers' compensation system applies to the employment relationship.

## OSHA Violations and Injury Claims

A workplace safety violation can be relevant to an injury investigation, but the existence of an OSHA violation does not automatically establish civil liability.

OSHA regulations are designed primarily to establish and enforce occupational safety requirements.

A separate injury claim may involve different legal standards.

For example, an injured worker may need to establish:

- That the injury was work-related
- That the applicable workers' compensation requirements were satisfied
- The nature and extent of the injury
- Medical causation
- Compliance with applicable filing requirements

In some circumstances, a third party may also be legally responsible. Examples can include an equipment manufacturer, property owner, subcontractor, or another company.

The potential claims and defenses depend on the applicable state and federal law.

## Third-Party Workplace Injury Claims

Workers' compensation does not necessarily eliminate every possible legal claim arising from a workplace incident.

For example, suppose a worker is injured by defective equipment manufactured by another company. Depending on the circumstances, a product-liability claim could potentially exist in addition to workers' compensation benefits.

Similarly, an employee working at a location controlled by another company could potentially encounter issues involving premises liability or the negligence of a third party.

These cases can be complicated because workers' compensation laws may affect claims against employers while different rules govern claims against outside parties.

## Employer Retaliation and Safety Complaints

Workplace safety laws also protect certain employees who raise safety concerns or participate in protected activities.

Employees may have rights concerning complaints about unsafe working conditions or cooperation with OSHA investigations.

The specific protections and deadlines depend on the statute involved. OSHA administers whistleblower protections under numerous federal laws covering different types of protected activities.

An employee who believes they were disciplined, terminated, or otherwise retaliated against after engaging in legally protected activity should promptly determine which law applies and what filing deadline governs the complaint.

## Documentation After an Injury

Workers can benefit from maintaining accurate records after an incident.

Useful documentation can include:

- Date and time of the incident
- Exact location
- Description of what happened
- Names of witnesses
- Photographs
- Medical visits
- Diagnosis and treatment
- Work restrictions
- Missed work
- Employer communications
- Accident-report information
- Equipment involved
- Safety instructions received

Workers should generally avoid altering records or making statements they know to be inaccurate. Accurate documentation can help establish the sequence of events and clarify disagreements about what occurred.

## When an Incident Involves a Safety Regulation

A regulation can become important when investigating a workplace incident, but it should be analyzed carefully.

Investigators may ask:

1. Did the regulation apply to this workplace?
2. What specific requirement did it impose?
3. Was the requirement satisfied?
4. If not, what caused the violation?
5. Did the violation relate to the hazard involved?
6. Did the condition contribute to the injury?
7. What corrective measures were taken afterward?

This approach helps distinguish a technical compliance issue from evidence directly connected to the injury.

## Workplace Safety Is an Ongoing Process

OSHA's recommended practices emphasize that safety management should be proactive. Employers are encouraged to identify hazards before injuries occur, involve workers in safety programs, evaluate controls, and continuously improve safety procedures.

Near misses can therefore be useful even when nobody is injured.

A near miss may reveal a dangerous condition before it produces a serious injury. Investigating it can allow an employer to correct the underlying hazard and reduce the likelihood of a future incident.

## Final Takeaway

Workplace safety in the United States involves a combination of OSHA regulations, employer safety programs, industry-specific requirements, state laws, and workers' compensation systems.

After an incident, employers may have obligations to provide emergency procedures, investigate the event, maintain injury records, and report certain serious injuries to OSHA. OSHA generally requires reporting of work-related fatalities within eight hours and inpatient hospitalizations, amputations, and losses of an eye within 24 hours.

For injured workers, the legal and administrative process can involve several separate questions. Workers' compensation may provide benefits for a qualifying occupational injury, while OSHA investigations address workplace safety compliance. A separate claim may also arise against a third party in appropriate circumstances.

An OSHA violation does not automatically establish negligence or determine the amount of compensation available. The significance of a safety violation depends on the applicable law, the evidence, and its relationship to the injury.

Anyone seriously injured at work should prioritize medical treatment, preserve relevant documentation, follow applicable reporting procedures, and consider obtaining advice about workers' compensation and any potential third-party claims under the law governing the workplace.
