A motorcycle collision can leave a rider dealing with injuries, damaged equipment, traffic hazards, and uncertainty about what happened. The first priority after a crash should always be personal safety and medical care. Documentation becomes important only after immediate dangers have been addressed.

Motorcyclists face particular risks in traffic collisions. According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), 6,228 motorcyclists were killed in U.S. traffic crashes in 2024, representing 16% of all traffic fatalities. NHTSA also reports that, per vehicle mile traveled, motorcyclists were almost 27 times more likely than passenger-car occupants to die in a crash in 2024.

Because a motorcycle and rider can be separated during a collision, documenting both the roadway and the surrounding circumstances can be particularly useful.

## 1. Get to a Safe Location When Possible

Immediately after a collision, assess whether you are in danger from moving traffic, fire, leaking fuel, damaged vehicles, or other hazards.

If you can move safely, get away from active traffic and follow instructions from emergency responders. Do not return to the motorcycle or roadway simply to retrieve belongings if doing so would create another danger.

If you or another person appears seriously injured, emergency medical assistance should take priority over photographs, insurance information, or other documentation.

## 2. Contact Emergency Services When Appropriate

Call 911 or the appropriate emergency service when there are significant injuries, dangerous roadway conditions, substantial vehicle damage, or other circumstances requiring immediate assistance.

When reporting the crash, provide:

- Your location
- The direction of travel
- Number of vehicles involved
- Apparent injuries
- Whether anyone is trapped
- Whether traffic lanes are blocked
- Whether there is fire, smoke, or a fuel leak
- Any other immediate hazard

Accurate location information can help emergency personnel reach the scene more quickly and determine what resources are required.

## 3. Seek Medical Evaluation for Injuries

Motorcycle crashes can produce injuries even when a rider initially feels capable of walking or communicating normally.

Seek appropriate medical attention for symptoms such as:

- Head or neck pain
- Dizziness
- Confusion
- Loss of consciousness
- Severe or increasing pain
- Numbness or weakness
- Difficulty breathing
- Significant bleeding
- Difficulty walking or using an arm or leg

A medical evaluation also creates a contemporaneous record of injuries and symptoms.

NHTSA emphasizes the importance of motorcycle helmets because riders are particularly vulnerable to serious injury in crashes. Current NHTSA guidance recommends helmets that meet Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 218.

## 4. Do Not Remove Your Helmet Without a Reason

If a rider has suffered a crash, helmet removal should not become an unnecessary part of the initial response.

If medical personnel are present, follow their instructions. A person who has suffered a significant impact may have injuries that are not immediately obvious.

The helmet itself can also become relevant evidence. If it struck the ground or another object, avoid discarding it or altering it before its condition has been documented.

NHTSA notes that motorcycle helmets are designed to reduce the likelihood of head injuries in crashes and that FMVSS No. 218 establishes performance requirements for motorcycle helmets.

## 5. Photograph the Scene When It Is Safe

Once immediate safety concerns have been addressed, photographs can help preserve information about the collision.

Useful photographs may show:

- The motorcycle from multiple angles
- Damage to other vehicles
- The overall roadway
- Lane markings
- Traffic signs and signals
- Skid or tire marks
- Debris
- Vehicle positions
- Road surface conditions
- Potholes or other roadway features
- Nearby intersections
- Visible surveillance cameras
- Weather or lighting conditions

Take both close-up photographs and wider images showing how the individual pieces of evidence relate to the surrounding roadway.

NHTSA's professional crash-investigation methodology similarly relies on scene photographs, measurements, vehicle inspections, and interviews to reconstruct crash circumstances.

## 6. Photograph Motorcycle Damage Before Repairs

The motorcycle should be photographed thoroughly before repairs or parts replacement when circumstances allow.

Document:

- Front and rear damage
- Side damage
- Handlebars
- Wheels and tires
- Forks and suspension
- Foot controls
- Exhaust components
- Mirrors
- Lighting equipment
- Brake components
- Fuel tank
- Fairings and body panels
- Scrapes or impact marks

Photograph damaged components from several angles.

If a component has separated from the motorcycle, photograph its location before it is moved when doing so is safe.

## 7. Preserve Protective Equipment

Do not immediately discard damaged riding equipment.

Potentially relevant items can include:

- Helmet
- Jacket
- Gloves
- Boots
- Pants
- Protective armor
- Reflective equipment
- Motorcycle accessories

Photograph damaged equipment and keep it in its post-crash condition when practical.

For example, a damaged helmet may contain visible impact marks that could help document what occurred. NHTSA recognizes protective clothing and equipment as important components of motorcycle safety, including protective jackets, pants, boots, and gloves.

## 8. Exchange Information With Other Drivers

When medically and physically able, obtain information from the other people involved.

Depending on local requirements, this can include:

- Full name
- Contact information
- Driver's license information
- Insurance company
- Policy information
- Vehicle registration information
- License plate number
- Vehicle make and model

Avoid turning the exchange into an argument about who caused the collision.

It is generally better to provide factual information to police and insurers than to make speculative statements about fault at the scene.

## 9. Identify Witnesses

Independent witnesses can potentially provide information about what happened before, during, or immediately after a collision.

If witnesses are willing to provide their information, record:

- Name
- Telephone number
- Email address
- General location from which they observed the collision

Do not pressure witnesses to adopt a particular version of events.

A witness may remember only part of a collision, and different witnesses may have observed different portions of the incident.

## 10. Look for Available Video Evidence

Many collisions occur near locations with cameras.

Potential sources include:

- Traffic cameras
- Business security cameras
- Parking-lot cameras
- Residential cameras
- Dashcams
- Helmet cameras
- Motorcycle cameras
- Other motorists' dashcams

If you notice a potentially relevant camera, document its location and the business or property associated with it.

Video systems frequently overwrite recordings automatically. For that reason, identifying a potential source promptly can be useful when a collision is disputed.

## 11. Preserve Digital Evidence

Modern motorcycles and riders may generate useful electronic information.

Depending on the equipment involved, potential evidence may include:

- Helmet-camera recordings
- Motorcycle camera footage
- GPS records
- Navigation history
- Smartphone location information
- Ride-tracking applications
- Photographs
- Text messages
- Time-stamped communications

Do not edit or overwrite potentially relevant original files.

When possible, preserve the original recording and make a separate copy for sharing or review.

## 12. Document Road and Weather Conditions

Road conditions can become difficult to reconstruct after a crash.

If safe to do so, document:

- Wet or icy pavement
- Standing water
- Gravel
- Oil or other substances
- Potholes
- Construction zones
- Damaged pavement
- Missing or obscured signs
- Visibility conditions
- Lighting
- Roadway obstructions

Record the approximate time and location of the photographs.

This information can be useful because roadway conditions may change shortly after the collision.

## 13. Obtain the Police Report Information

If law enforcement responds, obtain the information necessary to identify the crash report.

This may include:

- Report number
- Responding agency
- Officer information
- Date of collision
- Instructions for requesting the report

A police crash report can be an important source of information, but it is not necessarily the only evidence considered when determining what happened.

NHTSA explains that police crash reports document characteristics of crashes, vehicles, and people involved. More detailed crash investigations can incorporate scene evidence, vehicle inspections, interviews, and medical records.

## 14. Notify the Insurance Company

Review the applicable insurance policies and notify the appropriate insurer according to their reporting requirements.

The insurer may request:

- Crash details
- Police report information
- Photographs
- Motorcycle information
- Repair estimates
- Medical information
- Witness information
- Information concerning other vehicles or insurance policies

Keep copies of documents submitted to the insurer.

An insurance investigation may consider multiple sources of evidence rather than relying on one photograph, statement, or report.

## 15. Preserve the Motorcycle Before Repairs

If the motorcycle may be involved in an insurance dispute or potential legal claim, consider preserving its condition until important evidence has been documented.

Before repairs, useful steps can include:

1. Photograph the entire motorcycle.
2. Photograph individual damaged components.
3. Save repair estimates.
4. Keep replaced parts when practical.
5. Preserve photographs and video recordings.
6. Keep towing and storage documentation.
7. Record who has possession of the motorcycle.

Repairs may be necessary for safety or transportation, but important evidence can become unavailable once damaged components are replaced or discarded.

## 16. Keep Medical and Expense Records

Create a dedicated record of collision-related expenses and treatment.

Potential records include:

- Emergency-room records
- Physician records
- Prescription receipts
- Physical therapy records
- Medical bills
- Motorcycle repair estimates
- Towing invoices
- Storage charges
- Replacement transportation costs
- Receipts for damaged protective equipment

Keep the original documents when possible and maintain organized copies.

Medical documentation should accurately reflect symptoms, diagnoses, treatment, and follow-up rather than being altered to support a particular claim.

## 17. Avoid Speculation About Fault

A rider may be confident about what happened, but the legal determination of responsibility can involve evidence that is not available immediately after the crash.

At the scene, focus on observable facts.

For example, it is generally more useful to report that another vehicle entered your lane than to make a definitive statement about the driver's legal liability.

Similarly, avoid posting accusations or detailed theories about the crash on social media while the circumstances remain under investigation.

## 18. Understand That Documentation Does Not Automatically Establish Liability

Photographs, medical records, witness statements, police reports, and vehicle inspections can all be relevant, but none necessarily determines legal responsibility by itself.

A motorcycle collision claim can involve questions concerning:

- Negligence
- Traffic violations
- Comparative fault
- Roadway conditions
- Vehicle defects
- Insurance coverage
- Injury causation
- Damages
- Applicable state deadlines

The rules differ among jurisdictions. A traffic violation, for example, may be relevant evidence without automatically resolving every element of a civil claim.

## Final Takeaway

For a motorcycle rider, the best post-collision documentation begins with **safety and medical care**, not photography.

Once the immediate danger has passed, riders can preserve useful evidence by photographing the roadway and vehicles, documenting injuries and damaged protective equipment, identifying witnesses and potential video sources, preserving digital records, obtaining police-report information, and keeping insurance, medical, towing, and repair documents.

NHTSA's crash-investigation methodology illustrates why preserving information about the **scene, vehicles, people, and surrounding conditions** can be important when reconstructing a collision.

Because motorcycle-crash claims can involve state-specific insurance, negligence, evidence, and filing rules, riders dealing with significant injuries or disputed responsibility may benefit from discussing the circumstances with an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction.
