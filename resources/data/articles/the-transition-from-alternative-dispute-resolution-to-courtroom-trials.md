Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) and courtroom litigation are not necessarily separate, sequential systems. A civil dispute can move between negotiation, mediation, discovery, settlement conferences, motions, and trial as the parties and court evaluate whether the case can be resolved without a final judicial decision.

In the U.S. federal courts, judges commonly encourage parties to consider mediation, arbitration, settlement conferences, and other ADR procedures. If the dispute is not resolved, the case continues through the litigation process and can ultimately proceed to trial. The U.S. Courts explain that civil cases generally involve pleadings, discovery, motions, settlement efforts, and, when necessary, trial.

The transition from ADR to trial is therefore better understood as a change in the method used to resolve the dispute rather than a completely separate legal process.

## What Is Alternative Dispute Resolution?

Alternative dispute resolution encompasses procedures designed to resolve disputes without a conventional trial.

Common forms include:

- Negotiation;
- Mediation;
- Arbitration;
- Early neutral evaluation;
- Judicial settlement conferences;
- Mini-trials; and
- Other court-approved settlement processes.

The procedures differ substantially.

In **mediation**, a neutral third party facilitates negotiations but generally does not impose a decision. In **arbitration**, an arbitrator may hear evidence and arguments and issue a decision, which can be binding depending on the agreement and applicable law.

Federal courts use different ADR programs and local procedures. For example, federal district courts may offer mediation, early neutral evaluation, settlement conferences, and other processes under their local rules.

## Why ADR May Be Used Before Trial

A trial can require substantial preparation involving discovery, expert witnesses, motions, exhibits, witnesses, and court scheduling.

ADR can provide an alternative method for resolving some or all of the disputed issues.

The federal judiciary reports that ADR programs remain widely used. In 2025, 63 federal districts operated ADR programs, and those programs affected more than 40,600 civil cases.

ADR can also allow parties to negotiate solutions that a court might not be able to order through a conventional judgment.

For example, a settlement might include:

- Monetary compensation;
- A payment schedule;
- Contract modifications;
- Confidentiality provisions where legally permissible;
- Business arrangements;
- Corrective actions; or
- Resolution of only part of the dispute.

## Mediation Does Not Usually Eliminate the Right to Trial

One important characteristic of mediation is that it generally does not give the mediator authority to decide the dispute.

The U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California explains that its mediators cannot impose a settlement and that parties retain their litigation rights if mediation does not resolve the case.

This means that an unsuccessful mediation does not ordinarily mean the case is over.

If the parties cannot agree, the lawsuit can continue through discovery, motion practice, pretrial proceedings, and potentially trial.

## When ADR Occurs After a Lawsuit Is Filed

ADR is not limited to the period before litigation.

A civil lawsuit may already be underway when the court or parties initiate mediation.

Federal courts can have local rules requiring or encouraging ADR at particular stages. Some courts refer cases to mediation early, while others may schedule ADR after the parties have exchanged sufficient information to evaluate their positions.

For example, some federal districts require counsel to discuss ADR during case management, while particular courts may establish deadlines for mediation or settlement conferences.

The timing can therefore differ from one federal district to another.

## The Transition From Negotiation to Litigation

Many disputes begin before a formal complaint is filed.

The parties may exchange:

- Demand letters;
- Responses;
- Contracts;
- Insurance correspondence;
- Settlement proposals;
- Supporting documents; and
- Other information concerning the dispute.

If negotiations fail, the claimant may decide to file a lawsuit.

In federal court, a civil action generally begins when the plaintiff files a complaint. The defendant is then served with the complaint and must respond according to the applicable rules.

Filing a lawsuit changes the procedural environment, but it does not necessarily end settlement discussions.

## Pleadings Establish the Formal Dispute

Once litigation begins, the pleadings identify the legal and factual issues the parties are asking the court to resolve.

A complaint generally identifies:

- The parties;
- The factual allegations;
- The legal claims;
- The basis for the court's jurisdiction; and
- The relief requested.

The defendant may respond through an answer or, where appropriate, a motion challenging some or all of the complaint.

The pleadings help define the issues that later discovery and trial preparation will address.

## Discovery Changes the Settlement Landscape

Discovery is one of the major differences between informal negotiation and formal litigation.

During discovery, parties may be required to exchange information relevant to the claims and defenses.

Depending on the case, discovery can include:

- Interrogatories;
- Requests for production;
- Requests for admission;
- Depositions;
- Expert disclosures;
- Document exchanges; and
- Inspection of relevant property or information.

The U.S. Courts describe discovery as a process through which litigants obtain information about witnesses and documents and prepare their evidence for trial.

As discovery progresses, the parties may obtain information that changes their understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the case.

That can lead to renewed settlement negotiations.

## Motions Before Trial

Not every dispute reaches a full trial.

Parties can file motions seeking rulings on legal or procedural issues.

A particularly important motion in civil litigation is a **motion for summary judgment**, where a party argues that there is no genuine dispute over material facts and that the law permits judgment without a trial on the relevant issue.

Other motions can concern:

- Discovery;
- Expert testimony;
- Evidence;
- Procedural deadlines;
- Jurisdiction;
- Pleadings; or
- Other legal questions.

Federal courts conduct many proceedings besides trials, including motion hearings and evidentiary hearings.

A ruling on a motion can substantially change the issues that remain for trial.

## Settlement Conferences

A settlement conference is another mechanism that can occur during litigation.

A judge or magistrate judge may facilitate discussions between the parties in an effort to resolve some or all of the dispute.

The procedure differs from adjudication. The settlement judge generally does not decide the merits simply because the parties participate in a settlement conference.

Federal district courts use different models. Some provide judicial settlement conferences, while others rely primarily on private or court-appointed mediators.

## When Mediation Fails

An unsuccessful mediation does not necessarily indicate that the case is destined for trial.

The parties can continue negotiating afterward.

For example, mediation may narrow the dispute from several contested issues to one major disagreement. Later discovery or a court ruling may provide additional information that allows the parties to settle.

Settlement can therefore remain possible until the court enters a final judgment, subject to the circumstances and applicable rules.

## Preparing for Trial

When settlement does not resolve the dispute, the case continues toward trial.

Trial preparation can involve:

- Identifying witnesses;
- Preparing exhibits;
- Completing expert work;
- Filing motions in limine;
- Preparing jury instructions;
- Developing opening statements;
- Preparing direct and cross-examination;
- Organizing documentary evidence; and
- Addressing remaining legal issues.

The court establishes deadlines and procedures governing the case.

The parties must comply with those requirements even if settlement negotiations continue.

## Jury Trial or Bench Trial

A civil case can sometimes be tried before a jury or before a judge alone.

The U.S. Courts explain that in cases where a jury trial is available and requested, a jury may determine factual questions. If the right to a jury is waived or unavailable, a judge conducts a bench trial.

The distinction affects how factual disputes are decided.

In a jury trial, the judge determines the applicable law and instructs the jury. The jury then evaluates the evidence and reaches the factual determinations assigned to it.

In a bench trial, the judge performs the fact-finding function as well as applying the law.

## Rules of Evidence Become Central at Trial

Trial differs from mediation because the court must determine what evidence can be presented.

The Federal Rules of Evidence govern many federal trials, subject to applicable statutes and other rules.

Potential evidentiary questions include:

- Relevance;
- Authentication;
- Hearsay;
- Expert testimony;
- Privilege;
- Character evidence; and
- Unfair prejudice.

The U.S. Courts explain that judges apply rules of evidence to determine what information can be presented in the courtroom, while opposing attorneys can object to questions or evidence.

Evidence discussed during settlement negotiations therefore does not automatically become trial evidence.

## The Role of the Mediator Changes Completely

A mediator and trial judge perform fundamentally different functions.

A mediator generally:

- Facilitates communication;
- Helps identify disputed interests;
- Explores possible settlements;
- Does not decide the dispute; and
- Does not impose a judgment.

A trial judge, by contrast, controls courtroom proceedings, applies procedural and evidentiary rules, and decides legal issues. In a bench trial, the judge also determines the factual issues.

The distinction is particularly important because mediation is designed to preserve party autonomy, whereas trial results in an adjudicative decision.

## Confidentiality and Settlement Communications

ADR proceedings can have confidentiality protections, but the exact rules vary according to the applicable statute, rule, agreement, and court program.

For example, the Northern District of Ohio states that communications disclosed during its ADR proceedings generally cannot be provided to the court or others without appropriate written consent, subject to applicable rules.

Federal law also contains protections concerning certain dispute-resolution communications in specified proceedings.

Parties should therefore determine what confidentiality rules apply rather than assuming that every settlement-related communication receives identical protection.

## The Cost of Moving Toward Trial

As litigation progresses, the financial and administrative demands can increase.

Costs may include:

- Attorney time;
- Expert witnesses;
- Depositions;
- Court reporters;
- Document review;
- Investigations;
- Exhibits;
- Travel; and
- Other litigation expenses.

This does not mean that every case should settle. Trial may be necessary when the parties cannot reach an acceptable agreement, when important legal principles require judicial resolution, or when a party needs a formal judgment.

The U.S. Courts recognize that litigation can be time-consuming and expensive while also emphasizing the role of ADR in reducing unnecessary burdens in appropriate cases.

## ADR Can Continue Even Near Trial

The possibility of settlement does not necessarily disappear when trial approaches.

Parties may continue negotiating during:

- Discovery;
- Motion practice;
- Pretrial conferences;
- Settlement conferences;
- Trial preparation; and
- In some circumstances, the trial itself.

A settlement reached before a verdict can eliminate the need for the court to decide the remaining disputed issues.

The precise procedures for documenting and presenting a settlement depend on the court and circumstances.

## What Happens When the Case Actually Goes to Trial?

A civil trial generally involves several stages.

### 1. Jury selection, if applicable

Potential jurors are questioned and selected according to the applicable procedures.

### 2. Opening statements

The parties explain the evidence they expect to present.

### 3. Plaintiff's evidence

The plaintiff generally presents evidence supporting the claims.

### 4. Defendant's evidence

The defendant may present evidence supporting its defenses.

### 5. Rebuttal

Additional evidence may be presented according to the applicable rules.

### 6. Closing arguments

The parties explain how the evidence supports their respective positions.

### 7. Instructions and deliberation

In a jury trial, the judge explains the relevant law and the jury deliberates.

### 8. Verdict or judgment

The jury returns a verdict, or the judge issues findings and judgment in a bench trial.

The U.S. Courts describe these general stages as part of the federal civil trial process.

## The Standard of Proof in Civil Trials

In an ordinary federal civil case, the plaintiff generally bears the burden of proving the claim by a **preponderance of the evidence**.

The U.S. Courts describe this standard as requiring the plaintiff to establish that it is more likely than not that the defendant is responsible for the claimed harm.

Some claims or particular issues can involve different burdens or standards, so the general civil standard should not be treated as universal for every proceeding.

## After Trial

A trial does not necessarily end the litigation.

Depending on the circumstances, a party may seek:

- Post-trial relief;
- A new trial;
- Judgment as a matter of law;
- Modification of the judgment; or
- Appellate review.

Settlement can also sometimes occur after a verdict while post-trial or appellate proceedings remain possible.

The procedural options depend on the applicable rules and the nature of the judgment.

## Why the Transition Matters

The transition from ADR to trial changes the nature of the dispute-resolution process.

**ADR generally emphasizes:**

- Negotiation;
- Flexibility;
- Party participation;
- Confidentiality where applicable; and
- Voluntary resolution in mediation.

**Trial generally emphasizes:**

- Formal pleadings;
- Discovery;
- Rules of evidence;
- Judicial rulings;
- Witness testimony;
- Formal fact-finding; and
- A binding judgment subject to applicable review procedures.

Neither process necessarily excludes the other.

A case can move through several of these stages before reaching a final resolution.

## Final Takeaway

The path from alternative dispute resolution to a courtroom trial is usually a gradual progression rather than a single procedural switch. Negotiation may occur before litigation, mediation may take place after a lawsuit is filed, discovery can lead to renewed settlement discussions, and settlement conferences may occur shortly before trial. Federal courts actively incorporate ADR into civil case management; in 2025, ADR programs operated in 63 federal districts and affected more than 40,600 civil cases.

When ADR does not produce an agreement, the case can continue through discovery, motions, pretrial preparation, and eventually trial. At trial, formal rules of evidence and procedure govern the presentation of evidence, and a judge or jury determines the issues assigned to it.

Understanding this progression helps explain why a civil lawsuit can involve repeated settlement efforts even after formal litigation has begun. ADR and trial are not necessarily competing systems; they are different mechanisms that can operate at different stages of the same civil dispute.
