Civil justice systems provide mechanisms for resolving disputes between individuals, businesses, organizations, and, in some circumstances, public entities. One important area of civil law is **tort law**, which generally addresses civil wrongs that cause legally recognized harm and can result in remedies such as monetary compensation or court orders.

Although the underlying idea of compensating people for wrongful harm appears in many legal traditions, there is no single global tort system. Countries use different legal frameworks, terminology, courts, procedures, standards of proof, and rules governing compensation. The United States, for example, operates primarily within a common-law tradition, while many countries in Europe, Latin America, Africa, and Asia use civil-law systems based substantially on codified statutes. Some jurisdictions combine elements of several traditions.

Understanding these differences is important when comparing personal-injury claims or other civil disputes across countries.

## What Is Tort Law?

A tort is generally an act or omission that causes legally recognized harm and gives rise to civil liability. Tort law can address conduct such as negligence, intentional injury, defamation, trespass, nuisance, and certain forms of product-related harm.

The objectives of tort law can include:

- Compensating injured parties
- Allocating losses
- Holding responsible parties accountable
- Deterring harmful conduct
- Providing legal remedies for violations of private rights

The exact balance between these objectives differs among legal systems.

A tort claim is also different from a criminal prosecution. A criminal case is ordinarily brought by the state to address an alleged offense, while a civil claim generally concerns the legal rights and obligations of private parties.

## Common Law and Civil Law Traditions

Two of the world's most influential legal traditions are **common law** and **civil law**.

### Common-Law Systems

Common-law systems developed from the English legal tradition and place substantial importance on judicial decisions and precedent.

The United States is a common-law jurisdiction, although statutes and regulations are also major sources of law. Courts interpret legislation and develop legal doctrines through decisions that can become precedent.

In a common-law tort case, previous judicial decisions can therefore be highly significant. A court may examine earlier cases involving similar facts to determine how a legal principle should apply.

The precise importance of precedent varies according to the court and jurisdiction.

### Civil-Law Systems

Civil-law systems generally place greater emphasis on written codes and statutes.

Modern civil-law systems trace important parts of their development to Roman law and later codification, including the Napoleonic Code and other national civil codes. Today, civil-law systems are widely used throughout Europe and in many countries in Latin America, Africa, and Asia.

Judges generally apply and interpret codified provisions to the facts of individual disputes. Judicial decisions can still be influential, but the structure and formal authority of precedent differ from common-law systems.

## Hybrid Legal Systems

The distinction between common law and civil law is useful, but it should not be treated as an absolute division.

Some jurisdictions incorporate elements of multiple legal traditions.

Cornell's Legal Information Institute notes examples such as South Africa and Sri Lanka, where historical influences from different legal traditions have shaped the modern legal system. Louisiana and Quebec also demonstrate how civil-law principles can operate within broader legal systems influenced by common-law traditions.

Consequently, determining how a tort claim works in a particular country requires examination of that country's actual legislation and court procedures rather than relying solely on a broad classification.

## The Basic Structure of a Tort Claim

Although terminology differs internationally, many tort disputes involve several recurring questions.

### Was There a Legally Recognized Duty or Obligation?

The claimant generally must establish that the defendant had a legal obligation relevant to the circumstances.

For example, a driver may have duties concerning safe operation of a vehicle. A property operator may have responsibilities concerning dangerous conditions. A manufacturer may have obligations concerning product safety.

The source of the obligation differs among legal systems.

### Was the Obligation Breached?

The next question may involve whether the defendant's conduct fell below the legally required standard.

In a negligence case, this could involve unreasonable conduct, failure to take a required precaution, or failure to comply with an applicable legal obligation.

### Did the Conduct Cause the Harm?

Causation is another central issue.

A claimant generally needs to connect the defendant's conduct to the injury or loss for which compensation is sought.

A person can violate a legal rule without necessarily causing the particular injury involved in a lawsuit.

### Was There Legally Recognized Damage?

Civil systems differ in the types of harm they recognize and the remedies available.

Potential losses can include:

- Medical expenses
- Lost income
- Property damage
- Physical injury
- Certain forms of emotional harm
- Loss of services
- Other legally recognized losses

The availability and calculation of these damages depend heavily on the jurisdiction.

## Negligence as a Major Category

Negligence is one of the most widely recognized forms of tort liability.

In broad terms, negligence concerns harm resulting from a failure to exercise the level of care required under the applicable legal standard.

A typical negligence dispute can involve:

1. The applicable duty or obligation
2. The required standard of conduct
3. The defendant's conduct
4. Causation
5. The claimant's resulting damages

However, countries can structure these concepts differently.

Some civil-law jurisdictions may formulate liability through provisions in a civil code rather than using the same terminology found in U.S. negligence law.

The underlying factual inquiry can nevertheless be similar: what obligation existed, what happened, and what consequences legally resulted?

## Strict Liability

Not every tort claim requires proof that a defendant acted negligently.

Some legal systems recognize forms of **strict liability**, where responsibility can arise based on particular activities, products, relationships, or statutory provisions without requiring the same showing of fault required in an ordinary negligence claim.

Product liability provides an important example.

Depending on the jurisdiction, a manufacturer or supplier may face liability for a defective product under statutory, contractual, or tort principles.

The scope of strict liability varies considerably across countries.

## Intentional Civil Wrongs

Tort systems can also address intentional conduct.

Examples can include:

- Assault
- Battery
- Trespass
- False imprisonment
- Intentional interference
- Defamation

The terminology and legal elements differ among jurisdictions.

Some countries organize these claims under civil-code provisions rather than using the same categories found in American tort law.

## Who Must Prove the Case?

The allocation of the **burden of proof** is a major component of civil litigation.

In many common-law systems, ordinary civil claims are generally decided using a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard. This means the party with the burden must establish the relevant facts as more likely than not.

Civil-law jurisdictions can use different formulations.

Comparative legal scholarship has identified differences between common-law and civil-law approaches to proof, including differences in how judges evaluate whether the evidence establishes the relevant facts.

These distinctions can affect litigation strategy and the presentation of evidence.

## The Role of the Judge

Judicial roles also differ across legal traditions.

In many common-law proceedings, parties and their lawyers play a substantial role in presenting evidence and arguments, while the judge acts as a neutral decision-maker within the adversarial process.

Civil-law proceedings can give judges a more active role in establishing the facts and evaluating evidence. Cornell's overview of legal systems identifies the more investigative role traditionally associated with civil-law judges.

Actual procedures vary considerably from country to country, however.

A modern civil-law court may not resemble a traditional description of an investigative court, just as common-law courts differ substantially among jurisdictions.

## Juries and Professional Judges

Another major difference concerns who decides the facts.

In the United States, some civil cases can be decided by juries when the applicable constitutional and procedural requirements are satisfied.

Many other countries rely primarily on professional judges for civil disputes.

Some jurisdictions use mixed panels or specialized judicial bodies.

The identity of the decision-maker can affect how evidence is presented, how expert testimony is evaluated, and how proceedings are structured.

## Evidence in Civil Litigation

Evidence is essential to almost every tort dispute.

Potential evidence can include:

- Documents
- Photographs
- Video
- Witness testimony
- Expert opinions
- Medical records
- Financial records
- Physical evidence
- Electronic communications
- Government records
- Business records

The rules governing admissibility and evidentiary weight differ between legal systems.

Common-law jurisdictions may have detailed rules governing admissibility and discovery, while civil-law systems may use different mechanisms for gathering and presenting evidence.

## Discovery and Pretrial Information

One of the most significant procedural differences can involve **discovery**.

In U.S. civil litigation, parties can often obtain substantial information from opposing parties before trial through mechanisms such as:

- Interrogatories
- Requests for production
- Requests for admission
- Depositions
- Subpoenas

The scope and availability of these procedures depend on the court and type of case.

Many civil-law systems generally use more judge-directed evidence gathering and do not employ discovery in exactly the same way as U.S. litigation.

This difference can substantially affect litigation costs, timelines, and how parties prepare their cases.

## Expert Evidence

Expert evidence is particularly important in technical disputes.

Medical malpractice, engineering failures, construction defects, product safety, accounting disputes, and other specialized cases may require expert analysis.

Experts can address questions that ordinary fact witnesses cannot reliably answer.

The procedures for appointing and examining experts differ internationally. In some systems, courts may rely heavily on court-appointed experts, while other systems place greater emphasis on experts retained by the parties.

## Compensation and Remedies

Monetary compensation is one of the principal remedies in tort cases.

Cornell's overview of U.S. tort law identifies compensatory damages as a common remedy and notes that damages can include categories such as medical expenses, lost earnings, future earning capacity, and pain and suffering.

Other remedies can include:

- Injunctions
- Restitution
- Declaratory relief
- Repair or replacement
- Specific statutory remedies

The availability of punitive or exemplary damages varies substantially.

The United States permits punitive damages in certain circumstances, but many other legal systems approach punishment through separate regulatory or criminal mechanisms rather than routinely using punitive damages within private civil litigation.

## Settlement and Alternative Dispute Resolution

Not every civil dispute reaches trial.

Parties can resolve disputes through:

- Negotiation
- Mediation
- Arbitration
- Conciliation
- Court-sponsored settlement procedures

The availability and legal status of these mechanisms differ across jurisdictions.

Mediation, for example, allows parties to attempt to reach a negotiated resolution with assistance from a neutral third party.

Arbitration generally involves submitting the dispute to an arbitrator or panel rather than a court, subject to the applicable arbitration agreement and law.

## Appeals

Most legal systems provide some mechanism for challenging a lower court's decision.

An appeal can involve review of:

- Legal interpretation
- Procedural errors
- Evidentiary decisions
- Findings of fact, depending on the jurisdiction
- The calculation or availability of remedies

The scope of appellate review varies considerably.

Some appellate courts primarily examine legal questions, while others have authority to review factual findings under specified standards.

## Limitation Periods

Tort claims are generally subject to time limits.

These may be called:

- Statutes of limitations
- Limitation periods
- Prescription periods
- Filing deadlines

The period can depend on the type of claim, when the harm occurred, when it was discovered, the identity of the defendant, and other circumstances.

There is no universal international limitation period.

A claimant involved in a cross-border dispute therefore needs to determine which country's law applies and whether special rules affect the deadline.

## Cross-Border Tort Claims

Modern commerce and travel can create disputes involving multiple countries.

A collision might occur in one country while the injured person lives in another. A product may be manufactured in one jurisdiction, sold in another, and cause injury in a third.

These cases can raise questions about:

- Jurisdiction
- Applicable law
- Recognition of judgments
- Service of legal documents
- Evidence located abroad
- International arbitration
- Enforcement of judgments

International treaties and national procedural laws can affect these issues.

The **1980 Hague Convention on International Access to Justice**, for example, addresses certain issues involving access to justice in international civil and commercial matters.

## Access to Civil Justice

Having a legal right does not necessarily mean that a person can practically enforce it.

The United Nations identifies access to justice as an important component of the rule of law and emphasizes fair, transparent, effective, and accountable justice services.

Barriers can include:

- Legal fees
- Court costs
- Lack of legal representation
- Geographic distance
- Language barriers
- Procedural complexity
- Lack of information
- Lengthy proceedings

Legal-aid programs, simplified procedures, mediation, community legal services, and other mechanisms can help address some of these barriers.

## Informal Dispute Resolution

Formal courts are not the only way disputes are resolved around the world.

The United Nations recognizes that informal, traditional, customary, and other non-state justice mechanisms play important roles in many communities. These mechanisms can address property disputes, family matters, community conflicts, and other civil issues.

Their relationship with formal courts varies by country.

Some systems allow informal settlements to operate alongside formal courts, while others provide specific procedures for recognizing or enforcing agreements reached outside the traditional court process.

## How Tort Law Reflects Local Policy

Although many countries seek to compensate people harmed by wrongful conduct, the precise boundaries of liability reflect local legal and policy choices.

A jurisdiction may decide:

- How broadly negligence is defined
- Whether strict liability applies
- Which injuries are compensable
- Whether damages are capped
- Whether punitive damages are available
- How fault is allocated
- Who may bring a wrongful-death claim
- How long a claimant has to sue
- Whether certain defendants receive immunity

These choices explain why similar accidents can produce different legal outcomes in different countries.

## A Comparative Example

Consider a hypothetical pedestrian struck by a vehicle.

The factual investigation might be similar in several countries:

- Where was the pedestrian?
- What speed was the vehicle traveling?
- Was there a traffic signal?
- Who had the right-of-way?
- Were there witnesses?
- Was the driver distracted?
- What injuries occurred?

But the legal analysis could differ.

One country might rely heavily on judicial precedent concerning driver negligence. Another might apply provisions of a civil code concerning unlawful harm. A third might use a statutory compensation scheme for certain traffic injuries.

Even if the underlying facts are almost identical, the available remedies and procedures can differ.

## Why Legal-System Classification Is Only a Starting Point

Labels such as "common law" and "civil law" are useful for understanding broad legal traditions, but they do not replace jurisdiction-specific research.

Cornell's overview emphasizes that legal systems vary within the broad categories and that hybrid systems are common.

For a real dispute, the relevant questions are more specific:

1. Which country's law applies?
2. Which court has jurisdiction?
3. What cause of action is available?
4. What elements must be proved?
5. Who bears the burden of proof?
6. What evidence can be used?
7. What remedies are available?
8. What limitation period applies?
9. Can the judgment be appealed?
10. How will a judgment be enforced?

These questions matter more than the broad label attached to the legal system.

## Final Takeaway

Tort law is part of a much larger civil-justice structure that determines how private disputes are presented, investigated, decided, and resolved.

Across the world, legal systems share certain broad concepts—such as compensation for legally recognized harm, duties of care, causation, evidence, and judicial remedies—but they organize those concepts differently.

Common-law systems generally place greater emphasis on judicial precedent, while civil-law systems rely more heavily on comprehensive statutes and codes. Hybrid systems combine elements of multiple traditions.

The differences extend beyond substantive tort rules. Court structure, discovery, judicial involvement, expert evidence, standards of proof, jury participation, appeals, limitation periods, and available remedies can all vary.

For international or cross-border disputes, identifying the applicable legal system is therefore only the first step. The parties must also determine which jurisdiction's law governs the dispute, where proceedings can be brought, what evidence is required, and how any resulting judgment can be enforced.

Because civil and tort law is highly jurisdiction-specific, anyone dealing with a cross-border injury or civil claim should consider advice from an attorney qualified to practice in the relevant jurisdiction or jurisdictions.
