A motor vehicle collision can create several emergencies at once: injuries, damaged vehicles, blocked lanes, leaking fuel or other fluids, fire risks, and the possibility of another crash involving approaching traffic. For that reason, emergency response after a collision is not limited to calling 911. It involves protecting people, communicating accurate information, controlling traffic, treating injuries, documenting the scene, and eventually clearing the roadway.

The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) describes traffic incident management as a coordinated, multidisciplinary process designed to detect, respond to, and safely clear roadway incidents. Law enforcement, fire and rescue, emergency medical services, transportation agencies, towing companies, and other organizations may all have different responsibilities at a crash scene.

## 1. Protect People From Immediate Hazards

The first priority following a collision is safety.

Drivers and passengers should assess whether they are in immediate danger from:

- Oncoming traffic
- Fire or smoke
- Fuel or chemical leaks
- Downed electrical wires
- Unstable vehicles
- Broken glass or debris
- A vehicle stopped in an active traffic lane
- Other hazards created by the collision

If the vehicle can be moved safely and doing so is permitted under local rules, moving it out of an active travel lane can reduce the risk of a secondary collision. However, an injured person should generally not be moved unnecessarily unless there is an immediate danger that requires evacuation.

The appropriate response can depend on the location and severity of the collision. A crash on a busy highway, for example, may require substantially more traffic-control measures than a minor collision in a parking lot.

## 2. Contact Emergency Services When Necessary

A collision involving injuries, possible serious hazards, or significant vehicle damage may require emergency assistance.

When contacting 911 or the applicable emergency number, provide information such as:

- Exact location or roadway
- Direction of travel
- Number of vehicles involved
- Whether anyone appears injured
- Whether someone is trapped inside a vehicle
- Whether there is fire, smoke, fuel, or another hazardous condition
- Whether traffic lanes are blocked
- Any other condition that could affect the response

Accurate location information is particularly important because dispatchers use incident details to determine what personnel and equipment should be sent to the scene. FHWA guidance identifies accurate information about location, injuries, vehicles, traffic impacts, and special hazards as important components of effective incident response.

## 3. Follow Emergency Dispatch Instructions

After calling for assistance, remain available to answer questions and follow instructions from emergency dispatchers when possible.

Depending on the circumstances, dispatchers may need additional information about:

- The number of injured people
- Whether anyone is unconscious
- Whether a vehicle is on fire
- Whether someone is trapped
- Whether traffic is still moving through the crash area
- Whether hazardous materials may be involved

If conditions change—for example, a fire starts or an injured person becomes unresponsive—update emergency services.

## 4. Make the Crash Scene Visible

A crash scene can become dangerous when approaching drivers do not realize that vehicles or people are in the roadway.

Emergency responders use warning equipment, vehicle positioning, lighting, cones, signs, and other traffic-control measures to establish a safer work area. FHWA identifies scene management and traffic control as major components of traffic incident management because responders and crash victims remain exposed to moving traffic.

Drivers involved in a collision should avoid standing unnecessarily in active traffic lanes. If they are able to move to a safer location without creating another hazard, they should do so while following instructions from responding authorities.

## 5. Understand the Roles of Emergency Responders

Different responders generally perform different functions.

### Law Enforcement

Police or highway patrol personnel may:

- Secure the scene
- Control traffic
- Investigate the collision
- Speak with drivers and witnesses
- Document relevant circumstances
- Coordinate with other responding agencies

Their role can vary by jurisdiction and by the seriousness of the collision.

### Fire and Rescue

Fire and rescue personnel may:

- Respond to vehicle fires
- Stabilize damaged vehicles
- Extricate trapped occupants
- Address hazardous materials
- Provide emergency medical assistance
- Help protect the crash scene

FHWA identifies fire and rescue agencies as important participants in traffic incident management, particularly when rescue, fire suppression, or hazardous-material response is required.

### Emergency Medical Services

EMS personnel focus on evaluating, treating, and transporting injured people.

Their responsibilities can include:

- Triage
- Emergency treatment
- Determining transportation needs
- Coordinating evacuation
- Transporting patients to appropriate medical facilities

EMS priorities generally center on patient care and the safety of both patients and responders.

### Transportation and Highway Agencies

Transportation agencies may help with:

- Traffic control
- Lane closures
- Variable message signs
- Roadway debris
- Detours
- Incident clearance
- Communication with motorists

The objective is to protect people at the scene while restoring normal roadway operations as safely as possible.

## 6. Prevent Secondary Collisions

One of the most important parts of emergency response is preventing another collision.

FHWA notes that motorists approaching an incident can be vulnerable to secondary crashes because of sudden traffic slowdowns, lane changes, stopped vehicles, and emergency vehicles. Effective traffic-control procedures can reduce those risks.

Drivers approaching a crash should:

- Slow down
- Pay attention to emergency personnel
- Follow lane-control instructions
- Avoid distracted driving
- Avoid stopping unnecessarily to observe the crash
- Give emergency vehicles adequate space
- Follow applicable move-over requirements

A collision that has already occurred can therefore create a continuing safety problem until the roadway is secured and cleared.

## 7. Preserve Important Information Without Interfering With Emergency Operations

Once immediate safety concerns are addressed, drivers may need to document the incident.

Potentially useful information can include:

- Photographs of vehicle damage
- Photographs of the surrounding roadway
- Vehicle positions, when safe to document
- Traffic signs and signals
- Weather or roadway conditions
- Names and contact information of witnesses
- Insurance information exchanged by the drivers
- Police report information
- Medical documentation

Documentation should never interfere with emergency treatment, rescue operations, or instructions from police or other responders.

Photographs can be particularly useful because vehicle positions and roadway conditions may change once vehicles are moved and traffic resumes.

## 8. Cooperate With the Investigation

Drivers may be asked to provide identification, insurance information, or an account of what happened.

It is generally useful to provide accurate factual information and avoid guessing about matters you do not know.

For example, a driver who does not know how fast another vehicle was traveling should not invent an estimate. Similarly, a driver may describe what they observed without attempting to determine the final legal question of fault.

The responding officer's report can become an important record of the incident, but it is not necessarily the only evidence considered in an insurance claim or civil case.

Other evidence may include photographs, vehicle damage, physical evidence, witness statements, surveillance footage, electronic data, medical records, and other documentation.

## 9. Arrange Vehicle Recovery and Roadway Clearance

After injured people have received necessary care and the scene has been investigated, damaged vehicles may need to be removed.

Depending on the circumstances, towing and recovery personnel may:

- Remove disabled vehicles
- Recover vehicles from unusual positions
- Assist with debris removal
- Help restore blocked lanes
- Coordinate with law enforcement or transportation agencies

FHWA identifies quick clearance and recovery as an important component of traffic incident management because leaving vehicles or debris in travel lanes can prolong traffic disruption and increase exposure to secondary crashes.

However, vehicles involved in serious collisions may need to remain in place until investigators have completed necessary scene documentation.

## 10. Seek Medical Evaluation When Appropriate

Not every injury is immediately obvious after a collision. Some symptoms may become noticeable later.

Anyone experiencing pain, dizziness, confusion, weakness, breathing difficulties, loss of consciousness, or other concerning symptoms should seek appropriate medical attention.

Medical records can also provide documentation of the evaluation and treatment received after a collision. The appropriate level of care depends on the person's symptoms and circumstances.

## 11. Notify the Insurance Company

Insurance policies may contain requirements concerning prompt notification of an accident.

After emergency issues have been addressed, drivers should review their policies and contact the appropriate insurer. Information commonly requested can include:

- Date and location of the collision
- Vehicles involved
- Driver information
- Police report information
- Description of the damage
- Information about injuries
- Photographs or other documentation

An insurer's investigation is separate from the emergency response itself. Insurance adjusters may later review photographs, statements, repair estimates, police records, medical documentation, and other available evidence.

## 12. Emergency Response Does Not Automatically Determine Legal Liability

Emergency responders are primarily concerned with protecting people, providing medical assistance, investigating the incident, and restoring roadway safety.

Those functions are different from determining civil liability.

For example, a police citation may become evidence in an insurance or civil dispute, but a citation does not automatically establish every element of a personal injury claim. Likewise, the absence of a citation does not necessarily mean that no civil claim exists.

Questions about negligence, comparative fault, damages, insurance coverage, and available remedies can depend on state law and the specific facts of the collision.

## Final Takeaway

Emergency response following a motor vehicle collision is a coordinated process focused first on protecting people and controlling immediate hazards. Drivers should obtain emergency assistance when appropriate, provide accurate information, follow responder instructions, avoid unnecessary exposure to traffic, and document the incident once it is safe to do so.

Police, fire and rescue, EMS, transportation agencies, and towing providers may each have different responsibilities. FHWA's traffic incident management framework emphasizes coordination among these participants to protect crash victims and responders, reduce secondary collisions, and restore roadway operations safely.

For questions involving insurance claims, liability, or legal deadlines after a collision, the applicable state law and the specific facts of the incident should be reviewed. Consider advice from an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction when the circumstances are complex or significant injuries or losses are involved.
