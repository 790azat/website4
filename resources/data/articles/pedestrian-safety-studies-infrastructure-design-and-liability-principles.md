Pedestrian safety depends on more than driver behavior and pedestrian awareness. The design, operation, and maintenance of roads and pedestrian facilities can also influence the risk of crashes. Sidewalks, crosswalks, curb ramps, lighting, traffic signals, roadway geometry, and crossing distances are among the features transportation agencies consider when planning for people who walk.

In the United States, pedestrian safety studies examine these features to identify crash risks and evaluate potential improvements. At the same time, when a pedestrian is injured, questions may arise about whether a driver, property owner, government entity, or another party had a legal responsibility related to the conditions that contributed to the incident.

The relationship between **infrastructure design and legal liability**, however, is highly dependent on the circumstances and applicable state and local law. A roadway feature that presents a safety concern does not automatically establish that a particular party is legally responsible for an injury.

## Why Pedestrian Infrastructure Matters

Federal Highway Administration guidance treats pedestrians as legitimate users of the transportation system and encourages planners and engineers to account for pedestrians when streets and facilities are designed. Pedestrian facilities can include sidewalks, crossings, curb ramps, pedestrian signals, lighting, medians, and other features.

The importance of infrastructure is also reflected in national crash data. NHTSA reports that **7,080 pedestrians were killed in traffic crashes in the United States in 2024**, with more than 71,000 pedestrians injured.

Researchers therefore examine roadway characteristics alongside human behavior. Factors such as vehicle speed, traffic volume, roadway width, visibility, crossing distance, lighting, and intersection design can affect the environment in which pedestrians and drivers interact.

## What Pedestrian Safety Studies Examine

Pedestrian safety research can involve several categories of infrastructure.

### Sidewalks and Pedestrian Paths

Sidewalk continuity, width, surface condition, drainage, slopes, obstacles, and lighting can affect pedestrian accessibility and safety.

FHWA guidance addresses sidewalk installation, widths, buffers, grades, surface treatments, lighting, obstacles, and connections to other pedestrian facilities.

Maintenance is also important. FHWA has published guidance addressing inspection, accessibility, surface repairs, structural problems, and maintenance policies for sidewalks, curb ramps, crosswalks, pedestrian signals, and paths.

A damaged or obstructed sidewalk may therefore be relevant in an injury investigation, particularly when the condition existed for an extended period or had previously been reported.

### Crosswalks

Crosswalks are another major area of pedestrian safety research.

A crosswalk may be marked with pavement markings, but at intersections a crosswalk can also exist by operation of applicable law even when markings are absent. The precise legal definition varies by jurisdiction. FHWA research notes that crosswalk definitions and pedestrian right-of-way rules must be considered when evaluating crossings.

Research also demonstrates why simply asking whether a crosswalk is painted is not enough. FHWA's research found that pedestrian crash rates are influenced by factors including pedestrian volume, traffic volume, and the number of roadway lanes.

The study concluded that marked crosswalks alone should not necessarily be treated as a complete safety treatment at uncontrolled locations. Depending on the roadway, additional measures may include raised medians, pedestrian islands, traffic signals, curb extensions, improved lighting, or traffic-calming measures.

### Intersections and Crossing Geometry

Intersection design can affect how far pedestrians must travel across a roadway and how visible they are to approaching drivers.

FHWA materials identify design considerations such as curb extensions, crossing islands, medians, curb radii, turning movements, crosswalk placement, and curb ramps.

The goal is generally to create a crossing that is understandable, accessible, and appropriate for the roadway environment.

### Lighting and Visibility

Lighting can be particularly relevant to nighttime pedestrian safety.

NHTSA's pedestrian safety research notes that a substantial portion of fatal pedestrian crashes occur in dark conditions.

Visibility can depend on more than streetlights. Parking, vegetation, signs, utility structures, roadway curvature, vehicle position, and intersection geometry can also affect whether a driver can see a pedestrian.

## How Infrastructure Studies Can Be Used After a Crash

When infrastructure is alleged to have contributed to a pedestrian injury, investigators may examine the physical environment surrounding the incident.

Depending on the circumstances, relevant evidence may include:

- Photographs and video of the roadway
- Sidewalk and curb conditions
- Crosswalk markings
- Traffic signals and pedestrian signals
- Street lighting
- Posted speed limits
- Roadway width and number of lanes
- Sight distance
- Traffic volume
- Vehicle speeds
- Construction or work-zone conditions
- Prior crash data
- Maintenance and inspection records
- Complaints or reports concerning the location
- Engineering studies
- Roadway plans and design documents
- Applicable traffic-control standards

Crash reconstruction specialists or transportation engineers may also evaluate whether roadway characteristics could have affected the sequence of events.

Importantly, a safety study is not necessarily a determination of legal fault. Technical evidence may help establish what physical conditions existed, but a court must still apply the relevant legal standards.

## When Infrastructure Conditions May Raise Liability Questions

Potential liability depends heavily on who controlled the property or roadway and what duties the law imposes.

### Government-Owned Roads

Public streets and sidewalks may be controlled by municipalities, counties, states, transportation departments, or other governmental entities.

Claims involving public entities can be subject to special rules. Governmental immunity, notice requirements, statutory claim procedures, discretionary-function protections, and other limitations may apply depending on the jurisdiction.

For example, an injured pedestrian may need to establish more than simply showing that a dangerous condition existed. State law may require proof concerning the government's notice of the condition, its opportunity to correct it, or whether a particular immunity applies.

### Private Property

Private property owners may have different duties concerning sidewalks, parking lots, driveways, entrances, and other areas accessible to pedestrians.

A property-related pedestrian injury could involve conditions such as an obstructed walkway, inadequate maintenance, poor lighting, or an unsafe surface. Whether the owner or another party is legally responsible depends on applicable premises-liability law and the facts of the incident.

### Contractors and Developers

Construction companies, engineering firms, contractors, or developers may become relevant when a pedestrian injury involves a construction project or improperly installed facility.

Potential issues can include whether the work complied with applicable plans, permits, contracts, regulations, or safety requirements.

Again, compliance with a particular standard does not automatically resolve a civil-liability question. The legal duties and facts must be examined together.

## The Role of Traffic-Control Standards

The federal **Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD)** establishes national standards for traffic-control devices used on public roads and other facilities covered by the manual. FHWA states that the current edition is the 11th Edition with Revision 1, dated December 2025, effective March 5, 2026.

The MUTCD addresses traffic signals, signs, pavement markings, pedestrian facilities, and other traffic-control devices.

For example, the MUTCD contains specifications concerning crosswalk markings. The 11th Edition provides minimum dimensions for marked crosswalks and distinguishes between different marking designs.

However, the existence of a federal or state engineering standard does not automatically determine civil liability. Courts generally consider the applicable jurisdiction's statutes, regulations, case law, governmental-immunity rules, evidence, and the specific facts of the incident.

## Does a Poorly Designed Road Automatically Mean Someone Is Liable?

No.

This distinction is important when discussing pedestrian safety studies.

A study may identify a roadway characteristic as associated with greater crash risk. An engineer may conclude that a crossing could be improved. A facility may even fail to satisfy a particular standard.

None of those facts necessarily establishes that a particular defendant is legally responsible for an individual injury.

A civil claim generally requires an analysis of the legal duty involved, the applicable standard of care, causation, damages, and any defenses or statutory limitations. The precise elements vary by claim and jurisdiction.

For governmental defendants, additional requirements may apply.

For drivers, liability may depend on issues such as right-of-way, speed, visibility, distraction, impairment, and other facts.

For property owners, premises-liability rules may determine whether a duty existed and whether the owner had actual or constructive notice of a dangerous condition.

## Why Maintenance Records Can Matter

Infrastructure safety is not limited to original design.

A properly designed pedestrian facility can become hazardous through deterioration, construction, vegetation, damaged signs, faded markings, drainage problems, or other changes.

FHWA specifically identifies maintenance as an important component of pedestrian facility safety and addresses inspection programs, repair practices, accessibility, and maintenance policies.

After an injury, records showing inspections, complaints, repairs, work orders, or previous incidents may therefore provide useful context.

They can help establish when a condition existed and whether the responsible entity may have been aware of it. The legal significance of those records depends on the jurisdiction and the particular claim.

## What Pedestrians and Drivers Can Do

Infrastructure design cannot eliminate every crash risk. NHTSA recommends that pedestrians follow traffic signs and signals, use sidewalks when available, and cross at designated crossings when available. Drivers should remain alert and yield as required by applicable traffic laws.

FHWA's research also emphasizes that pedestrian safety is generally best approached through a combination of engineering, planning, operations, maintenance, education, and enforcement rather than relying on one measure alone.

## Final Takeaway

Pedestrian safety studies provide valuable information about how street design, crossings, sidewalks, lighting, traffic controls, and maintenance can affect pedestrian risk. Federal research supports evaluating the entire roadway environment rather than assuming that one feature, such as a painted crosswalk, is sufficient.

When a pedestrian is injured, infrastructure conditions may become relevant evidence, particularly when the incident involves an allegedly defective sidewalk, inadequate crossing design, poor visibility, missing traffic controls, or a maintenance problem.

But identifying a safety deficiency is different from proving legal liability. Whether a driver, property owner, contractor, or government entity may be responsible depends on the applicable law, the evidence, and the specific circumstances of the incident. Anyone considering a legal claim involving a roadway or pedestrian facility should consider advice from an attorney licensed in the relevant jurisdiction.
